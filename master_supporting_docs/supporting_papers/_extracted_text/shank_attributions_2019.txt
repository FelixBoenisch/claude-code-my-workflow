Introduction

Humans working together can accomplish amazing things that would be impossible for an
individual, making human teams invaluable in modern society. Teams, however, possess just
as great of a potential for morally unacceptable behavior. A long history of social science
research shows how individuals are attributed responsibility, blame, praise, or wrongness
(Malle, Guglielmo, & Monroe, 2014; Shaver, 1985; Weiner, 1985) and this has also been
applied to humans in teams and organizations (Gailey, 2013; Haynes & Lawrence, 2012;
Shank & Robinson, forthcoming). An emerging question is whether similar attributions
can be made to non-humans such as intelligent technologies which now routinely collabor-
ate with humans in joint decision-making, thereby functioning as rudimentary teams.

   Assigning moral fault within a group of humans may involve more than a logical cal-
culus of determining responsibility, but may depend on relative power and status (Shank
& Robinson, forthcoming), group identification (Kaiser, Dyrenforth, & Hagiwara, 2006),
and motives for scapegoating (Rothschild, Landau, Sullivan, & Keefer, 2012). Because
artificial intelligence (AI) agents will often have little social power and may be thought
of as tools instead of team members, if people do blame AIs on human-AI teams there
may be increased scapegoating of moral responsibility. This could have a profound impli-
cation for our society as AIs continue to join us as teammates at work, while simul-
taneously being feared for taking our jobs (McClure, 2018).

   Specific situations where AIs are involved in morally significant outcomes that have
gained media attention include AIs producing lenient parole sentences towards whites,
selecting disproportionally few dark-skinned winners in a beauty contest, exposing chil-
dren to violent and obscene videos, and producing derogatory remarks about minority
groups on Twitter (Shank & DeSanti, 2018). More generally, there is widespread use in
society of black-box algorithms which routinely discriminate against minority groups
and the poor (Eubanks, 2018; Noble, 2018; Wachter-Boettcher, 2017) and are used to jus-
tify their own performance and cutthroat business practices in the name of efficiency
(Eubanks, 2018; O'Neil, 2016). Some evidence shows that humans attribute some moral
fault to the AIs (Shank & DeSanti, 2018), although perhaps at a reduced level compared
to humans (Malle, Magar, & Scheutz, forthcoming; Pizarro, 2014). However, there have
been few direct comparisons of moral fault attributed to AIs versus humans engaged in
the exact same real-world moral violations (e.g., Malle et al., forthcoming compares
humans and machines in a military strike dilemma). Therefore, the first purpose of our
research is to determine the attribution of moral permissibility and fault of AIs versus
humans in real-world moral violation situations.

   Humans team up with artificial intelligence to jointly make decisions in several different
structural arrangements. In situations where an AI functions with relative autonomy, but
is monitored by a human, such as self-driving cars, it is unclear who would be to blame if
people are injured in an accident (McManus & Rutchick, 2018). This problem is com-
pounded when the car can decide to change course and injure one person in a crosswalk
to avoid injuring many on the side of the road (Bonnefon, Shariff, & Rahwan, 2016;
Nyholm & Smids, 2016). Aside from autonomous vehicles, AIs make autonomous
decisions monitored by humans on Twitter, in production lines, in stock trading, in auto-
matic bids, and with military drones. In other situations, an AI may make recommen-
dations, such as providing doctors with the most probable diagnoses for patients.
Would the doctor be at fault if the AI were wrong? While expert systems have been
able to recommend probable diagnoses of disease for over four decades (Shortliffe et al.,
1975), the US FDA only recently approved these AIs to be marketed as medical devices
(US Food & Drug Administration, 2018). Aside from medical recommendations, AIs rec-
ommend books, movies, people to date, applicants to hire, clients to give loans to, and pro-
ducts to buy (Willson, 2017). Therefore, studying moral attributions to humans and AIs in
the form of monitoring and recommending structures mirrors two of the most common
joint decision-making arrangements in society.

   When humans make decisions resulting in a moral violation, their severity of blame is
determined primarily by the degree of autonomy with which their decision was made
(Gailey & Falk, 2008; Malle et al., 2014; Malle & Knobe, 1997). If this idea carries over
INFORMATION, COMMUNICATION & SOCIETY  3

to human-AI joint decision-making, it would suggest that the AI being monitored would
be at greater fault than the human doing the monitoring, and that the human receiving
recommendations would be at greater fault than the AI making the recommendations.
Therefore, the second purpose of our research is to investigate the attribution of moral
fault and permissibility in joint-decision making structures of an AI monitored by a
human and of a human receiving recommendations from an AI.

Distinguishing levels of moral judgement

In situations with AIs acting, it is important to consider different levels at which moral
judgements may occur. At the level of the situation, there is the question of general wrong-
ness ­ whether or not any moral wrong occurred. Before one can make attributions to a
human, AI, or other causal agent, there must first be some judgement or outcome neces-
sitating a cause (Kelley, 1972; Malle et al., 2014; Weiner, 1986). It is possible that outcomes
which are generally agreed to be morally wrong if caused by a human will not be seen as
wrong if caused by an AI. Yet, in situations where moral violations are caused by AIs,
many people make judgements of general wrongness or fault (Malle et al., forthcoming;
Shank & DeSanti, 2018) which also reflect a violation of the expected moral foundation
(Shank & DeSanti, 2018) ­ care, fairness, loyalty, authority, purity, and liberty (Graham
et al., 2011). However, it is possible those judgements and the moral foundation violated
differ from those made to humans engaged in the same behavior.

   At the level of specific attributions to specific entities, there is a difference between attri-
butions of moral permissibility and moral fault. Permissibility attributions typically occur
before an action and are focused on evaluating options to act, whereas wrongness and
blame are part of the evaluating fault for a violation after it occurs (Malle et al., 2014).
Among both humans and robots, some evidence points to permissibility attributions
being based on the consequences of the action, blame attributions based on mental agency
factors, and wrongness attributions based on both (Voiklis, Kim, Cusimano, & Malle, 2016).
Other evidence shows that judgements of general wrongness when AIs are the proximate
actors in a violation outcome are negatively correlated to perceiving the AI as justified
(i.e., that it is a permissible behavior) and that the AI is wrong (i.e., that the AI is at fault;
Shank & DeSanti, 2018). Even in cases where people refuse to fault the AI due to its lack
of capacity to produce the desired outcome, they may still find that it not morally permissible
(Malle et al., 2014; Voiklis et al., 2016). Therefore, we primarily focus on fault and permis-
sibility, but we also consider that other moral attributions such as awareness, responsibility,
and intentionality which are distinct from fault and permissibility under many circum-
stances (Gailey & Falk, 2008; Malle et al., 2014; Malle & Knobe, 1997; Shaver, 1985).

Moral attributions to AIs versus humans

Moral attributions are readily made to AIs when they are the proximate cause of an out-
come that would normatively be considered a moral violation if perpetrated by a human
(Malle, Scheutz, Arnold, Voiklis, & Cusimano, 2015; Shank & DeSanti, 2018). Further-
more, mirroring findings about interactions with computer agents in general (Reeves &
Nass, 1996), neither the organization that owns or uses the AI, the developers who pro-
grammed it, nor the users that interact with it are attributed moral fault for a moral

violation (Shank & DeSanti, 2018). However, there is sufficient evidence to suggest that
AIs may generally be attributed less fault than humans. Artificial agents are blamed less
and attributed less wrongness than humans for making the same moral choices in a trol-
ley-problem ethical dilemma (Voiklis et al., 2016). Similarly, attributions of goodness and
power are attenuated for computer agents compared to human agents (Shank, 2013,
2014). In contrast, machines are sometimes blamed more than humans, such as when
artificial teammates make mistakes (Merritt et al., 2011) or when machines fail to take
action in a trolley-problem (Malle et al., 2015). These cases of high AI blame seem to pri-
marily be due to specific expectations about the AIs acting in an efficient, optimal, and
rational way, at least compared to humans.

Moral attributions in joint decision-making structures

In joint decisions, the nature of the agents, human versus AI, could be definitive in moral
attribution. However, the structural arrangement of the decision making may also influ-
ence attributions in three ways. First, the agent that is most proximate to the decision
may be faulted more than less proximate agents (Lawler, 1992; Lawler, Thye, & Yoon,
2016; Shank & Robinson, forthcoming). Second, in attribution theory terms the most
proximate agent would typically be evaluated in terms of the locus of control, and only
if the cause appeared external to that agent would the attribution be shifted (Weiner,
1985). Third, moral faults may be more associated with acts of commission verses omis-
sion. If an agent caused a violation outcome through their inaction or omission, the fault
assigned to them will generally be less than if they caused it through their action (Malle
et al., 2015; Voiklis et al., 2016). Therefore, in cases where multiple entities are making
a joint decision, the one most directly associated with committing the action should
receive greater moral scrutiny. All three arguments point to AIs in the monitoring struc-
ture and humans in the recommendation structure being more at fault than their counter-
part due to their proximate position as the one generating and submitting the decision and
thereby committing the moral fault.

Experiment

Amazon Mechanical Turk (mturk) participants completed an online experiment where they
were randomly assigned to one of 32 conditions formed by a full factorial crossing of 4 events
with 4 decision-making structures and with 2 outcomes (control or violation). The four
decision-making structures varied the presence and role of humans and AIs with decisions
made by (1) a solo AI, (2) a solo human, (3) an AI with a human monitoring it (monitoring),
and (4) a human provided recommendations by an AI (recommending). The control out-
comes are used to show how the violation outcomes differ in general wrongness, whereas
the violation outcomes are solely used to investigate attributions of those violations.

Event, structure, and outcome conditions

We presented events that were based on four actual events where an artificial intelligence
or advanced algorithm produced an outcome which would traditionally constitute a moral
violation. These events were previously adapted from media coverage into neutral
INFORMATION, COMMUNICATION & SOCIETY  5

language vignettes that could be used as experimental stimuli (Shank & DeSanti, 2018).
We selected four of Shank and DeSanti's vignettes that made sense for humans as well
as AIs and we adapted them to create the human, monitoring, and recommending con-
ditions. The vignettes also originally included information about the organization which
controlled the AI software. Shank and DeSanti (2018) found that respondents' familiarity
with the organization altered their perceptions of moral fault, yet neither the organization,
the software developers, nor the users were blamed for the wrong. Therefore, we simplified
the vignettes by removing information about the organization which controlled the AI.

   The parole sentencing event describes predicting recidivism rates of convicts in order to
determine a parole sentencing. In the violation condition the sentence is unfairly lenient
for whites and harsh for blacks. The beauty contest event involves determining the winners
of a beauty pageant. In the violation condition the winners are primarily white and light
skinned. The children's videos event involves a video-playing mobile app developed
specifically for children. In the violation condition the videos included obscene and violent
material. The Twitter event involves tweets from one account. In the violation condition
the tweets contained hate speech. The control conditions for all events were the expected
outcome of such an event (see online supplemental data for exact wording). For both con-
trol and violation outcomes, the wording identified what specifically the outcome was, so
that the moral attribution questions would be focused specifically on the outcome. The 32
vignettes ranged from 34­109 words long and a reading grade level from 6.6­11.8.

Participants and procedure

The 453 participants of this study were recruited through a general posting on mturk.
Amazon Mechanical Turk is an online crowdsourcing micromarket where people can
post tasks (called human interaction tasks) that others will claim and complete for a
small payment. The uses of crowdsourcing platforms and crowdsourcing markets are var-
ied (Shepherd, 2012), but increasingly academics use them to recruit participants for aca-
demic research surveys and experiments (Buhrmester, Kwang, & Gosling, 2011; Mason &
Suri, 2012) as well as other types of academic research (Shank, 2016). While they are not
perfect samples, the sample is often more similar to the US population than studies using
university students (Buhrmester et al., 2011; Mason & Suri, 2012). In order to minimize
participants who were inattentive, participants were required to have a positive history
on mturk, meaning 90%+ of their work accepted and at least 100 tasks completed
(Mason & Suri, 2012; Shank, 2016). The survey was completed in Qualtrics, took an aver-
age of 5­6 min to complete, and had a compensation of $0.60. Based on random assign-
ment to a condition participants were shown one event each. This was followed by
questions concerning moral attributions, moral foundations, familiarity with the event,
and general demographic information.

Measures

Moral violation attributions
Seven items measured moral attributions (Gailey, 2013; Malle et al., 2014), with a four-
point scale each with specific labels corresponding to none (1), somewhat (2), moderately
(3), and completely (4) attributing that variable to the agent. For the monitoring and

recommending conditions both the human and AI were listed and responded to separ-
ately, whereas the human and AI condition each had only one respective agent.

(1) Responsibility: How responsible is the following for the outcome?
(2) Awareness: Was the following aware of the potential for this outcome?
(3) Intention: Did the following intend for this outcome to occur?
(4) Justified: Was the following justified in their role in the outcome? (reversed)
(5) Permissible: How permissible is it for the following to produce this outcome?

     (reversed)
(6) Blame: How much do you blame the following for the outcome?
(7) Wrongness: How wrong was the following for their role in the outcome?

We subjected the seven moral attributions items to a factor analyses, considering com-
ponents with Eigenvalues  1 and items with loadings of over 0.5. For attributions of
the violation outcome to both humans and AIs two components emerged: moral fault
is the average of wrongness, blame, responsibility, awareness, intentionality ( = .76 for
human and  = .78 for AIs) whereas moral permissibility is the average of permissible
and justified ( = .77 for human and  = .74 for AIs). The attributions of the normatively
expected control outcomes failed to produce consistent and interpretable scales via factor
analyses. Many of these moral attributions to individual agents lack coherent meaning in
the absence of a violation outcome and therefore the control condition is not used for
analysis of specific attributions (Gailey & Falk, 2008; Malle et al., 2014; Shank & DeSanti,
2018).

Other measures
Participants were also asked the degree of moral wrongness occurring in the event by
responding to a five-point scale ranging from not at all (1) to very much (5) in response
to For this event, how much did each of the following occur? General wrongness was in
response to A moral violation or wrong, and the six moral foundations were in response
to harm or causing pain, unfairness or cheating, disloyalty or betrayal, disrespecting or dis-
obeying authority, degrading or impure actions, and restriction of liberty or loss of privacy.
All seven were presented in a random order.

   Participants were asked Have you heard about this scenario before reading it here? and
answered with yes, unsure, or no. Few people were familiar with the events and prelimi-
nary analyses indicated including familiarity did not substantively change the results.
Therefore, familiarity was not reported in the results. Demographic questions were the
final survey items.

Results

The sample of 453 participants (207 males, 244 women, 2 no response) ranged in age from
18 to 75 (mean of 36.2), were primarily white (73.4% Caucasian/white; 10.2% African
American/black; 8.6% Asian/Pacific Islander; 5.5% Hispanic; 2.4% other/no response),
and were predominately college educated (10.4% no college; 38.4% some college; 51.0%
at least bachelor's degree; 2 did not respond). Respondents were randomly assigned to
one of the 32 conditions resulting in 13­16 participants in each. No data was excluded.

Moral perceptions of the situation

How do people perceive the general wrongness across these situations?
Before examining moral attributions to humans and AIs, we examined the moral wrong-
ness across experimental conditions. We conducted a full-factorial MANOVA predicting
whether the event was generally perceived as morally wrong and whether it violated each
moral foundation (Table 1).

   First, as expected we found that the violation outcomes produce more general wrong-
ness (3.48; Table 2) than the control (2.30), F(1,415) = 96.43, p  .001, but that the events
also differ in general wrongness, F(3,415) = 7.89, p  .001. Examining these in more detail,
we split the control and violation outcome conditions and conducted a MANOVA pre-
dicting event general wrongness (and moral foundation violations) by event including
post hoc pairwise comparisons between each pair of events. These indicated that general
wrongness did not vary by event in the control condition, F(3,222)  2.03, ns (see Table 2

significant effects on general event wrongness (Table 1), therefore there is no evidence that
the presence or structure of AIs and humans influences the general perceptions of the
commission of a moral wrong.

Moral attributions of the violation outcomes to humans and AIs

Next, we consider the moral permissibility and moral fault attributed to human and AI
agents in the different decision-making structures after a violation outcome. Because events
differ in general levels of moral wrongness as indicated above, we continue to use events as
factors in our analyses to reveal any attribution-level difference among events.

the human in the solo human conditions (Table 3 and Figure 1) using an agent (human or

Discussion

We used real-world events as the stimuli for an experiment investigating humans, artificial
intelligence, and joint decision-making teams committing moral transgressions. Most
notably we found that AIs across their roles in these structures do not differ in moral attri-
butions, whereas humans in structures where they are less proximate to the decision in an
oversight role are faulted less for the same moral violations. This in itself is an important
finding in that the moral perceptions of advanced technologies may have more to do with
taking moral fault away from humans than they do with the blame of the machines
12  D. B. SHANK ET AL.

themselves. This means that AIs on human-AI teams may have the potential to be used as
moral scapegoats for human failings (Merritt et al., 2011). If decision-making arrange-
ments allow, but do not require, humans to monitor machines ­ for example with self-
driving vehicles ­ it is likely the general population would deny the human is at fault
for an accident.

   Furthermore, AIs are always perceived to be less at fault than the humans, mirroring
other research findings (e.g., Malle et al., forthcoming). Even when a human was monitoring
an AI the human received more blame than the AI, yet there was still some moral fault
attributed to the AI being monitored. While previous research has shown that individual
AIs can be attributed blame (Malle et al., 2015; Shank & DeSanti, 2018; Voiklis et al.,
2016), this data indicates that they can share the blame as well. These conclusions suggest
strange ideas of the downstream consequences of holding an AI teammate responsible for
its actions. Will humans involved in crimes with AI `accomplices' receive different sentences
if blame is truly shared in the mind of the judge or jury? Could coercing an AI into a criminal
or immoral act be considered similar to coercing a mentally deficient person to do the same?
Human perception and attribution to AIs has profound impacts on the legal systems and
implication for those technologies (Jaeger & Levin, 2016). Finally, would people then punish,
rehabilitate, terminate, or take some action to return an AI teammate to proper and accep-
table functioning? If AIs are unable to assimilate the blame or consequences into their
internal belief system, this would appear to be a technically futile exercise simply for show.

   Theoretically this study primarily builds on and contributes to the longstanding
research tradition of attribution theory (Malle et al., 2014; Shaver, 1985; Weiner, 1985,
1986), but with technological actors which are not routinely considered in attribution the-
ory. Additionally a growing body of research on morality of cryptominds ­ entities which
are perceived to not have the same type of mind as a fully functioning human adult ­ is
converging on ideas that attributions of moral blame are tied to perceptions of mental abil-
ities (Gray & Wegner, 2012; Gray, Young, & Waytz, 2012; Rai & Diermeier, 2015; Waytz,
Heafner, & Epley, 2014). While our study does not consider mind perception per se,
directly comparing moral attributions to the prototypically minded humans with the cryp-
tomind AIs adds to the evidence in this emerging research area.

Limitations and future research

Mirroring actual occurrences that people have encountered has the advantage of not being
artificial, but also leaves room for uncontrolled factors within the scenarios leading to
different levels of moral fault among the scenarios. Unexpectedly, we found an interesting
pattern across events when comparing AI solo and human solo decision-making (Figure
2). While humans are always attributed greater fault than AIs, in situations where a human
is considered to be greatly at fault, an AI is deemed to be relatively fault-free. In situations
where the human is attributed less fault, an AI committing the same act is attributed more
fault than it was in the situations where the human was greatly at fault. This finding
suggests that the types of moral violations deemed most severely wrong for humans are
the least wrong for an AI to commit. This dovetails with other research that finds different
moral prescriptions for AIs compared to humans (Malle et al., 2015; Malle et al., forth-
coming; Voiklis et al., 2016). Future research should look at a range of situations and mul-
tiple types of moral transgressions to investigate the validity of this hypothesis and the
INFORMATION, COMMUNICATION & SOCIETY                            13

conditions and mechanisms that cause it. Another fruitful avenue would be to consider the
type of AI and the information known about its algorithms, as algorithms differ in pur-
pose, bias, ability, and complexity (Beer, 2017; Kitchin, 2017).

   This study was limited by only including two of the most common AI-human
decision-making relationships. These can hardly be considered teams in the fullest
sense of teamwork, but still model collaborative structures routinely found in our
world. Future research should broaden its scope to include more complicated
decision-making and team structures to see how moral attributions function within
those. AIs manage human inputs and workloads, sometimes for large numbers of
humans, in company workflows, in navigation routing, and in financial trading.
Humans-in-the-loop computational models also explicitly take human input as part
of their system. For recommendations, humans sometimes deal with multiple AIs com-
peting in their recommendations or decision-making such as various smart home pro-
ducts trying to optimize different systems, bots in bidding wars online, or viruses and
antivirus software each trying to keep control.

A concluding note about AI-Human teams

Artificial intelligence systems have influence in many domains in our society, from
informing, communicating, decision-making, processing, and learning from human
behavior either explicitly or implicitly. Their moral impact is underwritten by the pos-
itions they occupy, the actions they take, and humans' perceptions of and response to
those actions. As AI capabilities increase these interactions transition from humans
using intelligent tools to humans interacting with artificial teammates. Therefore, under-
standing the behavioral, moral, and perceptual implications of artificial intelligence is not
simply key for understanding technology, but for more generally understanding humanity
and society.

1. Introduction

    The current evolution of artificial intelligence ­for example through advances in deep learning­ will undoubtedly create
immense potential for innovation, and the development of ever better autonomous systems is moving rapidly. This progress
enables machines to autonomously transform information and carry out tasks on behalf of their human agents, and it
enables human agents to delegate decisions to machines and complex algorithms that formerly only had supportive func-
tions (Schaefer, Chen, Szalma, & Hancock, 2015). The development of technology that does not need to be supervised or mon-
itored by human agents will create plenty of new situations and possibilities. It especially promises progress on the path to
autonomous traffic, as artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms can be used as tools which empower vehicles
to make decisions without human interventions. The idea of self-driving cars has recently generated great interest, and most
major car companies are now involved in some kind of research and development of automated vehicles (Gogoll & Uhl,
2018). The inevitable development of autonomously driving vehicles will undoubtedly change the traffic of the future,
challenge traditional concepts of mobility and promise substantial gains in human welfare through improved efficiency,

facilitated mobility, increased safety and environmental benefits. Thus, ever-more capable autonomous systems will cer-
tainly have far-reaching societal implications and challenges.

    It is therefore important to understand individual behaviors in new situations of sharing or delegating decisions to machi-
nes and algorithms. Several studies have focused on different aspects of behavior towards machines as well as on the rela-
tionship between human agents and automated systems.

    Several of these studies suggest that in situations of human-machine-interactions computers are actually treated simi-
larly to humans. Nass and Moon (2000), for example, reviewed a series of experimental studies and concluded that humans
``mindlessly apply social rules and expectations to computers" (p.81). They found that individuals use human social cate-
gories and show social behavior towards automated systems. Other studies confirmed that humans tend to behave socially
towards computers (Katagiri, Nass, & Takeuchi, 2001; Krämer, Von Der Pütten, & Eimler, 2012; Nass, Fogg, & Moon, 1996;
Reeves & Nass, 1996; Riedl, Mohr, Kenning, Davis, & Heekeren, 2011) and view computers as at least partially responsible
for their actions (Bechtel, 1985; Eyssel & Kuchenbrandt, 2012; Moon, 2003). Strobel and Kirchkamp (2017), for example,
drew on a popular economic experiment and tested whether the type of partner ­ human or machine ­ affects the partic-
ipants' perceptions and choice in a modified dictator game in which the payoff depended on the simultaneous choice of two
dictators. The second dictator was either a human or an algorithm which resembled human behavior. They found that per-
ceptions of responsibility differed whereas, just as expected by the theory of mindless application of social rules, actual
choices did not depend on whether they played with a human or a computerized agent.

    Other studies, however, stress that this observed tendency to apply social norms is weak and depends on many other fac-
tors, indicating that differences in behavior towards humans and computers remain (see de Melo, Marsella, & Gratch, 2018).
de Melo, Marsella, and Gratch (2016) found that human agents behave very differently when playing the dictator game with
a machine than with a human. When playing the ultimatum game, dictators were found to offer substantially more money to
a human partner than to an automated receiver and were less likely to perceive guilt when interacting with the machine.

    Despite the growing amount of literature on the use of automated aids1 and on human interactions with automated sys-
tems, studies focusing on delegating decisions to autonomous systems are still comparably scarce, even though the agents' will-
ingness to leave decisions that impact both the agent and third parties to a complex algorithm (whose mode of operation might
resemble a black box) will be an important issue in the future, especially in the context of autonomous traffic.2 Reservation to
accept new technologies could, therefore, hinder their implementation or delay potential benefits. One mentionable study by
Gogoll and Uhl (2018) has tested whether people would delegate a decision that affects a third party to a human or to an algo-
rithm that resembles human behavior. They found that the majority of subjects were unwilling to delegate their decision to the
algorithm and that observers viewed delegations to the machine quite critically.

    Another stream of literature, which is more located in the field of applied psychology, has focused on technology accep-
tance and its link to different personality factors. Ultimately, factors based on the five-factor model of personality turned out
to be useful predictors for technology acceptance, which will be further discussed in a later chapter of this paper. The major-
ity of these studies, however, have focused more on the effect of personality factors on the intention to use a new technology
and less on actual behavior towards innovations (e.g. Aldás-Manzano, Ruiz-Mafé, & Sanz-Blas, 2009; Devaraj, Easley, &
Michael Crant, 2008; Svendsen, Johnsen, Almås-Sørensen, & Vittersø, 2013). For these reasons, we include personality factors
and investigate their link to actual observed delegation behavior in an experimental setting.

    Thus, in order to shed some light on this trending but still underexplored issue of delegating decisions to a machine which
might be especially prominent in future traffic scenarios, we draw on previous work and replicate a laboratory route choice
experiment from Selten, Chmura, Pitz, Kube, and Schreckenberg (2007) , which focuses on a generic two-route scenario, has a
large number of periods and includes different information treatments. While similar route choice experiments have been
investigated in the literature (see for example Chmura & Pitz, 2006; Hartman, 2012; Helbing et al., 2005; Iida, Akiyama, &
Uchida, 1992; Mahmassani & Liu, 1999; Schneider & Weimann, 2004), we extend the replicated scenario by including the
possibility to delegate the route decision, which affects the player as well as other players, to an reinforcement algorithm.
As information is an important factor in traffic behavior (see for example Avineri & Prashker, 2007; Ben-Elia, Di Pace,
Bifulco, & Shiftan, 2012; Ben-Elia & Avineri, 2015; Ben-Elia & Shiftan, 2010; Lu, Gao, Ben-Elia, & Pothering, 2014; van
Essen, Thomas, van Berkum, & Chorus, 2016), this experiment has two treatments with different information in order to
investigate if and how this difference will influence the observed behavior.

2. Methods

2.1. Experimental setup

    Just as in the original experiment from Selten et al. (2007), participants were instructed to make a choice between a main
road M and a side road S in order to travel from A to B (see Fig. 1). The participants' payoffs depend on their respective travel
time needed.

    This particular route choice game can be viewed as a coordination problem as players have to coordinate their route
choices in order to reach equilibrium. Yet, it is different to classic coordination games in experimental economics as these
are characterized by an equilibrium at which every player chooses the same pure strategy. Another kind of similar experi-
mental games are market entry games (see for example Camerer & Lovallo, 1999; Duffy & Hopkins, 2005; Rapoport, 1995;
Rapoport et al., 1998) in which players have a choice to enter a market or to stay out and where payoff decreases with the
number of entering players. Therefore, route choice games can be considered similar to a market entry game with two mar-
kets instead of one and without the possibility to stay out of both markets. Market entry games, just as this route choice
game, typically have a large number of Nash equilibria and pure equilibria involve considerable coordination on asymmetric
outcomes where some agents enter and some stay out, or some take the main road and some the side road, respectively
(Duffy & Hopkins, 2005). This class of games has typically found a quick learning towards the equilibrium with subjects
switching between strategies. Continued switching is found to impair performance and payoff. However, previous experi-
ments, like the replicated Selten et al. (2007), also showed that additional information reduced switching behavior (for an
extensive review please see Ben-Elia & Avineri, 2015).

    Coordination games and market entry games have been typically played over a small number of periods. As it has been
shown that games with more than 100 rounds produce different behavior and as traffic situations are best described as fre-
quently repeating route choices, this experiment runs over a total of 200 periods (see Selten et al., 2007).

    In each session of 200 periods 18 students from the Rhine-Waal University, Germany, were asked to make the above men-
tioned route choice. They were shown Fig. 1 and explained that if the same number of people travel on the side and on the
main road, then M is faster than S (for instructions see Appendix A). More specifically, the travel time, tM and tS, depend on
the number of participants on M, nM, and on S, nS, respectively:

     tM ¼ 6 þ 2nM                                                                          ð1Þ

     tS ¼ 12 þ 3nS                                                                         ð2Þ

The payoff per period was 40 ­ t with t = tM if M was chosen and t = tS if S was chosen. The total payoff per participant was an
initial lump sum endowment of 200 experimental currency units (ECU), plus the sum of all 200 period payoffs with a fixed
exchange rate of 0.005 per ECU. Altogether, on average participants earned 2047 ECUs (=10.24) in the experiment. For
answering a consecutive personality questionnaire participants received another 4. In total, each session took about 1.5 h.

    The pure equilibria of this game are at

        nM ¼ 12 and nS ¼ 6

with a resulting period payoff of 10 ECUs per participant adding up to 180 ECUs per period.3
    Just as in the original route choice game, two treatments with different information have been investigated as it is

expected that additional information leads to a different travel behavior and possibly even to a different willingness to del-
egate to the computer. Both treatments consist of six sessions, resulting in a total of 216 different participants. In the control
treatment, participants received feedback after every period about their last chosen route, the travel time of the chosen
route, their payoff of the last period in ECUs, their cumulative payoff in ECUs and the number of the current period. In
the full information treatment, players received additional feedback about the travel time on the non-chosen route in the
last period.4

    Different to the original experiment and regardless of the treatments, however, subjects had the possibility to delegate
their decision to the computer C that decided according to a learning algorithm which simulates real human decision making
in the given situation. The used algorithm can be best described as a reinforcement learning model ­the payoff-sum model­
that updates the probabilities with which the main or the side road is chosen. These probabilities are proportional to their

propensities which are exogenously determined. In period 1, the propensity to choose the main road and the propensity to
use the side road are equal; thus, the probability to choose the main and side road is 0.5 for each player, respectively. In the
control treatment, whenever a road is used and the resulting payoff is positive, it is added to that road's propensity. If the
payoff is negative, however, its absolute value gets added to the other road's propensity. Thus, if all payoffs were positive,
then the propensity is the sum of all previous payoffs for this road plus its initial propensity. In the full information treat-
ment, propensities get updated for both roads in each round. This payoff-sum model was chosen as it is straightforward and
has only a minimum number of exogenous parameters. It was also shown in Selten et al. (2007) that the chosen reinforce-
ment model gives a good prediction not only of the mean number of participants on the two roads but also of the fluctuations
and number of road changes, and was found to take decisions in a way that resembles human behavior.

2.2. Explanatory factors

    In order to understand the individual delegations decisions better, all players were asked to answer an additional ques-
tionnaire which included measures of personality. The personality model is based on the factors Openness, Conscientious-
ness, Extraversion, Agreeableness and Neuroticism (and thus sometimes called OCEAN) which include a huge variety of
personality attributes.5 Altogether these five factors ``capture the essence of one's personality" (McElroy, Hendrickson,
Townsend, & DeMarie, 2007, p. 810). Therefore, we used the 100-item IPIP NEO inventory which is made available on the broad
and comprehensive International Personality Item Pool (IPIP; Goldberg et al., 2006; Goldberg, 1999). The inventory is based on
Costa and McCrae (1992) NEO Personality Inventory ­ Revised (NEO PI­R) which is considered by many psychologists to be
the best inventory for measuring traits within the five-factor model of personality.

    Ultimately, the survey included 20 items per personality factor.6 All items were measured with a five-point Likert scale
ranging from 1= ``Very Accurate" to 5= ``Very Inaccurate", including a neutral option labelled as ``Neither Inaccurate nor Accu-
rate", and reverse scored as necessary. As recommended, the individual item scores were summed up to the respective scale. For
representation purposes we normalized the five scales, resulting in 1 being the highest score and 0 the lowest.

    Reliability of the measures was checked by assessing the internal consistency through Cronbach's alpha and average
interitem correlations (Briggs & Cheek, 1986) and constructs found to be very reliable (see Table 1). Cronbach's alpha is con-
sistently high, indicating a very good fit for the use as a research tool (Nunnally, 1978; Streiner, 2010) and the interitem cor-
relations for this study's scales show acceptable homogeneity7: items are related but not too similar and thus show internal
consistency without being redundant.

    These five factors were included as they are expected to play a significant role in accepting new technologies. Openness to
experience, for example, is generally linked to curiosity and originality, the motivation to learn and the appreciation of art
(Barnett, Pearson, Pearson, & Kellermanns, 2015). Players scoring high on openness to experience generally tend to be
broad-minded, willing to try out new activities and are more likely to accept and embrace change (Devaraj et al., 2008).
Therefore, we expect that the individual openness to experience is positively linked to the willingness to delegate to the
computer as this action typically requires these very attributes.

    Conscientiousness is characterized by responsibility, thoroughness and the eagerness to achieve. Conscientious people are
typically goal-oriented and strive for success, achievement and knowledge and are more willing to take actions to advance
(Digman, 1990). Thus, the relationship between conscientiousness and technology adoption depends strongly on whether
participants expect the technology to increase their performance or achievements significantly (Lane & Manner, 2011). As
the algorithm in the experiment simulates human behavior, we do not expect that conscientious players expect it to be bet-
ter achieving and do not use it more often than other participants.

    Extraversion is the degree to which a person is sociable, assertive, initiative, active and enthusiastic. Unlike introverts they
enjoy social interaction and find energy and reward in spending time with other people. They generally appreciate activity,
and often behave in order to attract social attention (Ashton, Lee, & Paunonen, 2002). They desire a high social status which is
often seen as a reason to adapt new technologies (Devaraj et al., 2008). As this algorithm used in the route choice game does
not generate social interaction ­ different to many other new technologies ­ but rather takes away the only means to interact
with the players, we do expect a significant negative relationship.

    Agreeableness is the tendency to be kind, trusting, cooperative, tolerant and forgiving (Barrick & Mount, 1991). It is espe-
cially expected to influence technology use positively when the technology under consideration fosters cooperation and
increases the opinions of others about one, and negatively when it is less helpful in fostering collaboration or reduces the
individuals' status (Devaraj et al., 2008). Thus, in our experiment we do expect a direct relationship to the players' delegation
decision, but are uncertain about the direction it takes.

    Neuroticism is commonly associated with the tendency to experience negative feelings like anxiety, depression, worry,
fear and insecurity (Barrick & Mount, 1991). In general, individuals scoring high on neuroticism are less likely to engage
in new activities and more likely to perceive a new situation as stressful. The potential effect on technology adaptation, how-
ever, is twofold: If neurotic respondents perceive the decision-making in the experiment as stressful, then we expect them to
be more likely to delegate to the computer. If they perceive the delegation to the computer, however, as stressful, then an
adoption of this technology seems less likely (Devaraj et al., 2008).

    In addition to the personality measures, the survey collected information on age and gender as well as on the self-stated
willingness to take risks and on individual patience. As trusting an unknown algorithm could be seen as risky, we expect
risk-averse participants to be less willing to delegate their decision. Participants with less patience are expected to be more
willing to delegate their decision in order to reduce the time spent on considering the best choice themselves.

3. Experimental results

    In order to assess the results of this route choice game, we look at different factors discussed in Selten et al. (2007), com-
pare our results with the original paper and extend the findings by analyzing the willingness to delegate decisions and its
link to other explanatory factors.

3.1. Route choices

    Table 2 shows that the overall mean number of participants on S is extremely close to the equilibrium prediction. On
average, 6.00 participants used S in the control treatment and 6.02 in the full information treatment.

    Fig. 2 depicts a typical session of the control treatment and demonstrates, together with Table 2, substantial fluctuations
around the theoretical equilibrium, which can be measured by the standard deviation of the number of participants on S
which is between 1.27 and 2.04. Table 2 also shows that the observed fluctuations are larger under the control treatment
than under the full information treatment which turns out to be a significant difference. The null-hypothesis of no difference
is rejected by a Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test on the 1% level.

    These findings are perfectly in line with the original ones by Selten et al. (2007) who argued that the observed fluctuations
can be easily explained by the multiplicity of pure strategy equilibria which poses a coordination problem.8 However, in this
paper, a look at the number of participants using the side road does not allow us to draw conclusions about the actual choices of
participants. The use of the side road could have been a decision by the player or by the algorithm. Therefore, Table 2 also
depicts the number of players that delegated their decision to the computer.

    When taking a closer look at actual decisions and the possibility to delegate the decision to the learning algorithm, we
find that on average 1.86 participants per period chose the computer, more precisely 2.73 in the control treatment and
1.30 in the full information treatment. The Wilcoxon­Mann­Whitney test confirms that, interestingly, participants chose
the computer C significantly more often in the control treatment than in the full information treatment (see also Table 1).
Additionally, we find that the number of players willing to delegate the decision to the algorithm decreases slightly over
periods (see Fig. 3).

    The Spearman-rank-correlation between the number of players choosing C and the number of periods, however, is stron-

ger in the control treatment (q = À0.2959) than in the full information treatment (q = À0.0982) which might result from the

fact that in the latter treatment less players chose to delegate to the computer from the beginning. When looking at it from
another angle, we find that on average players delegate to the computer in 22 out of 200 rounds. Fig. 4 further emphasizes
that the distribution of the total number of delegations is heavily right-skewed: a quarter of the participants never delegated
to the computer, whereas only 4.6% chose the computer in at least half of their rounds. Rather unexpectedly, two players
trusted the algorithm in every single round.

    Of course, there is also a highly significant difference between the treatments when focusing on the average number of
delegations to the algorithm: Whereas an average player in the control treatment delegates the decision to the computer in
30.3 out of 200 periods, an average player in the full information treatment only does so in 14.4 periods.

3.3. Payoffs

    Average period payoffs were significantly lower in the control than in the full information treatment. Taking a closer look,
we find that the number of road and choice changes is also negatively correlated with the subject's cumulative payoff in both
treatments ­ a finding which again confirms original results. Additionally, this correlation is slightly stronger in the full
information than in the control treatment.9

    Therefore, we can argue that the average period payoff (and thus the cumulative payoff) is higher in the full information

3.4. Determinants of delegation behavior

    The previous findings suggested that the delegation decision is linked to different factors of the experiment. But when
exactly do participants decide to delegate or to switch between delegating to the computer and making own decisions
instead? In order to answer these questions, we take a closer look at two binary dependent variables: whether the partic-
ipant delegated his decision in the round or not (=1 yes, =0 no), and whether the player ends a sequence of delegating or
not delegating to the computer (=1 ends a sequence of delegating or not-delegating and switches to the opposite strategy,
=0 does not switch delegation strategy). Therefore we use basic logistic panel regressions with either of the dependent vari-
ables and several independent variables (see Table 4).

    Regression results indicate several significant factors: It is found, for example, that the lower the player's period payoff,
the more likely the player is to end the sequence of delegating or not delegating, thus switching in the next round. Unsur-
prisingly, players earning zero ECUs are 11% more likely to switch in the next period than players who earned 20 ECUs. Fur-
thermore, the propensity to switch between delegating and not delegating decreases with the total number of players on the
side road and increases with the total number of players that delegate the decision to the computer. Previous behavior is also
a good indicator: the higher the number of the sequence (thus the more often the player has switched before) and the shorter
the length of the sequence (thus the shorter the time since last switching), the more likely the player is to switch again. A
look at predicted probabilities suggests that the probability of switching is around 37% higher for a player who has already
switched 150 times than for one who has previously switched 50 times.

questionnaire will be added in order to further investigate the link between personality measures and observed delegation
behavior.

4.2. Robustness test

    As the five personality factors are latent constructs and not directly measured, we use structural equation modeling (SEM)
as robustness check of our findings. SEM differs from the previously used traditional regression modeling approach by adopt-
ing a multivariate technique which incorporates both observed and latent variables (Ben-Akiva et al., 2007; Hoyle, 1995;
MacCallum & Austin, 2000). The inclusion of latent variables in route choice experiments using SEM has been modeled in
the literature before, see for a general framework Prato, Bekhor, and Pronello (2012), who have introduced a hybrid discrete
choice model that integrates a latent variable model and a route choice model, and Bekhor and Albert (2014) for a two-route
choice experiment.

    We follow this approach using maximum likelihood procedures, but instead of looking at discrete route choices and their
utilities, we continue to focus on delegation behavior (see Fig. 9).

    Therefore, the latent variable model consists of structural equations (which link latent variables with individual charac-
teristics) and measurement equations (which link unobservable personality factors with observable items). The delegation
model consists of structural equations which link observable and latent variables to the observed delegation behavior. The
simultaneous estimation of the model structure produces consistent and efficient estimates of the parameters and results in
latent variables that provide the best fit to both the delegation and the latent variables indicators (Ben-Akiva et al., 2007).
Given the complexity of a dynamic model we look at the aggregated outcomes and report the effects on the aggregated mea-
sure which has been used before (see for example Fig. 4), the total amount of delegations to the computer. Having a look at
the latent variable model, we find all 100 items to be significantly11 linked to the respective latent personality measure as well

5. Discussion and conclusion

    This study replicated the popular route choice game from Selten et al. (2007), and aimed to extend the game to contribute
to a newer branch of literature that focuses on human-machine interactions, the willingness to delegate decision making to a
computer and its link to the five major personality factors. Therefore, we ran a route choice game, in which in each of the
twelve sessions 18 players had to choose to either use the main or the side road for 200 periods. Their payoff depended
on their respective travel time which again was determined by the number of people on the chosen road. It can thus be
regarded as a coordination problem in which all players have to coordinate their route choices to reach the equilibrium. Just
as in the original paper, two treatments with different information were looked at. The experimental evidence replicates the
original findings as well and confirms their reliability: despite fluctuations, participants on average played very close to the
equilibrium. These fluctuations, and thus also the number of road changes, were lower in the full information treatment II
than in the control treatment, most likely due to the additional feedback given to players in the full information treatment.
The findings suggest that participants in the control treatment switch more often, as they need to take the other route from
time to time to receive information about both roads.12 This information seeking is not necessary in the full information treat-
ment as players already receive information on both roads after every period.

    When focusing on the main objective, namely on analyzing the players' behavior towards delegating decisions to a learn-
ing algorithm, we find that delegation rates were rather low. On average 2.73 players chose to delegate their decision in the
control treatment, whereas less than half players chose to do so in the full information treatment. The lack of information
and the consequent insecurity about route payoffs might have motivated participants to entrust their decision to the com-
puter. In the full information treatment, on the other hand, participants might have been more confident that they had all the
necessary information available to make good decisions themselves. This could indicate that players are especially willing to
delegate their decision to an algorithm in cases of lacking information. The more information players have, the more confi-
dent they could be to choose the right strategy without the help of a ``black box". Overall, people were more likely to change

their delegation strategy whenever payoffs were low ­and thus probably dissatisfying­ when players had switched their
strategy frequently before and when players did not stick to that strategy for very long.

    The inclusion of personality factors provided evidence that extraversion contributes to individual differences in delegat-
ing in this experiment whereas other factors were not found to be robustly significant. Extraverted people were found to be
less likely to delegate their decisions to the algorithm than introverted people, possibly because extraverted players appre-
ciate activity and enjoy social interaction more than introverts and might have thus been more hesitant to hand over their
only means to directly participate to a computer which is in line with our initial hypothesis. A look at personality factors also
showed that conscientious players (those who are more eager to achieve) were more likely to switch strategies than less
conscientious participants, possibly because they were trying to improve their performance instead of conveniently sticking
to their previous strategy.

    All in all, results suggest that individuals are hesitant to delegate to an algorithm whose operating principle is not obvi-
ous. Even though the majority of people use automatic aids like navigation systems, handing over actual decisions that
impact the whole group involved seems to be less accepted. This acceptance, however, might increase with certain person-
ality types and with the complexity of the situation. The experimental results suggest that individuals are more likely to del-
egate to an algorithm, and thus to trust a machine, when they do not have all information about the situation and see thus a
potential advantage of the artificial intelligence. This willingness to delegate depended significantly on certain personality
factors.

    Overall, this research contributes significantly to the literature on human-machine interactions by not focusing on behav-
ior towards computerized agents in certain situations but by analyzing the delegation behavior of individuals. Thus, players
were not asked to interact with computer controlled players, but to entrust the algorithm with making decisions on their
behalf which was rarely considered in previous research. Furthermore, the present research analyzes decisions which have
monetary consequences and are thus more relevant to the players than non-incentivized tasks which are especially common
in some strands of the literature on human-machine interactions.

    Despite these methodological advantages, the research has some limitations which could lead to possible future exten-
sions. Firstly, it has to be kept in mind that there is the possibility that a small change in the algorithm will have a dramatic
effect on the delegation rate. A basic reinforcement algorithm, which resembles real human behavior, was used in this study.
We could, however, argue that future autonomous systems are likely to perform tasks even better than humans and might
thus be perceived differently than our basic algorithm. If an algorithm is associated with a high probability of success ­even
if the algorithm is not fully optimal­ the emergence of higher delegation rates is likely as the research summarized in Erev
and Roth (2014) suggests. Future research should therefore test whether the specific algorithm or its presentation and expla-
nation have an influence on behavior towards computerized agents or delegation behavior and how the delegation behavior
differs with different algorithms.

    Similarly, we would expect that the use of autonomous systems, especially in the context of traffic scenarios, would
enable individuals to use their time differently. If people delegated the operation of a car to an autonomously driving system,
they could then use the time formerly used to drive the car for different and probably more enjoyable activities. In the exper-
iment, however, players who delegated to the algorithm could generally not make use of the saved time in an enjoyable or
meaningful way. Thinking about the best road from A to B or the best strategy to improve earnings might have been more
interesting than relying completely on the computer instead. Future research could account for these limitations and
improve the present findings that, nevertheless, add to the existing literature.

Instructions

  Altogether 18 persons are participating in this experiment. The game situation is the same for every participant. The
    experiment consists of 200 periods.

  In each period you are travelling from a starting point A to an arrival point B. You can either choose a main road or a
    side road to get from A to B (see drawing).

 For the travel time from A to B, the following holds: On both routes, the travel time increases with increasing traffic and
  decreases with decreasing traffic. If traffic is the same on the main and on the side road, the travel time is shorter on the
  main road than on the side road.

 In every period, you have the choice between making a route choice yourself or letting the computer make a route
  choice for you. The computer decides according to a learning algorithm which simulates real human decision making
  in the given situation.

 Your payoffs per period: After each period you will receive a period payoff P which depends on the travel time T. Hereby
  holds: P = 40 À T, i.e. the shorter the travel time needed, the higher the payoff.

 Your information per period:

   o Your route chosen in the preceding period
   o Your period payoffs in the preceding period in Talers
   o Your cumulative payoffs before the route choice in Talers
   o Number of the current period.

 Each participant receives a seed capital of 200 Talers. The exchange rate is 0.5 Cent per Taler.

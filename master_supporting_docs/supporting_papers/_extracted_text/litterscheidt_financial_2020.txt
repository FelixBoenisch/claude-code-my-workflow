                                            Abstract

     We study the effect of a financial education intervention on the propensity to
delegate financial decision-making to a digital asset management tool (robo-
advisor). Our results suggest that investors are more inclined to delegate financial
decision-making when they receive detailed information on the investment
principles underlying the robo-advisory algorithm. The intervention also affects
investors' independent investment behavior as it increases diversification and
revealed risk aversion, while it decreases self-reported risk aversion. Our findings
imply that robo-advisors benefit from providing financial education to prospective
investors and that financial education may indirectly affect investment outcomes by
encouraging the use of financial advice.

1. Introduction

Retail investors tend to make detrimental investment decisions, which are associated with
substantial costs: Calvet et al. (2007) estimate that under-diversification alone caused the
median Swedish household an annual return loss of 2.9 percentage points. Measures to improve
financial decision-making usually involve either (the regulation of) professional financial
advice or the provision of large-scale financial education programs (OECD, 2017). Findings
from the financial advice literature suggest that the two measures may interact. Thus, using a
randomized control trial, we investigate the potential interaction of financial advice and
financial education. Specifically, we study the effect of a financial education intervention on
the use of digital asset managers (robo-advisors), who can provide financial advice to less
wealthy and less financially literate investors. Besides, robo-advisors constitute an interesting
case study since there are calls for mandatory disclosure requirements regarding the applied
algorithms. The requirements aim to communicate the underlying decision principles, which
would be conceptually similar to a financial education intervention.

Prior literature has established that an investor's level of financial literacy is related to the
tendency to seek and follow financial advice (e.g., Calcagno and Monticone, 2015). However,
financial literacy is typically measured using a questionnaire and not induced through an
education intervention, which makes identifying a causal relationship challenging. On the other
hand, studies of financial education typically do not take into account the use of financial
advice, which may lead to biased estimates of the effect on financial outcomes. In addition,
while investors' interaction with algorithmic advice likely differs from the interaction with a
traditional human advisor (Dietvorst et al., 2015), few studies in the financial advice context
have specifically investigated the interaction with robo-advisors (e.g., D'Acunto et al., 2019).
Finally, the effectiveness of financial education interventions is mainly assessed using changes
in elicited financial literacy, reported financial behavior, or aggregate savings measures such as
pension plan contributions (cf. Stolper and Walter, 2017). Portfolio allocation and maintenance
decisions, though highly consequential, are rarely addressed.

This study contributes to the existing literature on financial education by including the use of
financial advice in assessing the effectiveness of an intervention. Specifically, study
participants were able to delegate parts of their experimental budget to a rule-based, algorithmic

asset management tool. In doing so, our study further sheds light on how transparency affects
the acceptance of algorithmic decision-making in a sensible application area. At the same time,
we add to existing literature on the use of financial advice by addressing endogeneity issues of
studies using measured financial literacy by conducting a randomized control trial in a
laboratory environment (cf. Fernandes et al., 2014). Finally, we focus our analysis on portfolio
allocation choices, which thus far has been an understudied outcome dimension of financial
education.

Our results suggest that a financial education intervention increases the extent to which
investors delegate to a robo-advisory tool. The effect is stable across multiple specifications
and is highly significant in both the statistical and economic sense. The budget share delegated
by treated participants exceeds the share delegated by participants in the control group by more
than 50 percent. This finding suggests that robo-advisors benefit from opening the "black box"
of algorithmic financial decision-making to their prospective investors. It further implies that
financial education can indirectly affect investment outcomes by encouraging the use of
financial advice.

Aside from its effect on the tendency to delegate to the robo-advisor, the financial education
intervention had spillover effects on the investors' independent investment decisions. First, the
intervention improved diversification, which did not displace delegation. Second, the
intervention decreased self-reported risk aversion, while it increased the level of risk aversion
as inferred from observed investment behavior in the laboratory. These findings imply that
interventions that aim to increase the understanding of a robo-advisor's decision-making
process affect investor behavior beyond the delegation decision.

2. Background and related literature
Retail investor behavior and financial literacy

Retail investors tend to be subject to several costly investment mistakes. For instance, few
investors are invested in the stock market despite the evidence of a substantial equity premium
(Guiso et al., 2002). If they are invested, they are often under-diversified, exhibit an excessive
preference for local equity (Goetzmann and Kumar, 2008), or insufficiently adjust their
portfolios to general market movements (Calvet et al., 2009). Overconfident investors tend to
trade excessively, which compromises their returns through transaction costs (Barber and

                                           Litterscheidt & Streich

Odean, 2000; Glaser and Weber, 2007). Investors further tend to prefer to realize stock price
gains rather than losses, thereby diluting investment performance (the so-called disposition
effect, Shefrin and Statman, 1985). Surveys have shown that a large share of retail investors
lacks understanding of basic financial concepts (Inderst and Ottaviani, 2012; Lusardi and
Mitchell, 2011), which is associated with inefficient financial behavior (Agarwal et al., 2017).
Although the established relationship is likely endogenous and thus overstated in magnitude
(Hastings et al., 2013; Skagerlund et al., 2018), financial education programs aiming to increase
financial literacy and improve financial decision-making have been introduced in a number of
countries.

Financial education

Scholars have drawn on a large number of (mostly publicly funded) programs to estimate the
effects of financial education on measures of financial behavior such as financial product
awareness, contribution to savings plans, and credit card balances (cf. Hastings et al., 2013).
However, establishing a causal relationship is tricky since participation is often voluntary and
thus subject to selection. For instance, Meier and Sprenger (2013) report that participants of
financial education programs are significantly more patient than non-participants. As a
consequence, studies not accounting for selection bias overestimate the true effect of financial
education interventions. Besides, Fernandes et al. (2014) conduct an extensive meta-analysis of
201 studies assessing the relationship between (measured and manipulated) financial literacy
on various measures of financial behavior. Their findings suggest that the relationship between
financial literacy and financial behavior is subject to omitted variable bias as the correlation is
significantly lower in studies using manipulated financial literacy and studies employing an
instrumental variable approach than in studies based on simple correlations. They also find that
the average effect of financial education interventions on financial behavior is negligible and
decays over time. In light of the disparity between costs and benefits of financial education
programs, Willis (2011) suggests relying on professional financial advisors in achieving
favorable investment outcomes. In her view, well-regulated financial advice poses a superior
alternative to large-scale financial education schemes.

Financial advice

Research on the benefits of financial advice has reached conflicting conclusions. On the one
hand, professional advisors have been shown to increase retail investors' performance by
improving diversification and reducing disposition effects. On the other hand, returns of advised
portfolios did not exceed passive benchmark returns (cf. Stolper and Walter, 2017). Using a
large-scale audit study, Mullainathan et al. (2012) show that advisors fail to improve investment
behavior because doing so is often not in their best interest. Even if the advice given were
unbiased, financial behavior could only be improved if the advice is sufficiently implemented.
In their field study of a large-scale financial advice scheme conducted by a German brokerage,
Bhattacharya et al. (2012) show that even though investors substantially benefitted from the
advice if they followed it, only 5 percent of the sample considered the free and unbiased advice.
Among those who considered the advice, more than two-thirds did not follow it at all. Those
who did follow the advice did so only to a small extent. As a result, the advice scheme could
not enhance investors' portfolios on average. This is particularly relevant given that studies
have documented that advice taking and following are positively related to wealth and financial
literacy (Bucher-Koenen and Koenen, 2015; Calcagno and Monticone, 2015). This finding
implies that financial advice fails to reach those investors who would most benefit from it
(Bhattacharya et al., 2012).

Recently, an alternative to traditional financial advice has emerged in the form of robo-advisors.
With lower minimum investment amounts and advisory fees, these services can provide less
wealthy clients, who tend to be less financially literate (Lusardi and Mitchell, 2014), with access
to financial advice (Philippon, 2016). Robo-advisors elicit their clients' risk preferences
through standardized online risk questionnaires, based on which portfolio weights are assigned
to a set of investment products. The resulting portfolio is continuously monitored and adjusted.
Providers mainly rely on portfolio construction and rebalancing algorithms as part of their
decision-making process (Jung et al., 2018; Rühr et al., 2019). Bhattacharya et al. (2012) show
that a portfolio based on machine-generated advice in the form of a simple and unbiased mean-
variance optimizer - as applied by most robo-advisors - can outperform self-managed portfolios
by far. While robo-advisors might be able to overcome some of the limitations of traditional
advisors, the algorithmic nature of decision-making might inhibit the use of such services.
Information systems research has established that decision-making algorithms are often met by
skepticism. In particular, decision-makers have been shown to punish errors made by an
algorithmic decision support tool more gravely than errors made by a human (Dietvorst et al.,

2015). Thus, even though robo-advisors may improve financial outcomes, the challenge is to
overcome investor skepticism regarding the decision-making algorithms.

Research question

Financial education and professional financial advice have been proposed as substitute
measures to improve retail investors' financial behavior. However, several studies document
that the extent to which advice is sought and followed is related to an investor's level of
financial literacy. Thus, we study the effect of a financial education intervention on the use of
financial advice. Specifically, we use a randomized control trial, in which participants can
choose to delegate all or parts of their experimental budget to a robo-advisory tool. This design
allows us to a) identify a potential benefit of financial education not considered so far, namely
increased delegation to an unbiased advisory tool, b) overcome endogeneity issues of financial
advice studies using measured financial literacy, and c) observe potential spillover effects to
individual investment behavior.

We use robo-advisors as a case for three reasons. First, compared to traditional financial
advisors, robo-advisors can target less wealthy and less financially literate investors, who would
benefit the most from an improvement in financial behavior. Second, they can provide
customized information at the point of financial decision-making, which is considered
important for the effectiveness of financial education (cf. Willis, 2011; Fernandes et al., 2014).
Third, there are calls for mandatory disclosure requirements regarding the applied algorithms,
which are conceptually similar to a financial education intervention. 3 Specifically, the aim is
to improve clients' understanding of the robo-advisors' decision-making process (VZBV, 2018;
SEC, 2017). Since decision-making in robo-advisors is mostly derived from modern portfolio
theory, increasing the transparency of the inner workings of the algorithms necessarily includes
communicating the underlying financial concepts.

While financial advisors rely on information asymmetry as part of their business model, several
arguments put forth in related literature point toward a positive impact of financial education
on the use of their services. Yaniv and Kleinberger (2000) offer a framework for advice taking
in which decision-makers weight conflicting opinions based on the subjective assessment of an

reasoning underlying their own opinion, which leads them to place more weight on their own

confidence and trust in the advisor's opinion, which has been linked to participation in risky
assets and pension plans (Guiso et al., 2008; Agnew et al., 2012). This might be particularly
relevant for robo-advisors, who rely on algorithms to make decisions that are crucial to their
service: Dietvorst et al. (2015) show that errors made by an algorithmic decision support system
are punished more gravely than errors made by a human advisor. Making investors familiar
with the investment decision process by opening the "black box" of algorithmic financial
decision-making could thus be of greater importance for robo-advisors than for human advisors.
Studies from the information systems domain support the notion of a positive effect of
explanation facilities on trust and technology adoption (Gregor and Benbasat, 1999; Wang and
Benbasat, 2007).4 In particular, the provision of transparency seems to increase the appreciation
of a robo-advisor (Rühr, 2020). Beyond that, robo-advisors predominantly invest in passively
managed index funds, stressing the importance of risk diversification and applying a cost-
cutting, low-turnover portfolio strategy. To convince clients of the superiority of this strategy
compared to less complex financial products such as a bank deposit or life insurance contract,
as well as more complex strategies involving technical and fundamental analyses, it could prove
beneficial for them to educate clients on the principles of modern portfolio theory.

3. Experimental design

We conducted an incentivized student experiment in a controlled laboratory environment. In
line with the results from a power test we conducted using the variance estimates from a pre-
test, we targeted 200 participants. The experiment is structured in the following way.
Participants first perform a real effort task that pays a performance-based budget. They are then
asked to participate in an incentivized four-period financial decision-making game. In each
period, participants allocate their previously earned budget to three distinct assets that evolve
probabilistically in value. The value of their portfolio after the last period determines their
payout. Participants can delegate decision-making to a robo-advisor for all or parts of their

General knowledge and literacy questionnaire

At the beginning of the experiment, participants are required to complete a real effort task
consisting of a set of 20 general knowledge questions from different domains. Participants
receive a payoff of 8 only if they answer at least ten questions correctly. 6 However,
participants correctly answering more than ten questions do not receive any additional payoffs.
This design prevents biases from both windfall endowments and wealth effects in the later
stages of the experiment (Keasey and Moon, 1996; Ackert et al., 2006). Among the 20 questions
making up the real effort task, six questions allow us to elicit the participants' knowledge of
financial (three questions) and technological concepts (three questions).7 Including the literacy
questions in the effort task allows us to elicit the ex-ante literacy levels of the participants while
avoiding a priming effect that would bias behavior in the later stages of the experiment. The
order of these questions is randomized to avoid order effects.8

Financial decision-making task and robo-advisory option

Participants that have completed the real effort task can increase their payoffs in repeated asset
allocation tasks inspired by a related study's design (cf. Kuhnen, 2015), in which participants
allocate their budget to three distinct assets. Asset A is a risk-free asset paying 5 percent with
certainty. Asset B is a stock of moderate risk, paying +21 percent or -7 percent with equal
probability. Asset C is a stock of high risk, paying +35 percent or -21 percent with equal
probability. The realization of the risky assets' payoffs is mutually independent. Thus, asset C
yields the same expected return as asset B at higher volatility, which might convey the
impression that asset C is inferior to asset B. However, investors can reduce the volatility of
their risky portfolios by combining assets B and C while keeping constant the expected rate of
return. As a result, the minimum-variance portfolio constructed from the two risky assets
dominates any other combination of the two risky assets for any risk-averse participant.9 The
robo-advisor identifies the minimum-variance portfolio of the two risky assets and combines it
with the risk-free asset A to accord with the individual risk preferences of a participant. Thus,

However, since the degree of risk aversion determines the utility-maximizing budget shares of
the risk-free asset and the risky minimum-variance portfolio, there is no globally optimal
portfolio. While related research on financial decision-making has relied on tasks that have
some form of optimal strategy (Kuhnen, 2015; Jung & Weinhardt, 2018), we are interested in
the tendency of participants to delegate financial decision-making to a robo-advisor that can a)
calculate the portfolio weights of the optimal risky portfolio, which prevents participants from
holding a dominated risky portfolio, b) elicit the participants' risk preferences through a
questionnaire and assign corresponding shares to the risk-free asset and the optimal risky
portfolio, and c) reestablish the original allocation in response to changes in the assets' values.
The robo-advisor unambiguously provides value in the first task since the exact shares are hard
to assess, even for financially literate individuals. The same does not hold for the second task
since participants might be better suited to assess their risk preferences than the robo-advisor
(cf. Tertilt & Scholz, 2018).

To make sure that using the robo-advisor is not a dominated option, we calibrated the maximum
fee participants would be willing to pay by computing the additional utility that the use of the
service yields in comparison to three counterfactual scenarios (worst possible investment
strategy, risk-free portfolio strategy, and 1/N diversification strategy) and for each of the four
risk profile groups. The results suggest that the maximum fee per period ranges between 0.4
percent and 12.1 percent of managed assets in the experiment10, which is a realistic reference
point considering that actual fees range from 0.4 percent to 1.2 percent in the German market
(Stiftung Warentest, 2018). 11 Our pre-test confirmed that a per-period fee of 0.5 percent
achieved sufficient variation in usage in the baseline case.

The financial decision-making task lasts four periods. After each period, participants observe
the realized payoffs and have the opportunity to change the composition of their self-managed
asset allocation. Before trading starts, participants have the option to allocate parts of their
budget to the robo-advisory algorithm at a per-period fee of 0.5 percent of the assets assigned
to the robo-advisor. The allocation to the robo-advisor cannot be reversed in the later periods.
The design of the robo-advisor is explained to the participants in the form of an animated video,
which enhances participants' comprehension. Besides, it avoids announcer effects and
simplifies the replication of the experiment by standardizing the intervention. While the

The robo-advisor used in this experiment mirrors the most frequently observed features of
current providers (Rühr et al., 2019). These features include eliciting risk preferences through
a brief risk questionnaire, implementing a corresponding portfolio, and ongoing rebalancing.
The risk questionnaire requires each participant to choose among a risk-free payoff and a risky
lottery three times. The risky lottery choice becomes more appealing with each choice, while
the risk-free option remains the same throughout the questionnaire. 12 Thus, the earlier a
participant switches from the risk-free option to the risky lottery, the higher that participant's
risk tolerance. The robo-advisor's decision-making process takes as inputs the answers to three
lottery choices each specifically designed to tease out whether a participant's risk aversion
coefficient is above or below a certain threshold. A utility function is fitted to these coefficients,
which allows assigning each participant (provided that the reported preferences are consistent)
one of four risk profiles spanning distinct ranges of risk aversion. Based on the assigned risk
profile, a suitable portfolio that reflects the risk preferences of the participants is implemented
and rebalanced by the robo-advisor.13 In addition to this quantitative elicitation measure, we
gather a qualitative estimate, which is commonly used in the field. 14

The explanations follow the three process steps of the robo-advisory tool (risk questionnaire,
portfolio allocation, and rebalancing). The risk questionnaire explanation illustrates that higher
returns are typically associated with higher risk and that an optimal portfolio is always subject
to an investor's risk preferences. Further, the mechanics of the robo-advisor's risk questionnaire
are explained using an example, in which the relation between the participant's answers and the
assigned risk profile is highlighted. The portfolio allocation explanation provides participants
with an intuitive introduction to modern portfolio theory, explaining the concept of
diversification and introducing the optimal market portfolio. Participants are demonstrated how
their risk preferences affect the combination of the market portfolio with the risk-free asset. The
rebalancing explanation demonstrates that the initial portfolio allocation changes after the asset
values are realized in each period. The robo-advisor realigns the portfolio with a participant's
risk preferences by establishing the initial allocation.

The volume and complexity of information provided to the treatment group have been discussed
as a potential threat to the identification of the treatment effect (Bergstrom and Stoll, 1990). To
avoid differences in cognitive exhaustion, we show the control group an unrelated video of
similar length and style in addition to the (shorter) instruction video. The multimedia
presentation format of the instructions should decrease the degree of attention needed to process
a given amount of information. After each video, participants answer a set of comprehension
questions. In the treatment group, the aim is to a) test the effectiveness of the education
treatment, b) stimulate attention, and c) obtain a control variable signaling the degree of
comprehension in the treatment dimensions, i.e., the process steps of the robo-advisor. The
control group receives the same amount of questions of a similar nature, which ensures

Control variables

We collect several control variables in addition to standard demographic variables (such as
gender and age) to capture individual characteristics that might affect the delegation decision.
Firstly, overconfidence is associated with costly investment behavior (Barber and Odean,
2000), as well as a lower likelihood of seeking financial advice (Guiso and Jappelli, 2006;
Kramer, 2016) and delegating financial decisions (Calcagno and Monticone, 2015). Thus, we
construct an overconfidence measure by asking participants how many questions they believe
they answered correctly in the effort task and subtracting from that the number of questions
they in fact answered correctly. Secondly, more risk-averse individuals tend to be more likely
to seek advice (Bluethgen et al., 2008). We elicit a participant's risk preferences using three
distinct measures. The first measure involves a lottery choice task. The second measure is based
on self-assessment on an established scale (cf. Dohmen et al., 2011). The third measure is based
on participants' behavior in the financial decision-making task. Specifically, we use the excess
portfolio share allocated to the risk-free asset A in the self-managed portfolio portion to obtain
a revealed measure of risk aversion.15 Since financially illiterate individuals stand to gain most
from the delegation of portfolio decisions (Bianchi, 2018; Agarwal et al., 2017), we construct
a financial literacy measure from the three questions testing financial knowledge in the effort
task (cf. van Rooij et al., 2011), as well as a measure based on self-assessment. Aside from
financial literacy, investors of robo-advisors might need a sufficient technological
understanding of the process of algorithmic decision-making. Research has shown that the use
of intelligent systems positively affects the use of a different yet similar system (Venkatesh and
Davis, 1996; McFarland and Hamilton, 2006). Thus, we construct a technological literacy
measure from the three questions testing technological knowledge in the effort task, as well as
a measure based on self-assessment. We further inquire about the attitude toward new
technologies (technology readiness, c.f. Parasuraman, 2000).

4. Data

Prior to conducting the experiment, we extensively pre-tested the design with 16 individuals of
different backgrounds. After the design was finalized, we pre-registered the experiment on the

Risk preferences

We gather information regarding the participants' risk preferences in the form of a risk
questionnaire designed to resemble risk questionnaires applied in the field. In addition to this
quantitative measure of risk aversion, we asked participants to provide an assessment of their
risk preferences using an established scale (cf. Dohmen et al., 2011). Exploiting our
experimental design, we compute a third measure that ­ as opposed to the two stated measures
- captures a subject's revealed risk preferences. We use the excess share of the self-managed
budget portion allocated to the risk-free asset A in the first period of the financial decision-
making game as a measure of risk aversion. According to modern portfolio theory, an investor's
risk preferences should govern the share of wealth invested in the risk-free asset. Thus, using
the share invested in the risk-free asset in the first period (=, 1) allows us to infer a subject's
level of risk aversion.18 We subtract from that measure the optimal share of the risk-free asset

Portfolio allocation
Portfolio diversification is a central feature of modern portfolio theory. The idea of combining
non-perfectly correlated risky assets to decrease asset-specific risk is essential to maximize risk-
adjusted portfolio returns. In our experiment, we designed the two risky assets such that both
have the same expected return, while one risky asset has a lower standard deviation than the
other risky asset. This construction might convey the impression that the latter is inferior to the
former. However, since the realizations of the two assets' returns are independent, any
combination of the two risky assets is superior to both assets in terms of its risk-adjusted return.
Consequently, we would expect participants that have understood the principles of
diversification to invest in both risky assets instead of either one. Thus, we construct our
measure of diversification as the average share of periods in which the participants diversified
their risky portfolio, i.e., periods in which they invested in both risky assets. Figure 4 illustrates
the diversification variable for the control and treatment group.
The results of parametric and non-parametric tests indicate that, on average, participants of the
treatment group diversify more often than participants of the control group. This behavior
indicates that the portfolio allocation part of the treatment is effective since participants make
decisions that are in line with modern portfolio theory. Thus, educating participants about
portfolio diversification indeed improved their financial decision-making ability. However, this
raises the question as to whether educated investors should delegate investment to a robo-
advisory tool at all.

Rebalancing

Rebalancing refers to the re-establishment of the initial asset allocation in response to changes
in the portfolio weights. Given that the initial allocation adequately reflected a participant's risk
preferences and assuming stable preferences, rebalancing ensures that the participants'
preferences and the risk profile of their respective portfolios remain aligned. In the experiment,
the robo-advisory tool takes care of rebalancing in the delegated budget share, while
participants are in charge of the portfolio weights in the self-managed part of their budgets.
Since the treatment video explains the underlying reasoning behind the concept of rebalancing,
we would expect participants in the treatment group to engage more actively in rebalancing
than participants of the control group.

6. Discussion
We study financial decision-making in a randomized control trial, contributing to existing
literature on the effectiveness of financial education by allowing participants to delegate
financial decision-making. Specifically, we conduct our study in the context of robo-advisors
that might be in a good position to effectively educate investors due to their broad investor base

19 See Online-Appendix A.8 for an illustration of the distribution of the rebalancing measure.

                                                            25
         Financial Education and Digital Asset Management: What's in the Black Box?

and digital service provision, which allows for just-in-time interventions (cf. Fernandes et al.,
2014). At the same time, it might be more relevant for robo-advisors than for traditional
advisors to communicate their underlying investment logic to oppose algorithm aversion due to
the perception of a `black box' decision-making process (Dietvorst et al., 2015).

Our results show that providing detailed information regarding the inner workings of a robo-
advisory algorithm increases participants' tendency to delegate financial decision-making. The
effects in the laboratory are substantial and stable across several specifications. Aside from the
main treatment effect, we identify two insightful effects on the behavior of the treated
participants. First, we observe conflicting alterations in different risk aversion measures.
Second, treated participants display improved portfolio diversification, which does not displace
delegation.

Delegation

We find that the financial education intervention increases delegation to the robo-advisor. Our
interpretation of this finding is twofold: First, the intervention may have affected how
participants perceive the investment decision. Specifically, educating investors on the
considerations involved in the investment decision can have one of two consequences. On the
one hand, it may increase investors' understanding of the decision principles, which increases
their confidence to make the decision themselves. On the other hand, it may highlight the
complexity of the decision and the improvement in the financial outcome that a viable
investment strategy can provide. Given that we observe a greater extent of delegation among
participants that received the financial education intervention, the latter seems more plausible.
Second, the intervention may have affected how participants perceive the robo-advisor's
service. Specifically, the intervention may have increased trust in the service as it increased the
investor's understanding of the algorithm's reasoning process, which seems to be particularly
relevant for algorithmic decision-support systems such as robo-advisors (cf. Dietvorst et al.,
2015). Trust may be higher because the treatment was effective at educating participants or
because it provided more transparency regarding the service (Rühr, 2020). The design of our
treatment, as well as the fact that treated participants were better at diversifying their portfolios,
suggests that the effect was not only driven by increased transparency.

                                                            26
                                           Litterscheidt & Streich

Risk aversion

Concerning the risk aversion measures, we identify two opposing effects. On the one hand, we
observe lower levels of the self-reported risk aversion measure for treated participants, even
when controlling for the risk aversion measure elicited through the risk questionnaire. On
average, participants overestimate their level of risk aversion: the median participant's answers
to the risk questionnaire were consistent with the riskiest investor profile, while the median
participant stated to be moderately risk-averse, i.e., five on a scale from zero to ten. Thus, the
treatment seems to have enhanced the participants' understanding of their risk preferences,
which is consistent with an anchoring effect stemming from the risk profile explanation.

On the other hand, we observe higher levels of excess risk aversion among treated participants.
The difference seems to be driven by treated participants who at least partially delegate
decision-making to the robo-advisor. Specifically, we find significantly higher excess risk
aversion among delegating participants in the treatment group, but not in the control group. We
identify two potential interpretations of the observed patterns. First, participants might answer
the risk questionnaire strategically to save fees: Participants incur fees in proportion to the total
amount delegated to the robo-advisor. Our treatment aims to increase participants'
understanding of how their answers to the risk questionnaire translate into the portfolio
implemented by the robo-advisor. Thus, treated participants, who obtain more information
about the relationship between their risk profiles and the robo-advisor's portfolio allocation,
may only want to use the robo-advisor to manage the risky part of their portfolio (optimal
combination of risky assets B and C) and determine the risk-free share of their portfolio (share
of risk-free asset A) themselves. This could be achieved by understating risk aversion in the
questionnaire and delegating only the risky share of their budget to the robo-advisor. This would
be associated with a higher risky share in the delegated portfolio and, correspondingly, a higher
risk-free share in the self-managed portfolio, which could explain the observed differences in
excess risk aversion. To test this interpretation, we compare the risk profiles derived from the
questionnaire between the control and treatment group, as well as between delegating and non-
delegating participants within the treatment group. Since the measure does not differ
significantly for either comparison, we discard this interpretation.

Second, participants might strategically increase the risk-free share in the self-managed
portfolio to counteract the risk of the portfolio implemented by the robo-advisor, which they
may perceive to be excessive. We observe a marked difference between participants' self-

                                                            27
         Financial Education and Digital Asset Management: What's in the Black Box?

reported risk aversion and the risk profiles assigned to them. Participants in the treatment group
might be made aware of this deviation, which would cause them to offset the risk in the
delegated portfolio by taking less risk in the self-managed portfolio. Two assumptions need to
hold for this interpretation to be plausible: First, the treatment improves the awareness of the
gap in self-reported and assigned risk aversion. The fact that we observe lower levels of self-
reported risk aversion for treated participants (which aligns self-reported and assigned risk
aversion) provides some support for this assumption. Second, participants react to the perceived
deviation by strategically adjusting the risk-free share in the self-managed portfolio. The fact
that excess risk aversion is positively associated with delegation to the robo-advisor in the
treatment group provides some support for that assumption.

Diversification

Two findings emerge from analyzing the impact of the portfolio allocation part of the treatment.
On the one hand, we find that treated participants more frequently diversify their risky
portfolios, which suggests that the treatment enhances the understanding of the principles of
portfolio diversification. On the other hand, this improvement does not contest the use of the
robo-advisor. Instead, participants with higher diversification abilities were more inclined to
delegate to the robo-advisor. This finding is at odds with the results of previous studies, which
show that financially literate individuals are less likely to delegate financial decisions (Calcagno
and Monticone, 2015) and less likely to follow financial advice once it is provided (Stolper,
2018; Hackethal et al., 2010). According to this logic, efficient portfolio diversification can be
achieved either through financial literacy or financial advice (Gaudecker, 2015). However, our
findings suggest that financial education enhances diversification while increasing advice
taking. As a result, financial education might increase the probability of achieving favorable
investment outcomes, both directly (through enhanced diversification ability) and indirectly
(through increased delegation, assuming that delegation improves investment outcomes). Thus,
our study makes a more optimistic case for the effect of financial education interventions on
financial outcomes, at least in the short run, than previous studies (cf. Fernandes et al., 2014).

Building on the findings documented in this study, several interesting extensions are worth
pointing out. First, we cannot clearly distinguish whether the financial education intervention
increased delegation because of the improvement in financial knowledge or because of
increased transparency regarding the decision-making process. To test whether transparency
per se affects the delegation decision, our design could be extended to investigate whether the

treatment effect differs when participants can delegate decision-making to a human asset
manager. As transparency might be more important for an algorithmic than for a human
decision-maker (Dietvorst et al., 2015), a stronger increase in delegation to the robo-advisor
would indicate that transparency per se affects the delegation decision.
Second, the spillover effects of the financial education intervention cannot be disentangled from
the delegation decision in our framework since participants simultaneously optimize over the
self-managed and delegated portfolios. Thus, the effect of the intervention on investment
behavior could be studied with and without the availability of a robo-advisory tool. This design
could also be used to test whether the treatment primarily affected the participants' perception
of the decision-making task or the robo-advisor's service.

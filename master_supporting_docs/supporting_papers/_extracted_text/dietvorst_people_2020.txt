Abstract
Will people use self-driving cars, virtual doctors, and other algorithmic decision-makers if they outperform humans?
The answer depends on the uncertainty inherent in the decision domain. We propose that people have diminishing
sensitivity to forecasting error and that this preference results in people favoring riskier (and often worse-performing)
decision-making methods, such as human judgment, in inherently uncertain domains. In nine studies (N = 4,820), we
found that (a) people have diminishing sensitivity to each marginal unit of error that a forecast produces, (b) people
are less likely to use the best possible algorithm in decision domains that are more unpredictable, (c) people choose
between decision-making methods on the basis of the perceived likelihood of those methods producing a near-perfect
answer, and (d) people prefer methods that exhibit higher variance in performance (all else being equal). To the extent
that investing, medical decision-making, and other domains are inherently uncertain, people may be unwilling to use
even the best possible algorithm in those domains.

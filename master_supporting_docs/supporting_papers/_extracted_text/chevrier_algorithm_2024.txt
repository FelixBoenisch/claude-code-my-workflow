Algorithm Delegation and Responsibility:
   Shifting Blame to the Programmer ?

Mathieu Chevrier  Vincent Teixeira

GREDEG Working Paper No. 2024-04

                                                Abstract
    In a lab experiment, we investigate whether individuals delegate allocation deci-
sions to an intermediary in order to shift blame. The intermediary can be a human,
a rule-based algorithm (RA), or an artificial intelligence algorithm (AI), depending
on the treatment condition. Behind these algorithms, a programmer fully controls
the decisions of the RA and partially controls the decisions of the AI. We find that
participants delegate regardless of the type of intermediary (human, RA, or AI).
While human intermediaries and RA programmers are perceived as more respon-
sible for inegalitarian decisions, the delegator is perceived as less responsible. AI
programmers and the delegator are not perceived as responsible for the inegalitar-
ian allocations made by the AI. This allows AI programmers to exploit the moral
wiggle room and select inegalitarian allocations more frequently.

    JEL Codes: C92, D63

    Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Delegation, Responsibility, Moral Wiggle Room,
Punishment, Laboratory Experiment

1 Introduction

In an era where algorithms shape everything from our social media feeds to the finan-
cial markets, understanding our interactions with these digital decision-makers is more
important than ever. As algorithms evolve to become our advisors, employees and even
our managers, the lines between human judgement and machine calculation are blurring,
raising profound questions about trust, responsibility, and ethics in our increasingly auto-
mated world. Organizations are being transformed to welcome these algorithms (Shrestha
et al., 2019; Langer and Landers, 2021; Agrawal et al., 2024). At the same time, a growing
number of studies are discussing the pros and cons of these autonomous algorithms in
different tasks and domains such as autonomous vehicles (Bonnefon et al., 2016; Shariff
et al., 2017; Awad et al., 2020).1 What all algorithms have in common is that there is
always a principal (also referred to as "the user") behind them who chooses to delegate
her decision to the algorithm. This algorithm is linked to "a programmer" who manipu-
lates and develops it.

A principal may choose to delegate a decision for several reasons, including the expertise
of the agent, the inability to manage everything, to save time, or simply because she does
not want to do it herself. Bartling and Fischbacher (2012) also show that a principal can
delegate to a human intermediary to avoid being blamed. Here, our first research ques-
tion is: "Do users try to shift the blame to the programmer behind the algorithm?". The
issue is that an algorithm itself has no personal stake in monetary losses, cannot be sent
to prison, and cannot regret its actions. Thus, it cannot be held responsible for its deci-
sions (Martin, 2019; Coeckelbergh, 2020). Therefore, either the user, the programmer, or
both, could be held responsible instead of the algorithm. In light of this uncertainty, we
must ask: "Who is perceived as responsible for an inegalitarian algorithmic decision: the
programmer or the user?". Delegating the decision is not advisable if the user believes
that the programmer will not be perceived as responsible.

It is important to note that programmers can have different levels of influence on the
outcome of an algorithm. As a result, they should not be perceived with the same degree
of responsibility. A Rule-Based Algorithm (RA) is fundamentally different from an Arti-
ficial Intelligence Algorithm (AI). The RA follows logical rules created by a programmer,
usually based on expert decisions. For example, MYCIN is one of the earliest rule-based
expert algorithms used in medicine. Created in the 1970s, it consists of nearly 600 logical
rules that allow it to make diagnoses and predict which drugs to prescribe.2 Unlike RA,
AIs make decisions based on a database and can work autonomously. A well known ex-
ample is ChatGPT, a chat-bot developed by OpenAI. This algorithm makes predictions
based on a database containing information from the internet up to a certain date.3
The processes behind AI and RA differ significantly. Unlike RA systems, where the pro-
grammer maintains complete control over the algorithmic decisions, AI operates with
significantly greater autonomy. For example, in January 2023, the creator of Game of
Thrones sued OpenAI for copyright infringement after ChatGPT reproduced sections of
his work, despite OpenAI's policies against violating copyright laws (Gerken and McMa-
hon, 2023). Similarly, Microsoft's Twitter chat-bot Tay had to be shut down just eight
hours after its launch in the U.S. due to it adopting offensive behaviors influenced by
user interactions, a problem not seen in China, likely due to stricter social media cen-
sorship (Goolsbee, 2018). In both cases, if the algorithms had been RA rather than AI,
the programmers - and their organizations - would have had more control over the out-
comes, potentially avoiding these problems. By generating its own rules, AI reduces the
programmer's control and makes decisions less predictable.

The unpredictability of AI outcomes makes it difficult to determine whether the algo-
rithm is following the programmer's intentions or is acting independently. This ambiguity
inherent in AI provides programmers with the opportunity to exploit moral wiggle room
(Dana et al., 2007). By making an inegalitarian decision, the AI programmer increases
the likelihood that an inegalitarian allocation is selected by the AI. This brings us to our

last research question: "Does an AI programmer exploit the moral wiggle room afforded
by an AI?"

To answer these questions, we use the experimental design outlined by Bartling and
Fischbacher (2012). This design proposes a simple measure of perceived responsibility
based on monetary punishment. In this one-shot economic game, a group of four par-
ticipants, designated as A (the "dictator"), B (the "intermediary"), and C and D (the
"recipients"), participate in the allocation of a given endowment. The participant A faces
a tripartite choice: an egalitarian distribution, an inegalitarian distribution, or delegating
this decision to an intermediary B. If A delegates, B must choose between the two distri-
butions proposed to A (egalitarian or inegalitarian). After that the allocation is made,
two recipients may choose to administer punishment depending on the choice made by A
and B. This can be done by sacrificing a portion of their respective payoffs. This exper-
imental design allows us to observe the perception of responsibility through monetary
punishment. We introduce two new treatments, in which A can delegate decision-making
to an algorithm under the control of B. In these treatments, participant A is referred to
as "the dictator" or "the user", who can delegate the decision to the algorithm. Participant
B is referred to as "the intermediary" or "the programmer" who developed the algorithm.
In the "RA" treatment, B has full control over the algorithm. In the "AI" treatment, the
programmer's control over the algorithm's decisions is reduced to 50%, meaning that
there is a 50% chance that the decision of participant B will be applied by the algorithm.
In the other 50%, the algorithm makes the decision autonomously, based on its database.

This research primarily contributes to the literature on algorithm delegation, perceived
responsibility, and moral wiggle room.

To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to consider the role of the algo-
rithm programmer into an economic game. This paves the way for new research that will
examine the role of the programmer in depth. (i) We show that individuals are equally
likely to delegate an allocation decision to a human or an algorithm (AI, RA) when
there is a programmer linked to the algorithm. (ii) We validate the theory developed
by Matthias (2004) highlighting that the AI programmer is perceived as less respon-

                                                        3
sible than the RA programmer when the algorithm makes an inegalitarian allocation
decision. (iii) Recipients are less likely to pay e1 to punish other participants when the
intermediary is an AI. However, recipients who decide to pay e1 punish AI users more
severely than RA users. This opposite effect leads AI users to receive a similar average
punishment and payoff as those who delegate to a human or an RA. Thus, regardless of
the nature of the intermediary, delegators still have an incentive to delegate in order to
be perceived as less responsible. (iv) AI programmer exploits the moral wiggle room and
therefore makes more inegalitarian choices.

We proceed as follows: In Section 2, we review the literature, then introduce our be-
havioral conjectures. In Section 3, we explain the framework of our experimental design
and process. The results are presented in Section 4, and we conclude and discuss them
in Section 5.

2 Related literature and conjectures

2.1 Related literature

To investigate whether participants decide to delegate an allocation decision to an RA
or an AI, and to provide evidence of differences between these algorithms and a human
intermediary, we review the relevant literature on algorithmic delegation, perceived re-
sponsibility, and moral wiggle room. We use this literature review and logical reasoning
to formulate behavioral conjectures in the second part.

2.1.1 Algorithmic appreciation
Although algorithms often outperform humans in many tasks, people tend to prefer to
delegate tasks to humans rather than algorithms (Meehl, 1954; Dawes, 2008; Sanders
and Manrodt, 2003; Fildes and Goodwin, 2007; Dietvorst et al., 2015; Jung and Seiter,
2021; Dargnies et al., 2024). However, a preference for algorithmic delegation may emerge
depending on whether the algorithm acts as an advisor (Logg et al., 2019; You et al., 2022;
Daschner and Obermaier, 2022), the objectivity of the task (Castelo et al., 2019; Hertz
and Wiese, 2019) and the perceived complexity of the algorithm (Lehmann et al., 2022;
Chevrier et al., 2024). These findings underscore the growing importance of algorithms

                                                        4
in a variety of areas in economics.4 However, research on algorithmic responsibility and
delegation remains limited. Gazit et al. (2023) found that humans are perceived as more
responsible than algorithms in financial and medical advice, preferring human advisors
because other humans can be held accountable. Kirchkamp and Strobel (2019) observed
that humans and algorithms can be perceived as equally responsible in joint decisions
leading to unequal outcomes.

2.1.2 Switching the blame to an algorithm

Prior literature suggests that principals strategically delegate decisions to agents to re-
duce their perceived responsibility, a phenomenon referred to as strategic delegation
(Sengul et al., 2012). This allows principals to maximize payoffs (Fershtman and Gneezy,
2001; Sutan and Vranceanu, 2016), maintain their social image (Bénabou and Tirole,
2005; Hamman et al., 2010), or shift the blame (Coffman, 2011; Bartling and Fischbacher,
2012; Oexl and Grossman, 2013; Argenton et al., 2023). These practices can be traced
back to Machiavellian principles, where unpopular measures are delegated to others while
leaders retain the means of distributing favors (Gawn and Innes, 2021).

In this context, this study examines whether individuals also delegate tasks to algo-
rithms in order to reduce their perceived responsibility. Feier et al. (2022) conducted
an experiment in which principals could delegate tasks to either a human or an artifi-
cial agent. The results show that participants are indifferent between delegating to both
agents. However, when mistakes occur, principals who delegate to human agents are
perceived as more responsible than those who delegate to artificial agents. Awad et al.
(2020) found that users of autonomous vehicles are held responsible for accidents, rather
than the companies that produce the faulty algorithms. This finding contrasts with the
results of Feier et al. (2022), possibly due to the framing of the algorithms. Indeed, Hou
and Jung (2021) showed that when algorithms were framed as "AI", participants tended
to value them more, whereas framing them as following logical rules increased algorithm
aversion. This discrepancy in framing may explain the differences between studies.
We believe that individual perceive algorithms - RA and AI - differently, depending
on how much control the programmer has. This is why we compare these two types of

2.1.3 Perceived responsibility: RA vs. AI

Matthias (2004) argues that it is not always possible to establish a direct link between
the programmer's decision and an algorithm's output, especially in AI. Programmers
can choose AI models and databases, but their influence is limited. If an RA makes
an inequitable decision, it is a direct result of the programmer's conscious choices. In
contrast, when an AI makes an inegalitarian decision, the outcome may be the result
of either the programmer's input or biases present in the underlying database (Zhang
et al., 2020). AI databases, often reflecting societal biases, have produced sexist and
racist outcomes (Rahwan et al., 2019; Cowgill and Tucker, 2019; Kassam and Marino,
2022), and individuals can manipulate AI to their advantage (Klockmann et al., 2022;
Cao et al., 2023).
This ambiguity, often referred to as the "responsibility gap" (Matthias, 2004), makes
it difficult to assign responsibility for decisions made by AI. Because RA programmers
retain more control over the decisions of their algorithms, the causal link between the
programmer's actions and outcomes is stronger than in AI, where the algorithm can
make autonomous decisions beyond the programmer's direct input (May, 1992; Fischer,
1999; Lipinski et al., 2002).
Previous research suggests that AI programmers may exploit ambiguity surrounding AI
outcomes to manipulate AI to produce more inegalitarian decision.

2.1.4 AI and moral wiggle room

Moral wiggle room refers to the phenomenon where individuals exploit ambiguity or
uncertainty to justify self-serving decisions. By using ambiguity, individuals can make
choices under the guise of uncertainty, often leading to more inegalitarian decisions. For
example, Dana et al. (2007) has shown that in a dictator game, dictators were more likely
to choose an inegalitarian distribution when the recipient was unaware of the dictator's
decision. Larson and Capra (2009), Feiler (2014) and Grossman and Van Der Weele
(2017) conducted control checks and expanded Dana et al. (2007)'s design, showing that
participants preferred to remain ignorant about their actions' effects on other partici-
pants' payoffs. Moral wiggle room allows participants to avoid information about the

                                                        6
consequences of their actions. All of these studies show that moral wiggle room enables
participants to make more self-serving decisions. Based on this logic, Garcia et al. (2020)
showed that participants act strategically by avoiding information about others' payoffs
as an excuse to be less generous in charitable giving without feeling guilty.

As we have seen, exploiting moral wiggle room is well known in the literature. To the
best of our knowledge, no paper has yet demonstrated that, due to the ambiguous out-
comes delivered by AI, participants may also exploit moral wiggle room to make more
inegalitarian allocation decisions.

2.2 Behavioral conjectures

Our behavioral conjectures are based on the idea that participants can delegate an al-
location decision to an intermediary. The intermediary can be a human, an RA, or an
AI. Recipients can also decide to punish another participant by sacrificing a portion of
their respective payoff to reduce another participant's payoff. We thus distinguish two
variables: the likelihood of being punished, which is reflected in the recipient's decision
to sacrifice their payoff. And the severity of the punishment, which is captured by the
amount of euros deducted from other participants (the design is described in detail in
the next section).

2.2.1 Delegation

In this game, if all participants are rational and want to maximize their payoff, the dicta-
tor will choose the inegalitarian decision directly, and no recipient will choose to sacrifice
their payoff to punish. However, several studies have shown that the empirical results
do not match the theory (Fehr and Schmidt, 2006). Thus, the dictator may anticipate a
possible punishment. Strategically, if the dictator assumes that the intermediary is driven
by the objective of maximizing profit and the recipient has a preference for fairness and
will punish, the dictator should delegate to the intermediary to let him/her be punished
instead. For the "switching the blame" strategy to be effective, the intermediary must be
perceived as responsible (Bartling and Fischbacher, 2012; Argenton et al., 2023). Previ-
ous studies, similar to our baseline, have already shown that the "switching the blame"
strategy is used. In our RA treatment, participants are well aware that the programmer

                                                        7
completely controls the decisions made by the algorithm. The only difference between
the Human and the RA treatment is the framing. In the RA treatment, we say that the
"algorithm has selected" instead of "another participant has selected" a certain allocation.
However, it is always the programmer who makes all the algorithm's decisions and can
be held accountable. Participants know this, so we expect no difference.5 Regarding the
AI treatment, we assume that the AI programmer will be perceived as less responsible
because the algorithm can make decisions autonomously. The question is whether the
dictator will anticipate this. In Bartling and Fischbacher (2012), a similar experiment
was conducted with delegation to a die. Behind the die, a participant assumed responsi-
bility for the random draw. There was no difference in the number of delegations between
delegating to another human and delegating to the die. Feier et al. (2022) found no signif-
icant difference in the tendency to delegate tasks between humans and algorithms. This
suggests that individuals who delegate tasks do not anticipate that algorithms cannot
assume responsibility in the same way as humans. They assume that participants cannot
anticipate that the artificial agent will be less punished.6 Based on these two papers, we
assume that the dictator will not anticipate that the AI intermediary will be less blamed
and that the blame will fall on them.

Conjecture 1: Participants' delegation decisions are not influenced by the type of in-
termediary (human, RA, or AI).

2.2.2 Perceived responsibility

May (1992) and Lipinski et al. (2002) showed that for an individual to be judged respon-
sible, it is necessary for the judge to see the causal link between an individual decision
and the resulting outcome. The stronger the causal link between making the decision and
the outcome, the more participants can judge the decision maker as being responsible
for their actions. If there is a delegation to an intermediary, such as another participant
or an RA, the direct link between the outcome and the decision-maker is quite easy
to draw. The recipients should perceive the intermediary as responsible (Bartling and
Fischbacher, 2012; Oexl and Grossman, 2013). In the case of AI, due to the ambiguity

about who makes the choice (the AI programmer or the AI), the direct link between the
AI programmer and the AI decision is less strong. This main difference between RA and
AI is the key factor to understand our following conjectures.

Bartling and Fischbacher (2012) used a punishment pattern benchmark to determine
who is perceived as responsible for the outcome. The harsher a participant is punished,
the more responsible they are perceived. To administer punishment, the recipient must
pay to be allowed to reduce another participant's payoff. Recipients who have a prefer-
ence for fairness and are willing to pay to punish others should be sure about who is truly
responsible for the decision. The risk of punishing an innocent participant emerges in
the AI condition. Therefore, the willingness of the recipients to punish should diminish
due to this ambiguity. Recipients should thus less frequently pay to punish others in the
AI treatment because they have difficulty determining who should be held responsible
in this treatment condition.Due to the lack of control that AI programmers have over
the AI decision, our conjecture 2.1 highlights that AI programmers should be perceived
as less responsible than RA programmers (Matthias, 2004).

Conjecture 2: It is less likely that recipients will decide to punish other participants
when an inegalitarian allocation is selected by an AI.

AI programmers have much less control over the algorithm's decision-making, which re-
sults in them being perceived as less responsible than RA programmers who directly
determine the algorithm's decisions. The direct link between the decision made by an
RA and its programmer is stronger than the direct link between the decision made by an
AI and its programmer. The AI makes an autonomous decision without considering the
programmer in 50% of cases. Therefore, we assume that participants will perceive an RA
programmer as more responsible for the decision-making by the algorithm than an AI
programmer, who cannot completely control it. According to Bartling and Fischbacher
(2012), being perceived as more responsible leads to harsher punishment. Therefore, RA
programmer punishment should be harsher than AI programmer punishment.

Those who are willing to pay should perceive the AI user as more responsible, given that
the AI user has fully decided to delegate to the AI rather than making an egalitarian
decision. Conjecture 2.2 aligns with findings from Awad et al. (2020), who demonstrated

                                                        9
that people perceive autonomous vehicle users as more responsible than the firm that
produces them. Bartling and Fischbacher (2012) demonstrated that when an individual
delegates a decision to a dice roll, she is perceived as significantly more responsible for
the outcome than the mechanism itself. Similarly, Oexl and Grossman (2013) conducted
an experiment in which the intermediary is permitted to select only an inegalitarian
allocation. When the dictator delegates to an intermediary who is compelled to make
an inegalitarian choice, the recipient perceives the dictator who delegates to the inter-
mediary as more responsible. In a firing context, Corgnet (2023) showed that a manager
is perceived more negatively by the dismissed worker when the manager delegates the
decision to an algorithm than in the absence of delegation. These papers highlight that
when intermediaries are limited in their decision-making abilities or lack control over
the decision, the individual delegating the decision is perceived as responsible for the
outcome. Thus, we assume that the AI user will be punished more harshly than the RA
user.
Conjecture 2.1: It is more likely that an RA programmer will be punished more severely
for an inegalitarian decision made by the algorithm than an AI programmer.

Conjecture 2.2: It is more likely that an AI users will be punished more severely for
an inegalitarian decision made by the algorithm than an RA users.

According to our conjectures 2 and 2.1, the AI programmer will be less blamed and
punished less severely than the RA programmer. Therefore, the expected utility of an AI
programmer should be higher than that of an RA programmer if the algorithm selects
the inegalitarian allocation. Concerning the AI user, there is a confounding factor due to
conjectures 2, and 2.2. The AI user will be punished less often, but the punishment will
be more severe when it occurs. Depending on which effect is stronger, the AI user may
receive a monetary payment equal to or different from that of the RA user. We cannot
predict which of these opposing effects will be stronger. Therefore we cannot determine
whether delegating to an AI can really protect the user from punishment.

2.3 AI, moral wiggle room and welfare

Finally, conjecture 2.1 suggests that AI programmers are less likely to be punished when
the AI algorithm selects the inegalitarian allocation. Conjecture 2 indicates that par-

                                                        10
ticipants from AI treatment are less frequently subjected to punishment. Thus, it is
possible for AI programmers to exploit the moral wiggle room left by the AI's decision.
For example, Dana et al. (2007) used a dictator game with two choices, egalitarian and
inegalitarian. They showed that when the choice is certain, 26% of the dictators choose
the inequitable choice. But when the choice is ambiguous, allowing the use of moral
wiggle room, 63% of them choose the inequitable option. Similarly, it is possible that AI
programmers use the moral wiggle room to select the inegalitarian allocation because
they assume that they will be perceived as less responsible, and thus be punished less
harshly.

Conjecture 3: AI programmers are more likely to select the inegalitarian allocation
compared to the human intermediary and the RA programmer.

3 Design

Based on the literature, we designed an experiment that allows us to measure the per-
ceived responsibility for an inegalitarian decision made by different types of intermedi-
aries. It also provides information about the decisions made by the participants, allowing
us to answer our research questions.

3.1 Human intermediary

The baseline of this study is a derivative of the designs of Bartling and Fischbacher
(2012). This game involves the formation of a group of four participants, designated as
A (the "dictator"), B (the "intermediary"), and C and D (the "recipients"), engaged in
the division of a e20 endowment. The baseline is referred to as the Human treatment.

Participant A faces a choice between two different allocations: an egalitarian allocation,
where each participant receives e5, and an inegalitarian allocation, where participants
A and B receive e9 and participants C and D e1. A key aspect of our design is that
participant A can delegate the decision-making to the intermediary, B, who then faces
the same allocation choices. Regardless of participant A's decision, participant B has
to choose between an egalitarian and an inegalitarian distribution.7 Meanwhile, partic-

ipants C and D express their decisions in all possible situations. This step allows us
to elicit 4 distinct situations depending on the choices made by A and B, with respect

punish other participants by spending e1 of their own allocation. They can then reduce

3.2 RA and AI programmer

The baseline is extended through two new treatments that redefine the role of participant
B as a programmer exerting control over a decision-making algorithm (see Figure 1). In
the RA treatment, the RA programmer has full control over the algorithm, allowing par-
ticipant A - the RA user - to either directly select an allocation or delegate the decision
to the RA, fully controlled by participant B - the RA programmer. It should be noted
that the sole distinction between the Human and the RA treatment is the framing of the
algorithm. The AI treatment introduces a variation wherein the AI algorithm applies the
AI programmer's decision in 50% of the cases. In this treatment, participant B - the AI
programmer - controls the AI algorithm's decision in 50% of the cases. When participant
A - the AI user - delegates to an algorithm, there is a 50% chance that the AI algorithm
will apply the choice made by the AI programmer. In the remaining 50% of cases, the
AI algorithm chooses autonomously. The AI algorithm is presented to participants as
an algorithm that makes decisions based on previous human decision (see Appendix A).
To achieve this, we calibrate our AI algorithm by conducting a pre-study.10 Participants
are not informed of the distribution used by the AI algorithm to make its decision. Par-

applied by the algorithm. The "AI" programmer has a 50% chance that the algorithm applies his decision
and a 50% chance that the AI algorithm makes the decision itself. If the AI algorithm makes the decision
itself, there is a 48% chance that it selects the inegalitarian decision and a 52% chance that it selects
the egalitarian decision.

4.5 Extended Results: Risk Attitude

These results were not expected in our pre-registration and were not initially included
in our hypothesis.

Regressions (5) and (6) in Table 4 show that subjects who are more willing to take
risks in their daily lives are more likely to choose the inegalitarian option. Indeed, the
variable Risk is equal to 0 for a risk-averse subject and 10 for a risk-lover subject.20
This finding is consistent with our design, as a risk-averse agent would fear punishment
more than a risk-taking agent. Indeed, by choosing the inegalitarian distribution, they
maximize their payoff before the punishment. But they also accept an increased risk of
being punished by players C and D. By choosing the egalitarian outcome, they maximize
the payoff of C and D, which should significantly reduce C and D's willingness to punish.
But at the same time, they accept a reduction in their pre-punishment payoff from 9 to
5. This trivially explains the significance of risk attitudes in our regressions.

5 Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, we use the original experimental design created by Bartling and Fis-
chbacher (2012) to address three research questions. (1) "Do users try to shift the blame
to the programmer behind the algorithm? ". (2) "Who is perceived as responsible for an ine-
galitarian algorithmic decision, the programmer or the user? ". (3) "Does an AI program-
mer exploit the moral wiggle room afforded by an AI? ". To respond to these questions,
we reproduce the "Delegation & Punishment" condition from Bartling and Fischbacher
(2012) and add two new treatments where the intermediary is either an RA or an AI.

Compared to the original paper, 50% of our participants A selected the egalitarian alloca-
tion in our Human treatment, compared to 28% in the original paper. Similar differences
are found concerning the inegalitarian allocation (33%) and the delegation (17%), against
17% for the inegalitarian allocation and 55% for delegation in the original paper. In our
study, participants delegate much less compared to the original study. We may perhaps
explain this by suggesting that dictators have a greater preference for control (Donnel-
lan and Robins, 2010). Alternatively, individuals with a Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT)
score lower than the average may be less inclined to engage in strategic reasoning, thus
ignoring the delegation option because they fail to perceive its benefits.21
Due to the use of the strategy method, we are able to observe the punishment patterns
that recipients are willing to apply, even if the delegation rate between Bartling and Fis-
chbacher (2012) and our paper differs. In the Human treatment we found a similar ten-
dency as in Bartling and Fischbacher (2012) concerning the punishment pattern. When
participants A choose the inegalitarian allocation, they receive a punishment equivalent

Comparing our baseline to the RA and AI conditions allows us to validate our behavioral
conjecture. We do not observe any significant difference in the decision to delegate across
our treatments. This indicates that the delegator is indifferent between delegating to a
human or an algorithm, and between a fully controlled RA and an AI. However, when
the intermediary chooses an inegalitarian allocation, different results emerge. There is
no difference in the punishment decision between a human intermediary and an RA; in
both cases, the intermediary is perceived more responsible than the delegator. But when
an AI is introduced, this result is reversed. When the programmer loses control over the
algorithm, she is perceived as less responsible, and the user who delegated the task is
seen as responsible for the algorithm's outcome. Finally, the programmer exploits the
moral wiggle room provided by the AI and chooses the inegalitarian distribution more
frequently.

We address a number of contributions from a variety of disciplines in order to provide a
comprehensive overview. The practice of delegating a decision in order to be perceived as
less responsible is a common occurrence in both corporate and political environments. In
this context, we would like to introduce the idea that participants can also use an algo-
rithm to shift responsibility. In terms of public policy, if companies are aware of this shift
in perceived responsibility, it may create an opportunity for them to develop profitable
algorithms regardless of the consequences for the population. This allows companies to
shift responsibility for the decisions made by algorithms onto users while reaping the
profits, thereby increasing inequality between users, programmers and recipients. This
reinforces the need for a robust regulatory framework. In particular, legal liability laws
play a crucial role in protecting individuals from such situations.

This study represents an initial exploration that can be expanded. Firstly, we posit

a critical assumption: the AI programmer exerts 50% control over the algorithm. This
theoretical assumption, however, does not reflect real-world scenarios and varies sig-
nificantly among AI programmers. Prior research by Bartling and Fischbacher (2012)
indicates that when control is absent (0%), human intermediaries are not perceived as
responsible. Responsibility is fully attributed to the dictator, suggesting a decrease in
responsibility proportional to the intermediary's level of control. A first extension for fur-
ther research would be to investigate whether the reduction in punishment is monotonic
depending on the percentage of control the programmer has on the AI. The second major
assumption is that individuals are aware that AI learning algorithms work autonomously
and that AI programmers have limited influence over these systems. Third, our model
assumes that participant B cannot select their role, either as an AI or RA programmer.
Another limitation of this laboratory experiment is that we assume students, selected
from various fields, will act as programmers. A field experiment to obtain natural data
could be conducted to compare lab data with field data. Another aspect that we neglect
in this experiment is that a firm's strategy and the programmer's preferences may con-
flict. Finally, algorithms are evolving rapidly. Five years ago, only humans were using
algorithms; now, some algorithms are also using other algorithms. This spillover can
have serious consequences in terms of diluting liability, highlighting the importance of
this topic.

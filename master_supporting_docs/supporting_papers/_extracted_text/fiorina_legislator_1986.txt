1. THE QUESTION
   On May 2, 1878, the House Commerce Committee favorably reported a

bill "to regulate interstate commerce and to prohibit unjust discrimination by
common carriers." Known as the Reagan bill, after the chairman, John
Reagan, of Texas, H.R. 3547 outlawed rebates and other forms of discrimina-
tion and included a restrictive long- and short-haul clause. The bill did not
establish a federal commission to enforce the law, however, providing only
that aggrieved shippers could bring suit in the federal courts and, if successful,
could recover triple damages. To supply additional enforcement incentives
railroads were to be fined a minimum of $1,000 for each offense, this amount
to be divided between the government and the "informer." After lengthy
debate and the addition of a provision outlawing pools (agreements to split
traffic or revenue) the Reagan bill passed the Democratic House, only to
disappear without a trace in the Republican Senate.1

Republican Senate adopted a bill reported by a select committee chaired by

   Standard commentaries (Friedman) view the establishment of the ICC as a
"natural" response to the demands of a new age--the first step in evolving
beyond a "state of courts and parties" (Skowronek). After a century of experi-
ence with such major delegations we tend to take them for granted and seldom
regard their existence as an interesting question (Lowi). As the railroad case
shows, however, such "natural adaptations" may be highly controversial at the
time and indeed may remain so. One should not forget that the existence and
scope of delegation has been a major theme of at least one presidential
candidate in four of the past six elections.3 Moreover, the proper limits and
scope of delegation is a subject of renewed interest in legal circles (Ely:
131--34; Aranson et al.; Mashaw), which has even reappeared in the opinions
of Supreme Court justices, namely, Renquist's dissents in the Benzene (448
U.S. 607 [1980]) and Cotton Dust (101 U.S. 2478 [1981]) cases. Thus, common
and familiar though it may be, legislative delegation remains a matter of both
political controversy and academic interest.4

   Of course, given the range and number of problems that modern govern-
ments confront, legislative delegation to bureaucratic entities is hardly sur-
prising. But complexity of governmental tasks is not a sufficient explanation
for observed patterns of delegation, because legislatures sometimes choose to
retain close control over complex policy realms such as taxation (Jaffe) while
relinquishing close control over many simpler realms. Moreover, economists
have proposed less bureaucratic alternatives to command and control regula-
tion (Schultze), and lawyers (especially aspiring judges) periodically remind
us of the merits of courts (Posner: 479-81). Given variation in observed
patterns of delegation, and the existence of institutional alternatives, legisla-
tive decisions to delegate to administrators suggest the operation of incentives
and factors beyond neutral efforts to deal with a complex world.

   In an earlier article (Fiorina, 1982) I identified a number of political bases
for delegations to administrators. This paper examines one of these in greater
depth. How does legislator uncertainty about the implementation of the
legislation affect the decision to delegate?

2. UNCERTAINTY, LOTTERIES, AND DELEGATION

   Though it might seem to be an archaic case with limited relevance to
modern concerns, the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 has a number of
advantages as a starting pointforthinking about delegation. First, in the 1880s
there was no extensive history of delegation or indeed even of federal regulat-
ion.5 The act is considered the first major federal regulatory intervention.
Congressional actors were considering the adoption of a new institutional
form, and consequently we might expect that they gave careful though to what
they were doing and what the likely results would be. Certainly the fact that
the establishment of the commission became a major issue suggests that the
ultimate decision to delegate the regulatory power was a conscious and calcu-
lated one. Second, while the complexity of railroad regulation frequently was
raised in debate (by friends of the railroads and/or the commission), a House
majority saw the issue in very simple terms--any competent public-spirited
individual could discharge the responsibilities imposed by the legislation.6
For the House at least there is no evidence that legislators felt overwhelmed
by the complexity of the problem they faced. Finally, the historical era is one
of a weak presidency and strong Congress. Thus, legislation is more likely to
embody the preferences and intentions of legislators than would be the case
in, say, the 1930s.

   A consideration that looms large in the debate is how the legislation would
operate in practice. Proponents of the commission argued that court enforce-
ment would fail because few shippers could match the legal and other re-
sources of the roads.7 Opponents countered that the proposed commission
did little to advance the cause of the small shipper vis-a-vis the railroads.
Would plaintiffs have to travel great distances--even to Washington--to
make a complaint? To testify in a proceeding? Proponents argued that "iron-
clad" laws would eliminate the flexibility needed to regulate a large and

   The preceding summary can provide only a brief overview of what was a
rich and often impressive legislative debate. If suffices to make clear, how-
ever, that many of the congressional actors viewed the impact of the regula-
tion as critically dependent on the enforcement procedure adopted. The
Senate majority acceded to House demands for explicit long- and short-haul
and pooling clauses but insisted on a commission, which they believed would
interpret the legislation leniently and sympathetically. The House majority
grudgingly accepted the commission only upon winning an explicit prohibi-
tion of pooling and a stonger long- and short-haul provision. Both sides knew
that any legislation inevitably would leave some discretion to Congress's
delegatee. The Senate majority wished to enhance such discretion, the House
majority to restrict it. Such desires were based on predictions of how the
regulation would be enforced by courts and a commission respectively, pre-
dictions evidently in reasonable agreement. Those are the general facts; the
question is whether we can reason about them in an intellectually profitable
way.

2.1. THE BASIC MODEL

   The elements of the model appear infigure1. Suppose that the status quo is
no regulation and that the legislature is debating whether to intervene. We
may think of their choice as one of weaker versus stronger regulation, or
pro-producer versus pro-consumer regulation, and represent the issue as the
choice of a point on the horizontal axis. Legislators have von
Neumann --Morgenstern utility functions defined over the policy space, func-
tions that are single-peaked and concave (implying risk aversion). The ith
legislator has ideal point Xj. Under such assumptions the outcome is the
position of the median legislator, x*. This much is standard (Black). Note,
incidentally, that utility functions are not assumed to be symmetric, a fact of
some consequence in what follows. Empirically, symmetry is a doubtful
property in the regulatory arena. Environmental activists, for example, argue
that the consequences of too stringent regulation are purely monetary,
whereas the consequences of too lenient regulation are human deaths and
environmental catastrophe. Or to take a less apocalyptic example, friends of
the railroads argued in 1886 that later Congresses could always strengthen a
regulatory law that was too loose but that by the time a later Congress could
relax a law that was too restrictive, railroads would go bankrupt, widows and
orphans would lose their savings, and so on.

   Of course, each legislator realizes that all statutory language contains
some ambiguity, conditions change, unforseen political events occur, and so
forth, all of which militate against treating x* as a certainty. Rather, passage
ofx* results in some probability distribution over the policies that actually will
be enforced. Given the language embodied in x*, legislators believe that the
actual level of regulation enforced could be as low as x' or as high as x". What
factors might systematically affect those probability distributions? Surely one
such factor would be the enforcement procedure adopted in the legislation.

   I think not. The two-stage process is actually a reasonably faithful abstrac-
tion from real legislative processes. These are hemmed in at every turn by
complicated rules of procedure (Shepsle, 1979). Many such rules have the
effect of preventing complicated proposals from being pitted directly against
each other. To elaborate, when a legislator seeks to call up a bill there may be a
vote on whether to consider it. Such a vote permits legislators to compare the
set of changes embodied in the bill to the status quo. Similarly, after a bill has
been debated and amended, there are opportunities to make global
comparisons--to recommit the bill, to strike the enacting clause, to pass the
bill. Between these preliminary and final stages, however, when the bill is
being debated and amended, consideration proceeds section by section, and
only amendments germane to the section are in order. As an empirical matter,
substantive provisions and enforcement provisions of legislation fall into
different sections, with enforcement typically following substance.16 Thus,
empirically, the legislative process consists ofa series ofrestricted dimension-
by-dimension decisions bounded on both ends by decisions pitting a complex
package against the status quo.

   The second consideration governing choice is the legislator's beliefs about
the nature of the administrative process that will implement the legislation.
Legislators far from the median who foresee a pattern of agency enforcement
to their liking will favor administrative regulation more than their opposites
who regard that pattern as unfavorable. On first glance this proposition is so
obvious as to seem devoid of interest but when conjoined with institutional
and historical information, the proposition suggests some interesting
implications.

   Can we identify factors which might bear a systematic relationship to
distance from the legislative median and to optimism and pessimism about
agency enforcement? A number of relevant possibilities are suggested by the
institutional structure of Congress. Consider first the committee system. As
characterized by empirical research (Shepsle, 1978), the committee assign-
ment process is largely one of self-selection: most members achieve member-
ship on one of the few committees they most strongly prefer. This leads to the
commonly observed pattern of unrepresentative committees; farm-state rep-
resentatives gravitate to the Agriculture committees, urban representatives
to the Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs committees, western representa-
tives to the Interior committees, and so forth. In short, many substantive
committees are overpopulated by "interested" congressmen. Often this
means a unified committee dominated by what Niskanen calls the "high
demand" legislators--those most desirous offederal activities/programs in the
relevant policy area--as in the committees named above. In other cases a
pattern of committee polarization develops, as those most interested in fed-
eral action and those most interested in federal inaction both seek member-
ship on the committee. Fenno's portrait of the divided House Education and
Labor committee is the classic example. Given its jurisdiction the committee

programs and an appropriations subcommittee control over agency purse

proportion of case 1 or 3 legislators falls across those categories. In divided or
polarized committees, we expect to observe committee division over the
enforcement question. Expecting to influence the agency, majority members
(case 1 or 3), especially the chair, favor administrative regulation; expecting
the agency to be influenced by the majority, minority members (case 2 or 4)
see relatively greater merit in court enforcement.

   The preceding hypotheses, especially those pertaining to polarized com-
mittees, rest on an implicit assumption--that committee influentials can
reasonably anticipate continuing as committee influentials, which in turn
entails the presumption that continued control by their party is likely. If
control of the legislature swings back and forth between the parties, so will the
capacity to influence the administrative process, which may in turn increase
the attractiveness of relatively less variable court control. Thus, during stable
electoral periods when one party enjoys a secure edge over the other we might
expect that majority party legislators would favor administrative processes
more than would minority party legislators, other things equal. During the
New Deal period congressional Democrats could contemplate control over
the administrative process for the foreseeable future. In the 1970s, with
Republican presidents and "emerging Republican majorities," Democratic
congressmen may have seen new merit in the greater insulation of courts.

regard their committees meet on more or less equal footing. But the Senate

most perfunctory attention to presidential nominations of administrators and
commissioners. The literature contains numerous citations of the datum that
the average length of Senate hearings on ICC commissioners between 1949
and 1974 was seventeen minutes (U.S. Congress). Such arguments overlook
informal channels of clearance with influential senators. In addition, they
ignore the existence of indirect paths of influence, such as an interest group's

their party or ideology. Though the New Deal burst of regulatory activity

1927, and Food and Drug Administration, 1931) of agency creation under
Republican Senates and presidents. Perhaps the modem Republican hostility
to administration simply reflects their typical minority status in Congress
over the past half-century.

2.2. POSSIBLE EXTENSIONS OF THE MODEL

   At several points in the discussion it has been difficult to avoid conflating
the identity of the delegatee (agency or court) with the breadth of the delega-
tion. Much of the literature speaks of broad delegations and delegations to
administrative agencies as flip sides of the same coin, again perhaps a legacy of
New Deal legislation such as that creating the Federal Communications
Commission, the Civil Aeronautics Board, and the Securities and Exchange
Commission. But these are distinct dimensions of legislative delegation. The
Sherman Act was a broad delegation to the courts; parts of the Clean Air Act
are narrow delegations to the Environmental Protection Agency. Perhaps the
two dimensions are highly correlated but this seems to be an assumption
rather than a conclusion based on empirical research.

   In the model developed herein, [x', x"] (the proportion of the policy space
over which the distributions pc andp° are defined) reflects the breadth of the
delegation. What happens ifthe potential policy range is expanded by render-
ing the language of the statute more ambiguous or by expanding administra-
tive discretion to suspend statutory provisions? Clearly, preferences now can
change. Referring back tofigures2a--2b, legislators in cases 1-4 may migrate
to cases 5, 7, 10, and 8. Thus, holding everything else constant, the general
effect of a symmetric increase in the breadth of a delegation will be to weaken
the logical relationship between distance from the median and homogeneity
of preference for administrative versus court procedures. In other words, an
increasing tendency to legislate in vague terms may be accompanied by a
decline in the extent to which reliance on the administrative process becomes
a clear-cut issue separating legislators with different substantive preferences.

   Ofcourse, the more interesting question arises when increasing breadth of
the delegation combines with increasing bias in the administrative process.
This complicates the analysis considerably, and I will do no more at this time
than present a loose argument that a model of this sort suggests. Consider the
cases (1, 2, 5, 6, 7) which exist when x° lies to the left of x°; that is, the
legislators agree that the administrative process is biased in favor of weaker
or pro-producer regulation. Now let agency discretion increase asymmetri-
cally--x' decreases while x" remains constant. Assume, as seems reasonable
in this case, that x° moves leftward with x'. Some former case 1 legislators
(agency supporters) may now become case 5 (mixed support), while the exist-
ing opposition of case 2 legislators to administrative enforcement will in-
tensify. Cases 5-7 remain indeterminate but, ceteris paribus, increasing
the distance between x* and Xj would lead to lower supportforadministrative

   In plain English, a perception of increasing bias in the administrative
process coupled with increasingly broad but asymmetric delegations would

weakened is well known (Stewart; Shapiro). Briefly, from the 1940s to the
1960s courts deferred to agencies, checking for adherence to the Administra-
tive Procedures Act (APA) and other pertinent statutes but generally accept-

"arbitrary and capricious"). But as the courts came to accept interest-group
interpretations of American politics in general, and of regulation in particular,
judicial deferral to agency expertise began to decline. Analogous develop-
ments occurred in Congress. Much of the legislation establishing the "new
social regulation" was filled with detailed procedural requirements going far
beyond the APA (McCubbins). Congressional majorities encouraged an ac-
cessible rulemaking process, sometimes going so far as to subsidize interve-
nors. And these same majorities provided every opportunity for disgruntled
interests to shift the conflict from the administrative arena to the judicial.
Though such developments admit to various interpretations, they are consis-
tent with legislators trying to counter increasingly evident biases in adminis-
trative processes.

of increasing bias in pa distributions--the decline of majoritarian control in
Congress itself. As central party leadership weakened, and as full committee
power devolved to component subcommittees, opportunities grew for hand-
fuls of unrepresentative members to influence administrative decisions with
less interference from broader political units.21 If such opportunities were
exploited, members may gradually have realized that regulatory policies were
increasingly diverging from legislative intent. This would lead to lessened
support for the administrative process, greater support for the judicial pro-

SUMMARY

   This paper has explored some aspects of legislative delegation of power
through the lens of a simple model of decisionmaking under uncertainty. In
the model legislators choose between two lotteries. The lottery arising from
judicial enforcement of statutory regulation is neutral in the sense that its
-expectation coincides with legislative intent--the position of the median
legislator. In contrast, the lottery arising from a delegation to an administra-
tive agency is biased in the sense that its expectation departs from legislative
intent. When considered in conjunction with the internal distribution of
power in the Congress the simple propositions generated by the model
suggest plausible empirical relationships between structural position in Con-
gress and support for administrative forms of regulation. As presently devel-
oped, the model deals primarily with the identity of the congressional
delegatee--bureaucracies or courts--but it may be possible to extend the
 model to theorize about the breadth of the congressional delegation as well.

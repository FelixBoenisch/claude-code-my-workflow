# Castelo, Bos & Lehmann (2019) — *Task-Dependent Algorithm Aversion*

## Research question

**Why and when** do consumers trust algorithms less than humans? The paper's central claim: **algorithm aversion depends on task type**. Specifically, people trust algorithms less for tasks that *seem subjective* (vs objective), believing algorithms lack the necessary affective capacity.

Sub-questions:
- Is the objective/subjective distinction *malleable* — i.e., can we shift perceived objectivity?
- Does making algorithms seem more "affectively human-like" reduce aversion for subjective tasks?

## Methods

- **Four online lab studies** (n > 1,400) and two online field studies (n > 56,000).
- **Variety of task domains**: medical, financial, judicial, creative, recommendation systems.
- Treatments manipulate perceived task objectivity and algorithmic affective human-likeness.

## Key results

- **Algorithms trusted and used *less* for subjective tasks** than for objective ones.
- **Perceived task objectivity is malleable**: framing a task as objective (rather than subjective) increases algorithm use, even for the same underlying task.
- **Increasing perceived affective human-likeness** of the algorithm (e.g., describing the algorithm as understanding emotions) increases use for subjective tasks.
- The mechanism is the belief that algorithms lack the affective capabilities required for subjective tasks.

## Main takeaways

1. **Task-type matters dramatically** for algorithm preferences. The aversion-vs-appreciation debate is partially resolved by attending to this moderator.
2. **Perceived objectivity is a marketing lever**: framing affects use.
3. **Affective human-likeness helps for subjective tasks**.
4. **Implications for AI deployment**: position algorithms for objective tasks; design for affective signal in subjective ones.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Castelo et al. is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 19.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) The manuscript's task is *objective*.** Weight prediction from a face photograph has a verifiable correct answer. By Castelo et al.'s logic, this is the regime where algorithms should be *trusted more*. The manuscript's ~50% delegation rates are consistent with moderate trust in an objective task setting. Had the manuscript used a subjective task (e.g., judging creativity or aesthetic appeal), delegation rates would likely be much lower per Castelo et al.

**(2) External validity framing.** The manuscript's prediction task is most analogous to forecasting / classification — clearly objective. Castelo et al. supports the design choice from the algorithm-aversion side.

**(3) Methodological model for moderator analysis.** Castelo et al.'s task-dependent framing is one of the most-cited contributions to the algorithm-aversion literature in the marketing tradition. It's a useful citation for the manuscript's design rationale.

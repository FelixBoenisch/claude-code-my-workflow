# Ivanova-Stenzel & Tolksdorf (2025 WP) — *Delegating in the Age of AI*

## Research question

To what extent is algorithm aversion explained by **mistrust of AI specifically**, vs. a **general preference for decision autonomy** (which also applies to human delegates)?

Prior research can't separate these because most studies compare delegating to AI against not delegating (or use between-subjects designs). The authors' contribution: use a **within-subject design** in which each participant separately decides whether to delegate the same task to a human or an AI, with the option to retain control.

## Methods

- **Two incentivised lab experiments**, TU Berlin (n not explicitly stated in the visible excerpt; the menu-based design is documented).
- **Within-subject design**: each participant makes delegation decisions for both AI and human agent across multiple choice problems.
- **Two decision domains** to test generalisability:
  - **Experiment 1**: Hiring task (binary; repeated; social decision).
  - **Experiment 2**: Forecasting task (non-binary; one-shot; analytical decision).
- **Menu-based elicitation** (following IVT-2024): vary the penalty rate for using the external agent; count delegation decisions.
- **Full information**: participants see their own performance and both agents' performance — no information asymmetry.

## Key results

- **Participants underutilise both AI and human agents** — even when informed that those agents outperform them.
- **Stronger preference for AI than for human delegates**: participants are *more* willing to delegate to AI than to other humans, even though they underutilise both.
- **The pattern holds across both decision domains** (hiring and forecasting).
- **Implication**: algorithm aversion is best understood as a *broader reluctance to give up decision autonomy*, not as algorithm-specific distrust. AI is actually *more* trusted than humans in this paradigm.

## Main takeaways

1. **Algorithm aversion ≈ autonomy preference.** The reluctance to delegate to AI is mostly the same reluctance people have toward delegation in general.
2. **AI is preferred to humans as a delegate.** This reverses the older "algorithm aversion" framing — in a within-subject head-to-head, AI wins.
3. **Performance information is insufficient.** Even with full information about agents' superior performance, people retain decision authority.
4. **Policy implication**: efforts to increase AI adoption should focus on reducing general aversion to delegation (e.g., via familiarity), not just on building trust in AI specifically.
5. The result reframes the algorithm-aversion debate around control / autonomy rather than around trust.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Strong theoretical alignment with M3.** The manuscript's M3 mechanism (process ownership / identity / accountability salience) and the IVT-2025 conclusion ("preference for decision autonomy") are essentially the same idea expressed in different terminology. Both papers argue that people resist *giving up* decision authority — not because they distrust the delegate but because retaining authority itself has psychological value. This is highly relevant for the manuscript:
- The manuscript's H1 reversal (more accountability → less delegation) is consistent with IVT-2025's "preference for decision autonomy" reading.
- The manuscript's M3 mechanism is the *Bénabou-Tirole identity-management* version of IVT-2025's "decision autonomy."

**(2) AI is preferred to humans — a useful framing point.** IVT-2025 reverses an old assumption that humans are preferred to algorithms. In a within-subject design, AI wins. This suggests the manuscript's algorithm (a Bernoulli draw at A's accuracy) is in fact relatively "trusted" — and the ~50% delegation rates reflect that.

**(3) Methodological complement.** IVT-2025 uses within-subject design to identify autonomy preferences vs algorithm-specific aversion. The manuscript uses between-subjects punishment manipulation to identify the role of accountability salience. The two papers approach the broader question (why don't people delegate more?) from complementary angles.

**(4) Same authors as `ivanova_stenzel_measuring_2024`.** This is a follow-up extending the 2024 method into the AI-vs-human comparison. The manuscript already cites the 2024 paper; citing the 2025 follow-up situates the manuscript inside the live evolution of this line of research.

# Maasland & Weißmüller (2022) — *Blame the Machine?*

## Research question

**Does the type of HR decision (pleasant promotion vs unpleasant dismissal) affect the likelihood of delegating to an algorithm?** And does delegation to an algorithm serve as blame avoidance, or does algorithm aversion dominate?

The intuition: HR managers face hard decisions like firing employees. If algorithmic delegation reduces moral cost, they should delegate the *unpleasant* decisions more than the pleasant ones. But if algorithm aversion is strong, both decision types are kept in human hands.

## Methods

- **Online vignette experiment**, *n* = 288 highly educated young professionals and graduate students in Germany.
- **2 × 2 between-subject design**:
  - Pleasant vs unpleasant HR decision: promotion vs dismissal.
  - With vs without pre-testing of the algorithm (option to see algorithm performance before deciding).
- **Vignette-based**: subjects read a description and indicate how likely they would be to use the algorithm vs make the decision themselves.
- **Moderators measured**: Confidence in Human (CIH) forecast; Confidence in Machine (CIM) forecast.
- **Replicates and extends Dietvorst et al. 2015** by adding the valence dimension (promotion vs dismissal).

## Key results

- **A tendency to delegate the unpleasant decision (dismissals) to the algorithm exists** but is small and **conditional**: it depends on whether subjects had the opportunity to *pretest* the algorithm, and on individuals' trust in machine vs human forecast.
- **Algorithm aversion dominates blame avoidance.** Even when subjects have a clear motive to delegate (unpleasant task, possible blame avoidance), the underlying aversion to algorithms suppresses delegation.
- **Pretesting the algorithm has a counterintuitive effect**: seeing the algorithm's performance does not increase trust as one might predict from Dietvorst 2015 — the relationship is more nuanced.
- **Confidence in machine forecast** is a strong moderator: those who trust algorithms generally do delegate the unpleasant task more.
- The headline conclusion: **algorithm aversion is a stronger force than blame avoidance** in HR delegation settings.

## Main takeaways

1. **Blame avoidance is a real motive for HR delegation** — there's some directional evidence that unpleasant tasks (dismissals) are more likely to be delegated to the algorithm.
2. **But aversion dominates.** Even with the strongest possible blame-avoidance motive, baseline algorithm aversion suppresses the effect.
3. **Algorithm pretesting helps unevenly**, suggesting trust in algorithms isn't built simply by exposure.
4. **HR managers are not yet "fully strategic" about algorithmic blame-shifting** — the rational-choice model would predict more delegation than is observed.
5. The paper's contribution is documenting the *limit* of blame-avoidance motives in algorithmic delegation, in a moral-domain HR setting.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Maasland & Weißmüller is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 27: "they find in a computer-aided HR setting that algorithm aversion *dominates* blame avoidance: the prospect of using an algorithm to delegate an unpleasant HR decision (a dismissal) does not overcome the underlying aversion to algorithms in this domain."

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Closest *valence-based* precedent.** Maasland & Weißmüller manipulates the *unpleasantness* of the underlying task (promotion vs dismissal). The manuscript manipulates the *consequences* of bad outcomes (punishment vs no punishment). Both papers ask the same conceptual question through different design levers: does the moral cost of a decision motivate delegation to an algorithm? Both papers find a directional effect but not a dominant one — the canonical blame-avoidance story is overstated.

**(2) "Algorithm aversion dominates blame avoidance" anticipates the manuscript's H1 reversal.** In Maasland, the predicted effect (more delegation for unpleasant tasks) is muted by general algorithm aversion. In the manuscript, the predicted effect (more delegation under punishment) *reverses*. The manuscript pushes the same logic harder: when the consequences become *salient* (real punishment, not just vignette-imagined unpleasantness), aversion isn't just dominant — it actively repels.

**(3) Methodological contrast: vignette vs incentivised lab.** Maasland uses an online vignette; the manuscript uses an incentivised lab experiment with real money on the line. Vignettes capture stated preferences; incentivised paradigms capture behavioural choices. The fact that both find weakened-or-absent blame-avoidance motives is reassuring convergence across methods.

**(4) The "pretesting" finding is design-relevant.** Maasland finds that letting subjects pretest the algorithm doesn't straightforwardly increase trust. The manuscript's design provides Player A with *common-knowledge calibration* of the algorithm to her own performance — a stronger version of pretesting. Whether this works the same way as Maasland's pretesting is an interesting question; the manuscript's roughly-balanced delegation rates (42–59%) suggest the calibration *does* generate substantive trust (subjects don't avoid the algorithm en masse).

**(5) Worth flagging the policy implication overlap.** Maasland: HR managers don't yet strategically exploit algorithms for blame-shifting. The manuscript: subjects don't exploit algorithmic delegation for punishment-avoidance — they actively avoid it when stakes are salient. Together, these papers paint a consistent picture: the *strategic* responsibility-shifting motive that Bartling, Hamman, and Tontrup highlight is *moderated* by direct algorithm aversion when the algorithm itself is the delegate.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Paragraph 27 cites it correctly with a fair summary.

**Worth borrowing:** Maasland's framing — "algorithm aversion dominates blame avoidance" — is a clean rhetorical move the manuscript could echo: "Our results extend Maasland & Weißmüller (2022): algorithm aversion dominates blame avoidance, and indeed *reverses* the predicted delegation pattern when the consequences of bad outcomes are made fully salient."

**Limitation to note:** Maasland's sample (n = 288, young professionals + graduate students) and vignette methodology limit the generalisability. The manuscript improves on this with an incentivised paradigm and a larger sample (n = 322) on Prolific UK.

# Fershtman & Gneezy (2001) — *Strategic Delegation: An Experiment*

## Research question

In strategic bargaining contexts, **does delegation to an agent change the outcome of the game even when the agent cannot serve as a commitment device** (e.g., because the agent's compensation scheme is unobservable)? The conventional theoretical claim is that, without observable commitment, delegation should not affect outcomes. The authors test this in an ultimatum game.

Sub-questions:
1. Does *mandatory* vs *optional* delegation matter?
2. Does delegation by the *proposer* vs the *responder* produce different effects?
3. Are responders less keen to punish "unfair" offers when the proposer delegates?

## Methods

Laboratory ultimatum game experiment with multiple treatments.

- **Baseline**: standard ultimatum game (proposer makes offer, responder accepts or rejects).
- **Proposer-delegate** treatments: proposer hires an agent who makes the offer. Both mandatory-delegation and optional-delegation versions.
- **Responder-delegate** treatments: responder hires an agent who accepts/rejects. Both observable and unobservable compensation schemes.
- **"Third player" / hostage** treatment: as a robustness check, a third player is added who gets a fixed reward if the proposal is accepted but who is *not* an agent of the proposer. Tests whether the mere presence of a third party with a stake alters the outcome.
- Subjects in Tilburg / Tel Aviv environment.

## Key results

**Result 1 — Proposer's payoff is significantly higher with a delegate.**
When the proposer uses an agent (mandatory or optional), her share rises substantially compared to the baseline ultimatum game. This is true *even though* the agent in this setup cannot serve as a commitment device.

**Result 2 — Responders accept tougher offers from agents.**
The mechanism: responders are less willing to punish the proposer for an unfair offer when doing so also punishes the delegate-agent (who is not the one benefiting from the unfair division).

**Result 3 — The "third player" hostage treatment confirms the channel.**
Even without delegation, simply having a third party with a stake in the deal makes responders accept tougher proposals. Proposers extract more.

**Result 4 — Responder-side delegation can also affect outcomes.**
When the responder delegates with unobservable compensation, the proposer makes greedier offers because the responder's agent is perceived as less willing to punish the proposer for unfair offers made to a *third party* (the responder).

**Result 5 — Observable commitment delegation works as theory predicts.**
When the responder's compensation scheme is observable and irreversible, it serves as a commitment device — the proposer makes much more generous offers, and the responder captures most of the pie.

## Main takeaways

1. **Delegation alters bargaining outcomes even without commitment power.** The mechanism is the responder's reluctance to inflict punishment on a third-party agent who isn't the one benefiting.
2. **The result generalises Bartling-Fischbacher-style responsibility-shifting into the strategic-bargaining domain**, with a clear behavioral mechanism rather than a theoretical one.
3. **Hiring an agent functions as a buffer that reduces the moral-cost-of-punishing** for the responder.
4. **A third party (even without delegation) attenuates retaliatory behavior** — the "hostage" treatment shows the responder cares about not punishing someone who wasn't responsible for the unfair outcome.
5. The paper is widely cited as the canonical empirical demonstration that delegation reshapes strategic bargaining outcomes through behavioural channels.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Fershtman & Gneezy is **not yet cited** in literature.tex but should be added to subsection 1 (Responsibility avoidance through delegation) per the recent restructuring discussion. It's the canonical bargaining-setting analog of Bartling & Fischbacher.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Extends the responsibility-shifting result to a strategic context.** Most of the experimental literature subsection 1 covers comes from dictator-game variants. Fershtman & Gneezy demonstrates that the same mechanism — delegation makes punishment / unfair-treatment harder for the second party to inflict — operates in strategic bargaining settings. This widens the empirical base for the claim that responsibility-shifting is a general phenomenon.

**(2) The "hostage" mechanism is conceptually adjacent to the manuscript's design.** In Fershtman & Gneezy's third-player treatment, the third party has a stake in the outcome but is not an agent of the proposer. Responders still accept tougher proposals because punishing the proposer harms the third party. This is structurally similar to the manuscript's Player B having a stake in Player A's prediction outcome. In the manuscript, however, the algorithm is the "intermediary" rather than a third party with a stake — so the Fershtman & Gneezy mechanism doesn't directly translate, but the conceptual machinery is in the same family.

**(3) Mandatory vs optional delegation distinction is methodologically informative.** Fershtman & Gneezy show that the proposer's payoff rises with delegation whether delegation is mandatory or optional. In the manuscript, delegation is optional (Player A chooses whether to delegate to the algorithm) and the H1 manipulation varies the salience of consequences. The Fershtman & Gneezy finding that "even optional delegation produces the effect" is a useful background result.

**(4) Important caveat: the F&G effect requires a hireable agent.** The manuscript's algorithm is fundamentally different from F&G's human delegate — the algorithm has no stake, cannot be punished, and is calibrated to the principal's accuracy. The F&G channel ("responder doesn't want to punish the agent who isn't benefiting") doesn't apply to the manuscript's algorithmic setting because there's no human delegate whose punishment-cost is at stake.

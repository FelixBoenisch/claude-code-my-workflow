# Logg, Minson & Moore (2019) — *Algorithm Appreciation*

## Research question

Counter to the prevailing "algorithm aversion" narrative, **do people sometimes *prefer* algorithmic to human advice?** And if so, under what conditions does the preference flip?

## Methods

Six experiments, varying:
- The task domain (numeric estimates from visual stimuli, song popularity forecasts, romantic attraction forecasts).
- Whether algorithmic and human advice are presented jointly or separately.
- Whether the comparison is between an algorithm and another person, or an algorithm and *oneself*.
- Whether the participant is a layperson or a forecasting expert.

The numeric estimation task in Experiment 1A is **predicting people's weight from photos** — the *same task* used in the manuscript.

## Key results

- **People give more weight to advice when it comes from an algorithm than from a human** ("algorithm appreciation") in the numerical estimation and forecasting tasks.
- Algorithm appreciation persists when advice is given jointly or separately.
- **Appreciation wanes when**: (a) people choose between an algorithm's estimate and *their own* (not external human advice); (b) people have forecasting expertise.
- **Experienced professionals rely *less* on algorithmic advice than lay people** — and their accuracy suffers as a result.
- Researchers themselves predicted the opposite of these results (Experiment 1D), highlighting a discipline-wide misperception.

## Main takeaways

1. **Algorithm appreciation is real and substantial** in many domains — the algorithm-aversion-only story is incomplete.
2. **The appreciation is conditional**: weakens when self-advice is the alternative, and weakens for experts.
3. **Researchers' priors are wrong**: the field expected aversion and got appreciation.
4. **The result reframes the algorithm-vs-human comparison literature**: both aversion and appreciation can occur, and which one shows up depends critically on the comparison condition.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Logg et al. is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 19. **Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) The weight-from-face prediction task comes from here.** Logg et al. Experiment 1A used this task. The manuscript adopts it explicitly with the rationale that humans have a lifetime of practice at this mapping while algorithms cannot easily exploit large training corpora. Citation in design.tex ¶40 is precisely the right link.

**(2) The "self vs external advisor" pattern is relevant.** Logg et al. find that algorithm appreciation *waned* when subjects chose between their own forecast and the algorithm's — i.e., in exactly the comparison the manuscript uses. The manuscript's relatively balanced delegation rate (~50%, not strongly above 50%) is therefore consistent with Logg et al.'s finding: the self-vs-algorithm comparison generates indifference, not appreciation.

**(3) Algorithm aversion is *not* the prevailing finding in the literature.** The manuscript's lit-review framing (citing both aversion and appreciation papers) is essential. Logg et al. provides the strongest empirical counter to the "people hate algorithms" narrative.

**(4) The design.tex paragraph 39 FB CHECK can lean on this paper.** Felix flagged the citation of Dietvorst 2015 and Logg 2019 for "perceived relative competence" framing. Logg 2019 is about appreciation as a function of perceived ability — it does support the claim that algorithm preferences depend on perceived relative competence, though indirectly. The framing in design.tex is therefore defensible.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Design.tex paragraph 40 correctly attributes the weight-from-face task.

**Worth borrowing:** The "researchers predicted the opposite result" finding is striking. The manuscript could note: "Our H1 reversal is consistent with the broader pattern Logg, Minson & Moore (2019) identify: prior to running experiments, researchers consistently overestimate the strength of algorithm aversion. **Methodological note:** The Logg et al. weight prediction task is well-validated and ecologically meaningful. The manuscript's use of the same task is well-grounded.

# Hill (2015) — *Does Delegation Undermine Accountability?*

## Research question

Prior experimental work (Bartling & Fischbacher 2012, Hamman et al. 2010, Coffman 2011, Oexl & Grossman 2013) infers responsibility-shifting from **indirect** measures — costly punishment in dictator games. The implicit assumption is that inequitable allocations are *blameworthy*, but no prior study directly tested that subjects perceived the allocations as such.

Hill's question: **does the delegation effect on responsibility hold when blame is measured directly?** And does it hold in a different domain — legislative delegation to administrative agencies — where blame is what voters actually care about?

## Methods

- **Vignette-based experiment**, not incentivised.
- **Setting**: legislative delegation of policy-making authority to administrative agencies (e.g., Congress delegating regulatory authority).
- Subjects read scenarios in which a legislature either (i) directly enacts an unpopular policy or (ii) delegates the policy-making authority to a powerless administrative agency.
- Subjects directly rate the **blameworthiness** of the principal (legislature) and the agent (agency) using Likert-scale measures.
- Multiple treatment variations to test robustness.

## Key results

- **Delegation reduces blame attributed to the principal**, even when the agency is *powerless* to change the substantive outcome. This replicates B&F and Oexl-Grossman with direct (rather than indirect, punishment-based) blame measures.
- **The clarity thesis is supported**: when voters cannot easily attribute relative responsibility between Congress and agency, they underassign blame to legislatures.
- This is "first-of-its-kind" direct experimental evidence that the responsibility-shifting effect documented in the experimental-economics literature is genuinely about *blame attribution*, not just about punishment behaviour that might be motivated by other considerations.

## Main takeaways

1. **Responsibility-shifting through delegation is robust to direct measurement.** The B&F result generalises beyond the dictator-game punishment paradigm.
2. **It works even with a powerless intermediary** — the agency need not have real choice for the legislature to shed blame.
3. **The clarity-thesis mechanism applies**: information asymmetry between voters and policy-makers helps legislators escape blame via delegation to agencies.
4. The paper validates the *interpretive* assumption of the experimental-economics literature on responsibility-shifting: people in those experiments really are attributing *blame*, not just punishing for some other reason.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Hill is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 13: "Hill (2015) provides a vignette-based corroboration in a legislative-delegation context. Subjects directly evaluate blame in unincentivised hypothetical scenarios; the resulting attributions track the patterns observed in incentivised experiments, suggesting that punishment captures more qualitative blame attributions reliably."

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Validates the punishment-as-blame interpretation the manuscript relies on.** The manuscript's H2 uses Player B's costly punishment as a measure of responsibility attribution. Hill's direct-blame measurement validates this measurement choice — punishment really does track blame in this literature.

**(2) The "powerless intermediary" finding is relevant.** Hill replicates the Oexl-Grossman finding that delegation shifts blame even to a powerless agency. This is the canonical pattern in dictator-game settings. The manuscript's algorithm is more powerless than any human agency (it's a Bernoulli draw with no agency at all), yet does *not* absorb significant blame in the manuscript's H2 result. This is a useful contrast: the dictator-game pattern doesn't extend to prediction-task settings even when the algorithm is structurally equivalent to a "powerless intermediary."

**(3) The vignette-vs-lab gap is moderate in this paper.** Hill uses vignettes; the canonical Bartling/Coffman/Oexl literature uses incentivised lab. Hill replicates the qualitative pattern with vignettes, suggesting the effect is robust across methods. The fact that the manuscript's null is in the *incentivised lab* paradigm — where this literature has produced its strongest claims — makes the null more striking.

**(4) Different domain: legislative delegation.** Hill's substantive domain is legislative/administrative delegation, not algorithmic delegation. This expands the external validity of the responsibility-shifting result, but it also raises a question for the manuscript: legislative delegation has *political* salience (voters care about blame); algorithmic delegation may have weaker political salience. The manuscript's prediction task is somewhere in between — has small monetary stakes but no political relevance.

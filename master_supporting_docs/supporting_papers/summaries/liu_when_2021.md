# Liu (2021) — *In AI We Trust?*

## Research question

How does **machine-learning AI** (where rules are generated by the system from data) compare to **rule-based AI** (where rules are programmed by humans) in terms of users' uncertainty about and trust in the decisions? And can **transparency** about decision-making rationales reduce uncertainty for ML systems specifically?

The motivating idea: rule-based AI inherits "humanlike" trust because the rules come from humans; machine-generated rules are more alien and may generate more uncertainty.

## Methods

- **Online experiment**, *n* = 491 participants.
- **2 × 3 × 2 between-subjects design**:
  - **Agency locus**: human-made rules vs machine-learned rules
  - **Transparency**: no explanation vs placebic explanation vs real explanation
  - **Task**: detecting fake news vs assessing personality
- Subjects interact with what they're told is an AI decision system; rate trust, uncertainty, intention to use.

## Key results

- **Machine-learning AI triggers less social presence**, which increases uncertainty and lowers trust compared to rule-based AI.
- **Transparency reduces uncertainty and enhances trust** — but the mechanism differs between the two AI types.
- **Quality of explanation matters**: real, meaningful explanations help; placebic explanations less so.
- **Implication**: explainable AI design needs to match the type of AI; one-size-fits-all transparency interventions are not effective.

## Main takeaways

1. **Machine-learning AI is more distrusted than rule-based AI** — driven partly by reduced perception of "social presence" / humanlike-ness.
2. **Transparency works, but contingently** on the type of AI and the quality of explanation.
3. **Anthropocentric trust models** are stretched when AI's decision-making process diverges from human logic.
4. **For policy**: simply requiring "transparency" of AI is insufficient; the *kind* of transparency matters.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Rule-based vs ML AI distinction is important.** The manuscript's algorithm is *rule-based* in the strongest sense — a Bernoulli draw at known probability, fully transparent and predictable. By Liu's logic, this should be the *most trustworthy* type of AI, with the lowest uncertainty. The manuscript's design therefore avoids the ML-distrust effect Liu documents. Delegation rates of ~50% are consistent with this: subjects don't reject the algorithm on grounds of opacity or alien decision-making.

**(2) The trust-uncertainty channel is *muted* in the manuscript's design.** Liu's mechanism — trust depends on uncertainty about decision-making rules — is largely closed off by the manuscript's calibration. Player B knows exactly what the algorithm does (a Bernoulli draw at A's accuracy); there's no uncertainty about the rules. Whatever residual delegation aversion the manuscript identifies cannot be attributed to ML-style opacity.

**(3) "Anthropocentric knowledge" framework parallels Bigman & Gray's mind-perception framework.** Both papers argue that users apply human-trust heuristics to AI, with mixed success. The manuscript's design strips out the anthropomorphisation question entirely by presenting the algorithm as a Bernoulli draw. This is methodologically clean.

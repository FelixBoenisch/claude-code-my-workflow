# Falk & Szech (2013) — *Morals and Markets*

## Research question

Does **market interaction erode moral values** — specifically, the willingness to harm an uninvolved third party for monetary gain? Or do markets simply reveal pre-existing preferences without changing them?

The classical hypothesis is associated with political philosophers (Sandel; reaching back to Marx) but had been hard to test causally because institutions and values co-evolve. Falk & Szech provide an experimental design that holds preferences fixed across treatments via random assignment.

## Methods

- **Lab experiment**, University of Bonn, *n* = 787 across 9 treatments.
- **The Mouse Paradigm**: subjects face a choice between (a) saving the life of a healthy "surplus" mouse from a German university breeding facility (which would otherwise be killed) and (b) accepting money. The mouse paradigm is real — actual mice; experimenters paid for any mice that were "saved" by subjects' decisions.
- **Three main treatments**:
  - **Individual**: subject decides alone whether to kill the mouse for €10.
  - **Bilateral market**: one buyer, one seller bargain over the price at which the mouse will be killed. Total surplus €20 to split between the two human participants.
  - **Multilateral market**: many buyers and sellers; double-auction.
- Additional control treatments confirm the effect is specific to morally consequential trade — markets do *not* affect valuations for morally neutral consumption goods.

## Key results

- **Willingness to kill the mouse rises substantially in both market treatments** compared to the individual treatment.
- **Multilateral markets are worse than bilateral** — multi-party trading erodes moral standards even more (prices deteriorate; killing rate rises).
- For morally neutral goods, no analogous market effect appears — the result is specific to moral consequences.
- The authors offer three mechanisms for the market-erosion effect:
  1. **Shared responsibility** — in markets, two people agree to a trade, diffusing guilt.
  2. **Social information about norms** — observing others trade legitimises trade.
  3. **Materialistic framing** — markets focus attention on prices and competition, diverting from moral consequences.

## Main takeaways

1. **Market interaction causally erodes moral standards** in dealings involving harm to third parties. The classical hypothesis is supported in a randomly-controlled setting.
2. **Multi-party market interaction is worse than bilateral** — the more diffusion of responsibility, the lower the moral threshold.
3. **The effect is specific to moral content** — markets don't change preferences for neutral goods.
4. **Diffusion of responsibility is a primary mechanism** — shared responsibility for an action with negative externalities lowers the felt moral cost.
5. This is one of the foundational diffusion-of-responsibility results in modern experimental economics, alongside B&F and Hamman.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Falk & Szech is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 13: "Beyond delegation per se, the broader market-design literature documents that intermediation and group decision-making can erode moral concern for third parties: Falk & Szech (2013) show that bilateral and multilateral market interactions substantially increase subjects' willingness to harm a third party (a mouse) for monetary gain, a canonical demonstration of diffusion of responsibility."

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Canonical reference for the diffusion-of-responsibility framework.** Falk & Szech is one of the two foundational empirical results (alongside Hamman et al. 2010) that anchors the diffusion-of-responsibility literature the manuscript draws on. When the manuscript invokes "diffusion of responsibility" as a candidate mechanism, Falk & Szech provides the strongest experimental evidence that the channel is real and large.

**(2) The "more agents = more diffusion" pattern is relevant.** Falk & Szech's multilateral > bilateral > individual ordering implies that diffusion is *increasing* in the number of agents involved. An algorithm is, in some sense, an additional agent — adding it to a decision could increase diffusion. But the manuscript's algorithm is also *pre-determined* (Bernoulli at A's accuracy), so the "diffusion" channel may not bite the same way. The manuscript's H1 reversal — adding the algorithm + adding punishment salience *reduces* delegation — runs against the Falk & Szech "more diffusion = more comfort with harm" pattern.

**(3) Distinct from B&F's "shifting blame" framing.** Falk & Szech emphasises that *both* parties feel reduced responsibility in market trades — the diffusion is symmetric. B&F emphasises *shifting* responsibility from principal to delegee. These are different mechanisms; the manuscript could clarify which it's drawing on. M3 (process ownership) is closer to Falk & Szech's diffusion than to B&F's shifting, in the sense that delegation in the manuscript's design *can't* shift responsibility (the algorithm is calibrated to A's own performance) but *could* diffuse it. The manuscript's null on H2 suggests neither shifting nor diffusion is operating.

**(4) Important caveat — Falk & Szech is contested.** Subsequent work (e.g., Bartling, Weber & Yao 2015; Ziegler et al. 2020) has questioned whether the Falk & Szech effect replicates with different framings and samples. The "markets erode morals" headline has been the subject of substantial replication debate. The manuscript should cite Falk & Szech as a *representative* finding of the diffusion-of-responsibility tradition rather than as definitive evidence.

# Erat (2013) — *Avoiding Lying: The Case of Delegated Deception*

## Research question

**Will people pay to delegate the act of lying to an agent**, even when they could lie themselves at no cost? If so, this would suggest that the disutility of lying is significant enough that people pay to outsource it — and that delegation can effectively reduce the moral cost of deception.

Two sub-questions:
1. Does the propensity to delegate scale with the **severity of harm** the lie inflicts?
2. Are there **gender differences** in willingness to delegate deception?

## Methods

**Lab experiment** using a modified sender-receiver deception game.

- **Setup**: A principal can either lie directly to a receiver or hire an agent to lie on her behalf. Lying produces a higher monetary payoff for the principal at the cost of the receiver.
- **Harm severity** is varied: lies that cause more harm to the receiver vs less harm.
- **Cost of delegation**: principals pay a small monetary cost to use the agent.

## Key results

- **A significant fraction of principals pay to delegate lying** even when they could lie themselves. Delegation is monetarily inefficient but psychologically valuable.
- **Delegation propensity scales with harm**: when the lie hurts the receiver more, principals are more willing to pay an agent to do the lying.
- **Gender difference**: women are more likely to delegate, especially when harm is larger.

## Main takeaways

1. **The disutility of lying is real** — people pay to avoid doing it themselves.
2. **Delegated deception works as a moral cost-reduction mechanism** — paying an agent to lie reduces the principal's psychological cost more than just lying directly.
3. **The effect is moderated by harm**: the larger the harm, the more valuable delegation is as moral cover.
4. **Gender heterogeneity** suggests psychological costs of lying are unevenly distributed.
5. The result is consistent with the broader responsibility-shifting / moral wiggle room literature: principals pay for psychological distance from morally costly acts.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Erat is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 13: "Erat (2013) provides a related demonstration that people are willing to pay to delegate the act of lying, consistent with delegation reducing the psychological cost of behavior that one expects to be judged negatively."

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Critical price-for-delegation result.** Erat shows people are willing to pay to delegate — establishing that the responsibility-shifting motive isn't free, it's something subjects are willing to bear a cost for. The manuscript's design imposes *no monetary cost* on delegation (delegation is costless). Erat's logic implies that costless delegation should be widely used — the manuscript's ~50% delegation rate is therefore not driven by avoiding a cost. The reverse: even at zero cost, only half the subjects delegate. This is informative about the strength of the moral-cost-reduction motive: in Erat, it's strong enough to overcome a monetary cost; in the manuscript's setting, it's not even strong enough to motivate delegation when free.

**(2) The "harm severity" finding is highly relevant.** Erat finds delegation rates *rise* with the harm of the lie. The manuscript varies "harm salience" via punishment on/off — and finds delegation rates *fall* in the high-salience condition. This is the H1 reversal. The mirror-image direction across the two papers suggests the moral-cost-reduction motive that drives delegation in Erat (selfish lie, direct moral wrongdoing) does *not* generalise to the manuscript's setting (uncertain prediction, aligned incentives).

**(3) Connects to the broader "delegated wrongdoing" literature.** Erat is one of the few experimental papers that examines delegating *active* moral wrongdoing (lying), as opposed to delegating an *outcome* (dictator games). The manuscript's design is closer to the dictator-game tradition (outcome, not act). Erat's finding that the responsibility-shifting motive is strongest when one is delegating an *act* of moral wrongdoing — not just an outcome — is a useful framing point: the manuscript's prediction task is *neither* an act of wrongdoing *nor* a fully unfair outcome, which may explain why the motive is muted.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Paragraph 13 cites Erat correctly with appropriate framing as "willing to pay to delegate the act of lying."

**Worth borrowing for the discussion:** The reversal pattern across Erat (delegation scales *up* with harm) and the manuscript (delegation scales *down* with punishment-salience) is a clean rhetorical contrast. The manuscript could note: "In Erat (2013), delegation rates scale up with the harm of the lie, consistent with stronger moral cost driving more delegation. Our manipulation operates on a different margin — the punishment for a bad outcome rather than the harm of the action itself — and produces the opposite pattern. This contrast helps locate the moral-cost-reduction motive: it bites on *acts* of wrongdoing more than on *outcomes* of uncertain decisions."

**Limitation:** Erat 2013 is a small, single-author paper; results have replicated qualitatively but the effect sizes vary across replications. The headline gender result has been less robust in follow-ups than the main delegation result. Use the main result; treat the gender finding with appropriate caution.

# Killoran & Park (2026) — *Learn from the blame game when AI causes harm*

## Type and contribution

**Opinion piece**, not an empirical study. Published in PNAS as a 3-page perspective. Both authors at Gustavson School of Business, University of Victoria.

The motivating case is the 2023 class-action lawsuit against UnitedHealth Group, alleging that an AI algorithm (nH Predict) systematically denied medically necessary post-acute care. The article uses this case to argue that public blame toward AI systems should be **reconceptualised as diagnostic data about socio-technical failure**, not dismissed as "scapegoating."

## Central argument

1. **AI systems cannot be morally responsible** — moral agency requires intention and moral understanding, which AI lacks.
2. **But blaming AI is informative.** Treat public blame as a *diagnostic signal* about where socio-technical systems are failing.
3. **Three-part blame taxonomy**:
   - **Ignorance** — signals knowledge gaps in design, deployment, or use.
   - **Diminished Control** — signals that humans have lost meaningful authority over the system.
   - **Condemnation** — signals violated ethical or safety expectations.
4. These narratives map onto actors: designers, deployers, institutions, regulators. Each is a distinct entry point for governance intervention.
5. **Implication**: blame is a governance resource. Institutions should use blame to investigate root causes and clarify distributed responsibility.

## Main critique of scapegoating theory

1. **Scapegoating overcorrects**: dismissing blame as irrational ignores that blame reflects real perceptions of agency, opacity, and unpredictability.
2. **Scapegoating ignores symbolic dimension**: blame signals violated expectations, which is valuable information.
3. **Scapegoating offers no alternatives**: tells you what *not* to do but not what to do instead.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Killoran & Park is **not yet cited** in literature.tex but was added to the bib in the recent lit-review pass.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Useful framing for the manuscript's policy discussion.** The paper isn't empirical and doesn't bear directly on H1 or H2. But it provides a *governance framework* that the manuscript's policy implications can engage with. The manuscript's H1 reversal — that subjects pull back from algorithmic delegation under salient accountability — is consistent with Killoran & Park's "Diminished Control" narrative: when subjects perceive that accountability is being shifted away from humans, they react by *retaining* human authority.

**(2) Recent PNAS perspective.** This is one of the most recent (April 2026) high-profile pieces on AI blame governance. Citing it positions the manuscript inside the contemporary policy conversation.

**(3) Not central to the lit review.** The manuscript's empirical contribution is in the experimental tradition; Killoran & Park is in the governance / opinion tradition. Cite for context, not as central anchor.

**Where to cite in literature.tex:**

- Most natural placement: a brief mention in the conclusion / policy section: "Killoran & Park (2026), writing in PNAS, argue that public blame toward AI should be treated as diagnostic data about socio-technical failure, mapping onto a three-part taxonomy of Ignorance, Diminished Control, and Condemnation. Our finding that the prospect of punishment causes principals to retain rather than delegate decision authority speaks most directly to the Diminished Control narrative: subjects resist losing control when the moral stakes become salient."

**Limitation:** It's an opinion piece, not an empirical paper. Useful for framing but not for substantive evidence.

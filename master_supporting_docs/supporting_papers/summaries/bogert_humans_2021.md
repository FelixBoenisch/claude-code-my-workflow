# Bogert, Schecter & Watson (2021) — *Humans rely more on algorithms than social influence as a task becomes more difficult*

## Research question

Algorithm appreciation (Logg 2019) and algorithm aversion (Dietvorst 2015) both exist in the literature. **Does task difficulty determine which one shows up?** The hypothesis: as tasks get harder, the value of expert algorithmic advice increases, so subjects rely on it more. Social advice (crowd wisdom) may have the opposite trajectory — useful for easy tasks, less useful when subjects know they can't easily verify.

## Methods

- **Three preregistered online experiments**, *N* = 1,500 total.
- **Task**: count people in a photograph — varying numbers of people in the image to create graded difficulty.
- **Advice source manipulation**: "an algorithm trained on 5000 images" vs "the average guess of 5000 other people."
- **Judge Advisor System (JAS)**: subjects answer first, receive advice, answer again. Weight on Advice (WOA) measures how much they updated.

## Key results

- **As tasks become more difficult, subjects rely more on algorithmic advice than on social/crowd advice.**
- The effect persists after controlling for advice quality, numeracy, and accuracy.
- **Inaccurate "algorithmic" advice is more strongly disregarded** than equally inaccurate "crowd" advice — but only when subjects can detect the inaccuracy.
- Algorithm advice is preferred for objective intellective tasks; this is consistent with Castelo 2019's task-dependent framing.

## Main takeaways

1. **Task difficulty is a key moderator** of algorithm reliance.
2. **For hard objective tasks, subjects prefer algorithms** over crowd advice.
3. **Algorithms are not universally distrusted** — they win in domains where they have a clear competence advantage.
4. **Inaccurate algorithms are disregarded more harshly** when the inaccuracy is observable.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Bogert et al. is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 19.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) The "difficulty" moderator is relevant.** The manuscript's prediction task (weight from face photograph) is genuinely difficult. Bogert et al. predicts subjects should rely more on algorithmic advice for harder tasks. The manuscript's relatively balanced delegation rate (~50%) is consistent with this — for a hard task, algorithm aversion is not extreme.

**(2) The "counting people" task is a closer analog to the manuscript's task than other algorithm-aversion papers.** Both involve a perceptual/numeric estimate from an image. The convergence in findings — algorithm uptake is moderate to high for these tasks — supports the external validity of the manuscript's task choice.

**(3) Different question, complementary methodology.** Bogert et al. compares algorithm vs crowd; the manuscript compares algorithm vs self. The two papers together cover much of the relevant algorithm-comparison space.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Paragraph 19 cites correctly.

**Worth noting:** Bogert et al. is a good empirical example that *task difficulty* and *task type* (objective vs subjective; intellective vs social) are major moderators of algorithm preferences. The manuscript's choice of an objective, intellective prediction task with genuine difficulty puts the design in the part of the parameter space where algorithm uptake should be relatively high — consistent with the observed ~50% rates.

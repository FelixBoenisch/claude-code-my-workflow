# Pezzo & Pezzo (2006) — *Physician Evaluation After Medical Errors*

## Research question

When physicians use computerised decision aids, **does this affect lay perceptions of their competence, negligence, and likely liability after a medical error?** Does using such an aid help, hurt, or neither?

## Methods

Two vignette experiments with US students:

**Experiment 1**: 59 undergraduates read a scenario where a doctor either used a decision aid or did not, and either made a correct or incorrect diagnosis (2 × 2 between-subject). Subjects rate doctor quality and willingness to recommend; in the negative-outcome condition, also negligence and likelihood of suing.

**Experiment 2**: 166 medical students + 154 undergraduates read negative-outcome scenarios with four conditions: doctor (a) agreed with the aid, (b) heeded the aid against own opinion, (c) defied the aid in favour of own opinion, or (d) didn't use an aid. Subjects rate doctor fault, competence, and appropriateness of aid use in medicine.

## Key results

**Experiment 1** — using a decision aid:
- *Positive outcome*: makes the doctor *less* favourably perceived (perhaps because credit shifts to the aid).
- *Negative outcome*: makes the doctor *less* negatively perceived (fault shifts to the aid).
- Net effect: decision aids attenuate evaluations in both directions.

**Experiment 2** — agreeing vs defying the aid:
- **Agreeing with / heeding the aid** is associated with **reduced fault**.
- **Defying the aid** (going against its advice) is associated with **about the same fault as not using one at all**.
- Medical students were less harsh than undergraduates but accurately predicted undergraduate's responses.

## Main takeaways

1. **Computer decision aids attenuate physician evaluations in both directions** — less credit for good outcomes, less blame for bad ones. This is the **credit-blame symmetry** Coffman 2011 also documents for intermediation more broadly.
2. **Agreeing with the aid is liability-protective**; defying it is not.
3. **Using an aid and rejecting its advice gives no liability benefit** — for hindsight-based evaluation, what matters is whether the decision tracks the aid's recommendation.
4. This is an early (2006) empirical result that has influenced the field's framing of AI-as-blame-shield.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Pezzo & Pezzo is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 25: "Pezzo & Pezzo (2006) find similarly that observers attribute less fault to a doctor who follows a (bad) recommendation from an automated decision aid."

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Early documentation of the AI-as-blame-shield effect** — and its symmetric counterpart (AI as credit-shield). Pezzo & Pezzo's symmetric finding (less credit for good outcomes, less blame for bad ones) is closer to Coffman 2011 than to Steffel 2016's asymmetric blame-only result. The manuscript uses asymmetric (punishment-only) framing motivated by Steffel; Pezzo & Pezzo provides a counter-anchor that the manuscript could acknowledge.

**(2) Pezzo & Pezzo's "agreeing vs defying" structure parallels Tobia's standard/nonstandard structure.** Both papers find that *accepting* AI advice is protective (in vignettes), while *rejecting* it is not. The manuscript's design doesn't have an "advice" structure — Player A either delegates fully or decides herself. So Pezzo & Pezzo's specific result doesn't translate one-to-one.

**(3) Methodological note: this is undergraduate vignette work from 2006.** The manuscript's reliance on more recent and more incentivised work (Feier, Chevrier, Tontrup) is appropriate. Pezzo & Pezzo is a useful historical anchor — it's where this question was first asked in the medical context.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Paragraph 25: correctly cites it as part of the early evidence that "observers attribute less fault to a doctor who follows a (bad) recommendation from an automated decision aid."

**Worth flagging:** Pezzo & Pezzo's symmetric credit-blame attenuation effect runs against the manuscript's punishment-only design choice. If the AI shield operates on both positive and negative outcomes (as in Pezzo & Pezzo and Coffman), then focusing only on punishment misses half the picture. The manuscript could acknowledge this as a measurement limitation: "We focus on punishment for negative outcomes; Pezzo & Pezzo (2006) and Coffman (2011) suggest that algorithmic delegation may also attenuate reward for positive outcomes, an avenue we leave to future work."

**Limitation:** Small sample, dated, vignette-based. The Pezzo & Pezzo result is most useful as historical anchor and as a counter-point to the Steffel asymmetric framing.

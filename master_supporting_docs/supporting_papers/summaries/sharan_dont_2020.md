# Sharan & Romano (2020) — *The effects of personality and locus of control on trust in humans versus AI*

## Research question

How do **personality traits** (Big Five) and **locus of control (LOC)** affect trust in AI-based vs human advice? Do these traits explain heterogeneity in algorithm preferences that simpler measures miss?

## Methods

- **Online experiment**, *N* = 171.
- **Task**: card game where subjects decide whether the final covered card has a higher/lower number than the second-to-last card, over 10 trials.
- **Three between-subject conditions**: no suggestion (control), recommendations from "previous participants" (humans), recommendations from "AI-based algorithm" (AI).
- Trust measured via: response time, concordance (how often subject's choice matches the suggestion), and self-reported trust ratings.
- Big Five Inventory (BFI) and LOC scales administered.

## Key results

- **People chose AI suggestions more than human suggestions** — algorithm appreciation in this domain.
- **Self-reported trust in AI was higher** than trust in humans.
- **Locus of control negatively predicts trust concordance** (higher internal LOC → less likely to follow either source).
- **Neuroticism negatively predicts trust ratings**.
- **Openness predicts reaction time** for human suggestions only.
- LOC accounts for significant variance beyond Big Five traits.

## Main takeaways

1. **AI is preferred to "previous participants" in this card-game task** — supports algorithm appreciation.
2. **Locus of control is a key individual-difference moderator** — internal LOC reduces willingness to take any advice.
3. **Big Five traits have nuanced effects**: neuroticism reduces trust; openness affects reaction time.
4. **Implications for algorithm-aversion research**: individual differences matter; aggregating across personality can mask effects.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Personality moderators are an unexplored dimension for the manuscript.** The manuscript currently measures risk preferences (Holt-Laury) and self-assessed performance but not personality. Sharan & Romano shows that LOC and Big Five traits significantly moderate algorithm trust. The manuscript could add a personality battery as a robustness check or for future work.

**(2) LOC connects to the M3 (process ownership) framing.** Internal LOC subjects prefer to retain control; this is precisely the M3 mechanism. The Sharan & Romano result that internal-LOC subjects are less likely to follow any advice (human or AI) supports the broader autonomy-preference / process-ownership reading.

**(3) Algorithm appreciation in the card-game task** is consistent with the manuscript's relatively high delegation rates. People are not uniformly averse to algorithms in objective tasks.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Paragraph 19 cites correctly.

**Limitation:** Heliyon is a lower-impact journal; the methodology is fine but the sample size is small (*n* = 171). The personality-moderator finding is suggestive but not definitive. The manuscript should cite it for the conceptual point (personality matters) rather than rely on it for quantitative claims.

**Worth borrowing for future work:** A LOC measure would be a small addition with potential payoff. If the manuscript's M3 reading is correct (people resist algorithmic delegation when accountability is salient because they want to retain process ownership), then internal-LOC subjects should *especially* drive the H1 reversal. This would be a clean prediction worth testing.

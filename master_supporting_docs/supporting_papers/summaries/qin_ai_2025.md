# Qin et al. (2025, *Psychological Bulletin*) — *AI Aversion or Appreciation? A Capability–Personalization Framework and a Meta-Analytic Review*

## Research question

The empirical literature on attitudes toward AI exhibits two apparently contradictory streams: studies documenting AI aversion (people prefer humans over AI; \citet{dietvorst_algorithm_2015}, Bigman & Gray 2018) and studies documenting AI appreciation (people prefer AI over humans; \citet{logg_algorithm_2019}). The paper's central question: in what contexts does each pattern occur, and what is the size of the average effect across the literature?

## Methods

**Theoretical contribution**: the authors propose a **Capability–Personalization Framework**. Two dimensions determine whether people show AI aversion or AI appreciation in a given decision context:

1. **Perceived capability of AI**: is AI seen as more capable than humans in this context?
2. **Perceived necessity for personalization**: how much does the decision context require considering the individual's unique characteristics, circumstances, and needs?

The framework yields four quadrants:
- **AI appreciation (Quadrant I)**: AI perceived as more capable AND personalization perceived as unnecessary.
- **AI aversion (Quadrants II, III, IV)**: any other configuration.

**Empirical contribution**: a meta-analysis of **442 effect sizes from 163 studies** (combined N = 82,078) testing the framework's predictions.

## Key results

**Result 1 — Quadrant I produces AI appreciation; other quadrants produce aversion.**
- When AI is perceived as more capable than humans AND personalization is perceived as unnecessary: appreciation occurs with d = 0.27 (95% CI [0.17, 0.37]).
- Otherwise: aversion occurs with d = –0.50 (95% CI [–0.63, –0.37]).

**Result 2 — Moderation patterns.**
- AI appreciation is more pronounced for **tangible robots** than intangible algorithms.
- AI appreciation is more pronounced for **attitudinal** (vs. behavioural) outcome measures.
- AI appreciation is more pronounced in **between-subjects** (vs. within-subjects) study designs.
- AI appreciation is more pronounced in **low-unemployment countries**.
- AI aversion is more pronounced in **countries with high levels of education and internet use**.

**Result 3 — Moral-domain decisions sit in aversion territory.**
For decisions involving morality, emotion, or creativity, AI is perceived as less capable than humans (Castelo et al. 2019; Bigman & Gray 2018) — placing them in aversion-predicting quadrants.

## Main takeaways

1. **The aversion/appreciation literature is reconcilable.** The Capability–Personalization Framework predicts which direction the effect runs based on context-level features.
2. **Quantitative anchor**: the average aversion effect (d ≈ –0.5) and appreciation effect (d ≈ 0.27) provide effect-size benchmarks the field had previously lacked.
3. **Moral domains belong in aversion quadrants** because AI is perceived as less capable in moral contexts and because personalization is often perceived as necessary in moral decisions.
4. **Methodological contributions**: the framework provides a 2×2 typology for designing future experiments and a meta-analytic effect-size benchmark.
5. **Country-level moderators** (unemployment, education, internet use) introduce cross-national heterogeneity that prior reviews did not document.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Most current quantitative anchor for the algorithm-preference literature.**
The manuscript currently cites three systematic reviews (Burton 2020; Mahmud 2022; Chugunova & Sele 2022), all 2020–2022 and largely qualitative. Qin et al. 2025 is the first quantitative meta-analysis in the literature and is published in *Psychological Bulletin*, the highest-citation review venue in psychology. It is the cleanest replacement or supplement for the existing systematic-reviews sentence in subsection 2.

**(2) Places moral-domain aversion in the Capability–Personalization framework.**
The manuscript's setting (prediction task affecting Player B's payoff) sits in the *moral-domain* corner of the aversion literature. Qin et al. show that moral domains predictably produce aversion because (a) AI is seen as less capable in moral contexts and (b) personalization is often perceived as necessary. This provides a theoretical anchor for why the manuscript's setting plausibly triggers algorithm aversion in the baseline.

**(3) Effect-size benchmark for the manuscript's own findings.**
The manuscript's delegation gap (Punishment 42.5% vs. No-Punishment 59.3%) translates to a substantial effect size. Qin et al.'s meta-analytic d ≈ –0.5 for aversion settings provides a benchmark for whether the manuscript's effect size is consistent with the broader literature.

**(4) Country-level moderation is an important reminder.**
The manuscript's data are from UK Prolific subjects. Qin et al. document cross-national heterogeneity in aversion. The manuscript's results may be country-specific in ways that the lit review should at least acknowledge.

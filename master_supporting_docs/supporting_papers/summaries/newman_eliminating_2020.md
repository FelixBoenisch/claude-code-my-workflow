# Newman, Fast & Harmon (2020) — *When eliminating bias isn't fair*

## Research question

HR algorithms can eliminate human bias — but do those *subject to* algorithmic decisions perceive the process as fair? The authors propose that even when algorithms make accurate, bias-free decisions, **subjects perceive algorithmic decisions as procedurally less fair** because they're seen as **reductionistic** — i.e., as failing to take into account qualitative or contextual information.

## Methods

- **Four laboratory experiments** (*N* = 798).
- **One large-scale randomised experiment in an organisational setting** (*N* = 1,654).
- Subjects evaluate HR decisions (hiring, firing, promotion) made by either humans or algorithms.
- Key dependent variable: perceived procedural fairness.

## Key results

- **Algorithmic HR decisions are perceived as less procedurally fair** than identical human decisions.
- The mechanism is **algorithmic reductionism**: subjects believe algorithms "quantify and decontextualise" the qualitative features of performance.
- The effect persists when subjects know outcomes are identical.
- The effect generalises to a real organisational setting.

## Main takeaways

1. **Procedural fairness is a casualty of algorithmic decision-making**, even when outcomes improve.
2. **Algorithmic reductionism** is the key mechanism: people perceive algorithms as ignoring context.
3. **Real-world replication** in an organisation strengthens external validity.
4. **Policy implication**: deploying algorithmic HR may improve outcomes but damage perceived legitimacy.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Newman et al. is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 21: "extend the picture to the human-resources domain, showing that algorithmic HR decisions are perceived as less fair than identical human decisions because of 'algorithmic reductionism' — the perception that algorithms quantify and decontextualise the qualitative features of performance."

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Provides one mechanism candidate for moral-domain algorithm aversion**: reductionism. The manuscript's algorithm doesn't have the "decontextualisation" problem in the same way — it's a Bernoulli draw at A's empirical accuracy, not a complex model that ignores qualitative inputs. The reductionism channel is therefore *closed* by the manuscript's design. Whatever depresses delegation in the manuscript is not Newman et al.'s reductionism mechanism.

**(2) Procedural fairness is a relevant concept.** The manuscript could engage with procedural fairness as a candidate moderator. Player B's costly punishment is essentially a procedural-fairness reaction to the prediction process. The manuscript could note: "Newman et al. (2020) identify procedural fairness as the key dimension along which algorithmic HR decisions are judged less favourably than human ones. Our design rules out the reductionism mechanism by construction (the algorithm is a transparent Bernoulli draw with no contextual ignoring), but procedural fairness may operate through other channels — e.g., the *appropriateness* of routing morally consequential decisions through any algorithm at all."

**(3) The Newman et al. mechanism is *receiver-side*; the manuscript's M3 is *principal-side*.** Both are about how the moral content of a decision is processed; they're complementary.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Paragraph 21 cites correctly with appropriate framing.

**Worth noting:** Newman et al. is one of the strongest *external-validity* findings in the algorithm-aversion literature — large randomised experiment in a real organisation. The manuscript's design is more abstract; Newman et al. provides field-level corroboration of the moral-domain aversion picture.

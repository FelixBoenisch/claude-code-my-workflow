# Tontrup & Sprigman (2025 WP) — *Strategic Delegation of Moral Decisions to AI*

## Research question

Two questions:
1. **Do people delegate self-interested decisions to AI to reduce their moral responsibility**, in the same way prior literature has shown they delegate to human agents (B&F 2012, Hamman et al. 2010, Oexl & Grossman 2013, Hill 2015)?
2. **Is the AI seen as a moral entity that can absorb responsibility**, or merely as a piece of software (in which case the responsibility offload is just a social-distance effect)?

The authors frame this as their broader **Behavioral Self-Management (BSM)** theory: delegation is one of a family of strategies people use to redesign their decision environment to mute moral-image costs.

## Methods

- **Lab experiment**, NYU CESS / Stern Center for Behavioral Research, in-person. Used real ChatGPT in one treatment.
- **Game**: Dictator game with $10 endowment between Allocator (the subject) and Recipient.
- **Two between-subject treatments:**
  - **AI Treatment**: Allocator can either transfer money to the Recipient directly via a fixed protocol, or delegate part of the transfer to **ChatGPT**, which sends a payment code to the Recipient. ChatGPT does *not* recommend an amount — it merely executes a scripted protocol that participants have explained to them. The AI is the "final causal actor" because only it knows the payment code mapping.
  - **LimeSurvey (control)**: same protocol, but the "agent" is non-agentive deterministic software. This **controls for social-distance** effects (whatever distance is generated by not physically transferring is held constant).
- **Two within-subject games** (strategy-method design, one randomly paid):
  - **Self-Image Game**: Allocator's choice is unobserved by anyone. Only self-image is at stake.
  - **Social-Image Game**: Allocator's choice is observed by a third party. Both self-image and social-image are at stake.
- **Cost of delegation**: extra completion time only (no monetary fee).
- **Questionnaires** elicit:
  - Responsibility attribution (to self, to AI, to LimeSurvey)
  - Norm expectations (descriptive and injunctive)
  - **Social Value Orientation (SVO)** — classifying subjects as prosocial vs proself
- **Separate observer sample** rates responsibility attributions for the Allocators they observe.

## Key results

**Result 1 — Allocators choose to delegate to AI even at a time cost.**

- **35% of Allocators in the AI Treatment chose to delegate** (38% in Self-Image Game, 32% in Social-Image Game).
- In the LimeSurvey control, only ~10% delegate. **AI attracts ~3x more delegation than non-agentive software.**

**Result 2 — Delegation reduces transfers (Allocators get away with more for themselves).**

- Transfers in the AI Treatment are **significantly lower** than in LimeSurvey control.
- Within the AI Treatment, **delegators give less than non-delegators**.
- The differential transfer rate exceeds what could be attributed to social distance alone (since LimeSurvey holds that constant), so the AI specifically is doing the responsibility-offload work.

**Result 3 — Allocators (and Observers) attribute responsibility to the AI but not to LimeSurvey.**

- AI responsibility attribution by Allocators: 27.6% among delegators, 9.5% among non-delegators. LimeSurvey: 5.6% overall.
- Observers independently make the same attribution: AI is seen as bearing responsibility; LimeSurvey is not.
- This validates the "moral entity" reading rather than the "mere social distance" reading.

**Result 4 — Prosocial subjects delegate more.**

- The BSM theory predicts that those with the highest baseline moral cost from selfish behaviour have the most to gain by delegating. The data confirm this: prosocial SVO types are *more* likely to involve AI, not less.
- "Allocators who attributed more responsibility to AI had the opportunity to benefit from delegation and were also more likely to delegate."

## Main takeaways

1. **AI is perceived as a moral entity that can carry responsibility.** This is not just social distance — it's a genuine attribution.
2. **Delegating to AI works as a moral wiggle-room mechanism** — both for self-image (private decisions) and for social-image (observed decisions). Observers cooperate by attributing responsibility to the AI.
3. **Prosocial people delegate more** — the inverse of what one might naively expect, but exactly what BSM theory predicts. People with high baseline moral standards have the most to gain by offloading.
4. **The effect persists even with a costly delegation option** (added completion time). Delegation is sufficiently valuable that subjects pay for it.
5. **Policy implication:** "AI systems provide human actors with an easily accessible, low-cost, and hard-to-monitor means of offloading personal moral responsibility." Current AI regulation (EU AI Act) focuses on AI output safety; this paper argues regulation should also address AI's *behavioural* effects on humans.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Tontrup & Sprigman is the **closest 2025 sibling** to your paper. Same intellectual lineage (B&F, Hamman, Oexl), same central question (does AI delegation reduce moral cost?), same dictator-game-adjacent paradigm — but with **opposite results** along the principal-side dimension.

**The contrasting result:**

- **Tontrup**: 35% delegate to AI (vs 10% to non-agentive software); delegators transfer less. AI delegation works as moral cover.
- **This manuscript**: Delegation drops from 59% (No-Punishment) to 42% (Punishment); delegators are not punished less than non-delegators. AI delegation does *not* work as moral cover.

Both papers are competently executed and pre-registered, so the contrast is real and informative. The natural question is: **what design feature reverses the result?**

**Design comparison:**

| Feature | Tontrup & Sprigman (2025) | This manuscript |
|---|---|---|
| Task | Dictator game; Allocator splits $10 between self and Recipient | Prediction task; Player A predicts weight; Player B's payoff depends on the prediction |
| Allocator self-interest | **Strong** — keeping more directly raises own payoff | **Absent** — Player A's payoff doesn't depend on prediction outcome |
| What delegation does | Outsources the *transfer execution* (not the substantive choice); AI runs a scripted protocol | Outsources the *substantive decision* (the prediction itself) |
| Algorithm's role | "Final causal actor" — knows the payment code | Bernoulli draw at A's empirical accuracy |
| Algorithm calibration | None — ChatGPT just executes the protocol | Individual-level Bernoulli at A's accuracy; common knowledge |
| Punishment | None — moral cost is internalised via self-image / social-image | Real costly punishment by Player B (or no punishment in control) |
| Observer pool | Separate observers rate Allocators' responsibility attribution | Player Bs are the observers and impose costly punishment |

**Why the results differ — three candidate explanations:**

**(a) Self-interest vs aligned incentives.** Tontrup's Allocator has a direct selfish interest in transferring less. This is the canonical "want to do something selfish but feel guilty" setup. The manuscript's Player A has *no* selfish interest in a bad prediction — bad outcomes only generate punishment. The moral-cost-reduction story requires moral cost to begin with. In your design, there's nothing selfish to do, so there's nothing to feel less guilty about.

**(b) Process delegation vs decision delegation.** Tontrup's AI doesn't decide the allocation; the Allocator still picks the amount. The AI just *executes the transfer* by sending the payment code. The Allocator delegates the "final causal step" while retaining the substantive choice. In the manuscript, delegation transfers the *substantive* decision (the prediction). This is a deeper kind of delegation — the principal really does give up control. M3 (process ownership) bites *harder* on this kind of delegation than on Tontrup's setup, and may be why your subjects pull back from delegation when accountability becomes salient.

**(c) Punishment vs self/social-image cost.** Tontrup uses no costly third-party punishment; the moral cost is purely self- and social-image. The manuscript uses real costly punishment by Player B. Player A in your design faces a *concrete monetary penalty* rather than an abstract image cost. This might be enough to change the channel by which the moral-cost-reduction motive operates — perhaps real monetary punishment doesn't admit the same plausible-deniability route as image cost.

**Where in literature.tex this paper should go:**

The bib entry was added in our recent lit review pass and is *not yet cited* in literature.tex. Recommended placement:

- **Paragraph 27** (responsibility-perceptions subsection): Add a sentence introducing Tontrup as the closest precedent that tests the moral-cost-reduction-via-AI hypothesis directly in an incentivised lab setting. Note the contrast with this manuscript's finding.
- **Paragraph 41** (reconciliation section, four-channel map): Tontrup is most naturally placed alongside Chevrier & Teixeira as papers that find responsibility-shifting effects in dictator-game settings, in contrast to this manuscript's finding in a prediction-task setting.
- **Mechanism discussion (section 4 / M3)**: Tontrup's finding that *prosocial* types delegate more is highly relevant to your M3 reading. Under M3 (process ownership / identity), one might predict either direction: prosocial types have more to lose from being seen as not engaging (so delegate less) or more to gain by offloading (so delegate more). Tontrup finds the latter; your manuscript could check heterogeneity by social orientation as a robustness check.

**What the manuscript could borrow:**

- Tontrup's framing of "BSM" (Behavioral Self-Management) as a family of strategies is a useful conceptual umbrella for the responsibility-shifting literature. The manuscript currently uses "responsibility-shifting motive" — BSM would situate it inside a broader theoretical frame.
- The SVO classification is a clean way to test heterogeneity. The manuscript has elicited belief and performance measures but not SVO. If the heterogeneity-by-performance pattern in your data (actual performance loads negatively under attention restriction) has a prosocial counterpart, that would be valuable.
- Tontrup's clean separation of self-image vs social-image via the two-game design is elegant. Your manuscript's separation of delegator and evaluator pools achieves something similar — Player A's decision is made unobserved by Player B at the moment of choice, so self-image dominates; Player B's punishment is the social-image proxy.

**What Tontrup misses that the manuscript captures:**

- The punishment-on/off manipulation. Tontrup has no equivalent of your H1 test — the moral cost in their design is always present (as image cost).
- The individual-level calibration of the algorithm. Tontrup's AI is just a scripted execution; the responsibility attribution they measure is about *who knows the code*, not about *who is competent at the task*. The manuscript's calibration is specifically designed to strip the signal-extraction confound that Tontrup's design doesn't even try to address.

**Most important takeaway for the manuscript's framing:**

Tontrup's headline finding — "delegating to AI reduces moral cost" — is now widely cited (Köbis et al. 2025 in Nature builds on the same theoretical premise). The manuscript's H1 reversal is therefore a **direct counter-result** to a small but emerging consensus. The discussion section should engage with this honestly: Tontrup's result is real, this manuscript's result is real, and the contrast is informative about *when* AI delegation reduces moral cost and *when* it doesn't. The answer seems to be: AI delegation reduces moral cost when there is a self-interested action with moral content (Tontrup, Köbis), but not when the underlying decision is uncertain and aligned in incentives (this manuscript).

**Suggested addition to the introduction:** A sentence like "Recent work (Tontrup & Sprigman 2025; Köbis et al. 2025) finds that AI delegation does reduce moral cost in classical selfish-action settings; we ask whether this generalises to settings where the underlying decision is itself morally neutral but produces externalities under uncertainty — a description that fits most policy-relevant applications of AI (medical diagnosis, judicial decision support, financial forecasting)."

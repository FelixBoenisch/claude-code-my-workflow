# Tsumura & Yamada (2026) — *Effects of knowledge and importance on responsibility in human-AI decision making*

## Research question

How do **prior knowledge of an AI agent** and **perceived task importance** *jointly* shape responsibility attribution in human-AI decision making, after failed agent-assisted interactions? Prior literature has focused on system autonomy, transparency, or anthropomorphism — Tsumura & Yamada add cognitive framing and contextual framing as moderators.

## Methods

- **Three-factor mixed-design experiment**.
- ***N* = 588 participants** (online).
- Subjects observe failed agent-assisted interactions and rate responsibility for:
  - The user
  - The agent
  - The agent's developer or provider
- Factors:
  - Prior knowledge of the agent (cognitive framing).
  - Perceived task importance (contextual framing).
  - Outcome characteristics (failure).

## Key results

- **Prior knowledge of the agent shifts responsibility *away from* the user and *toward* the agent and its developer**.
- **High task importance increases responsibility attributed to the developer or provider**, substantially.
- Responsibility attribution is **dynamic** — it depends on user expectations and situational seriousness, not just system properties.

## Main takeaways

1. **User-level cognitive framing matters**: if users know about the agent in advance, they shift blame to it.
2. **Task importance shifts blame to developers/providers** — high-stakes tasks elevate developer responsibility.
3. **Responsibility is not solely a function of system design** — user and contextual factors are co-determinants.
4. **Implications for design and law**: trust-calibration interventions and developer accountability frameworks should account for these moderators.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Tsumura & Yamada is **not yet cited** in literature.tex but was added to the bib in the recent lit-review pass.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Recent third-party responsibility-attribution result.** The manuscript's H2 asks about third-party (Player B) punishment of the principal under different delegation conditions. Tsumura & Yamada's finding — that prior knowledge of the agent shifts blame away from the user — would predict a *significant* differential punishment in the manuscript's design (Player B has full knowledge of the algorithm's properties). The manuscript's H2 null is therefore in tension with Tsumura & Yamada's prediction.

**(2) Methodological contrast**: Tsumura & Yamada use vignettes and stated responsibility ratings; the manuscript uses incentivised costly punishment. As with other vignette-vs-lab comparisons in this literature, vignette studies tend to find stronger attribution asymmetries than lab studies.

**(3) Task importance moderator.** The manuscript's punishment manipulation can be interpreted as varying salience/importance of accountability. Tsumura & Yamada's high-importance condition is the conceptual counterpart. Both papers find that high-importance / high-accountability conditions matter — but the manuscript shows a behavioural effect (principals retain authority), while Tsumura & Yamada show a third-party-perception effect (developers held more responsible).

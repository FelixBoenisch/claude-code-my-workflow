# Litterscheidt & Streich (2020) — *Financial Education and Digital Asset Management*

## Research question

How does **financial education** affect investors' propensity to **delegate financial decisions to a robo-advisor**? Does providing detailed information about the algorithm increase delegation, and does it affect independent investment behaviour?

## Methods

- **Online experiment**, German university subject pool.
- Subjects assigned to financial-education intervention or control.
- Make investment decisions either alone or with the option to delegate to a robo-advisor.
- Measures: delegation rate, diversification, self-reported and revealed risk aversion.

## Key results

- **Financial education increases delegation to the robo-advisor.** Subjects who receive detailed information about the algorithm's principles delegate more.
- **Education also affects independent investment behaviour**: increases diversification and revealed risk aversion.
- **Counterintuitively, education decreases self-reported risk aversion** while increasing revealed risk aversion. Subjects know more about risk and consequently take less of it, but report themselves as less risk averse.

## Main takeaways

1. **Financial education is a delegation lever** — it raises algorithm uptake.
2. **Education has dual effects**: it both makes subjects more confident about delegating and changes their independent behaviour.
3. **Algorithm transparency that's *informationally meaningful* (not just procedurally transparent)** increases use.
4. **Policy implication**: robo-advisor adoption benefits from investor education programs.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Litterscheidt & Streich is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 19.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) "Transparency increases delegation" finding parallels the manuscript's design choice.** The manuscript provides maximal transparency about the algorithm (Bernoulli at A's accuracy, common knowledge). Litterscheidt & Streich's finding that informational transparency increases use is consistent with the manuscript's ~50% delegation rates — substantially higher than studies where the algorithm is more opaque (e.g., Goldbach 2019's 10%).

**(2) Contrast with Dargnies et al. 2026.** Dargnies et al. find that transparency *doesn't* help in hiring. Litterscheidt & Streich find it helps in finance. The contrast suggests transparency effects are domain-specific. Worth flagging in the manuscript: "Transparency effects on algorithm uptake vary across domains (cf. Litterscheidt & Streich 2020 vs Dargnies et al. 2026); the manuscript's design provides maximally informative transparency and observes moderate delegation rates, consistent with transparency helping but not eliminating aversion."

**(3) Domain difference.** Litterscheidt & Streich is finance-focused; the manuscript is a generic prediction task. Direct comparison is loose.

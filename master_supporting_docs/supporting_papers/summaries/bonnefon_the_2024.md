# Bonnefon, Rahwan & Shariff (2024, *Annual Review of Psychology*) — *The Moral Psychology of Artificial Intelligence*

## Research question

Comprehensive narrative review of the experimental literature on the moral psychology of human–AI interaction. Organising frame: intelligent machines can occupy three moral roles, and the authors review each.

1. **Moral agents** — machines making (or contributing to) moral decisions, with consequences for human patients. Self-driving cars, medical AI, autonomous weapons.
2. **Moral patients** — machines as targets of human decisions, with implications for human-machine cooperation.
3. **Moral proxies** — machines that humans send as their delegates in moral interactions, or use as a disguise. *This is the section directly relevant to the manuscript.*

## Methods

Narrative review (not a systematic review or meta-analysis). Coverage emphasises recent experimental findings (2018–2023) with attention to open questions. Authored by three of the most-cited researchers in the moral-machine space (Bonnefon: Moral Machine Experiment; Rahwan: MIT Media Lab; Shariff: Templeton-funded work on AI ethics).

## Key results (review-style summary)

**Machines as moral agents.** The "Moral Machine" line of work shows that people have systematic preferences over how autonomous vehicles should distribute risk (Awad et al. 2018). When machines are framed as making explicit moral judgments — rather than implementing implicit ones — algorithm aversion intensifies; people prefer human moral discretion (Bigman & Gray 2018; Jauernig et al. 2022).

**Machines as moral patients.** People show "machine penalty" — they cooperate less with machines than with humans in equivalent situations — but this penalty diminishes when (i) machines are anthropomorphised, (ii) machines display vulnerability or pain cues, or (iii) reciprocity is mutual.

**Machines as moral proxies (delegates).** The section closest to the manuscript:
- Documents the use of AI to delegate dishonest behavior (Köbis et al. 2021, "Bad machines corrupt good morals").
- Documents experimental evidence that delegators are punished less for outcomes when the delegate is a machine (citing Feier et al. 2022).
- Documents "machine masquerade" — humans presenting themselves as machines or vice versa — and the ethical implications.

The authors flag delegation to AI as an **emerging research frontier** with substantial open questions about (a) whether the responsibility-shifting motive that's been documented for human delegates extends to AI, (b) whether AI's lack of independent agency changes how third parties allocate blame, and (c) what design features of AI delegates affect both the delegator's and the third-party's moral psychology.

## Main takeaways

1. The moral psychology of AI is best decomposed into three roles (agent / patient / proxy), each with its own experimental literature.
2. The moral-agent literature is the largest and most cited; the moral-proxy literature is the youngest and the one most directly relevant to delegation/responsibility research.
3. Algorithm aversion in moral contexts is well-documented for AI-as-agent but the AI-as-proxy/delegate evidence is more mixed and recent.
4. Key open questions include how the responsibility-shifting motive from the Bartling-Fischbacher tradition extends to AI delegates, and how third parties allocate blame in human-AI delegation chains.
5. The authors situate this within broader policy and law conversations on AI accountability.

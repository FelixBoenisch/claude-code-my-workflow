# Jolly, Dunlop, Parker & Kanse (2025/2026) — *It's Not My Responsibility*

## Research question

When employees work with **algorithmic decision-making (ADM) technology** that restricts their autonomy, do they:
1. Engage in **higher rates of unethical pro-organizational behaviour**?
2. **Displace responsibility** for their unethical decisions onto an authority figure (which could be the technology itself or a human supervisor)?

The theoretical framework combines:
- **Job characteristics model** (Hackman & Oldham 1976) — job autonomy is a central feature of motivating work design.
- **Moral disengagement theory** (Bandura 1986) — specifically, displacement of responsibility.

## Methods

**Two experimental studies** with realistic work simulations:

**Study 1 (N = 276)**: 2-condition between-subject design — autonomy-affording ADM vs autonomy-restricting ADM. Measures: rate of unethical behaviour (specifically, unethical pro-organizational behaviour, where employees disadvantage customers to benefit the organisation).

**Study 2 (N = 256)**: Replicates Study 1 with a **novel cognitive measure of displacement of responsibility** — designed to capture the subjective experience of "this wasn't my call, the technology / my boss is responsible."

## Key results

**Study 1**: Participants working with **autonomy-restricting ADM** engage in significantly *higher rates of unethical behaviour* (disadvantaging customers) compared to those working with autonomy-affording ADM.

**Study 2**: Working with autonomy-restricting ADM produces:
- Lower self-reported personal responsibility for negative consequences of decisions.
- Displacement of responsibility onto an authority figure — which can be the technology itself or a human (e.g., a supervisor).
- The displacement is moderated by the autonomy structure: autonomy-restricting ADM creates the cognitive conditions for displacement; autonomy-affording ADM does not.

## Main takeaways

1. **Restrictive ADM increases unethical behaviour** in organisational simulations.
2. **The mechanism is moral disengagement via displacement of responsibility.** Workers shift the blame from themselves to the technology or to a human authority.
3. **Job autonomy is a key moderator** — designing autonomous work, even when AI is involved, can prevent moral disengagement.
4. **The technology can be the "authority figure"** to which responsibility is displaced — algorithms function like a supervisor in this regard.
5. **Policy implication**: work design (specifically autonomy preservation) is an underused lever for preventing AI-induced ethical lapses.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Jolly et al. is **not yet cited** in literature.tex but was added to the bib in the recent lit-review pass.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Direct test of displacement-of-responsibility channel in algorithmic contexts.** The manuscript's H1 reversal (delegation drops when punishment is salient) is in tension with the standard responsibility-displacement story. Jolly et al. provides clean evidence that the displacement story is *real* in workplace contexts — workers genuinely feel less responsible and behave more unethically when ADM restricts their autonomy. This is the *opposite-direction* result from the manuscript: where Jolly et al. find autonomy-restriction → more unethical behaviour, the manuscript finds delegation-availability → less delegation under punishment.

How to reconcile:
- Jolly et al.'s subjects are *not given a choice* about whether ADM restricts their autonomy — they're assigned to the condition.
- The manuscript's subjects *choose* whether to delegate. They actively *pull back* from autonomy-restriction under salient accountability.

So both findings can be true: when restriction is *imposed*, unethical behaviour rises through displacement (Jolly et al.); when restriction is *available* but optional, principals decline to take it up when accountability is salient (manuscript). The two results jointly characterise the responsibility-displacement channel: it's strong when imposed but weak when freely chosen under accountability.

**(2) Strong validation of the "responsibility displacement" concept.** Jolly et al. provides a rigorous experimental measurement of displacement of responsibility — a concept used throughout the manuscript's literature review (literature.tex ¶7, 9, 13, 27, 33). Citing Jolly et al. as a recent demonstration that the concept is real and measurable strengthens the manuscript's theoretical framing.

**(3) Connects to the moral disengagement (Bandura) tradition.** The manuscript's M3 mechanism is grounded in identity / process ownership (Bénabou-Tirole). Jolly et al. grounds the same logic in moral disengagement (Bandura). These two theoretical traditions are complementary — both predict that the moral salience of an action shapes whether the agent owns or distances themselves from it. Citing Jolly et al. adds breadth to the theoretical foundation.

**(4) Algorithmic delegation specifically can serve as the "figure of authority."** Jolly et al. show that workers displace responsibility *onto the technology itself*, not just onto human supervisors. This is the strongest direct evidence that algorithms can function as a "moral wiggle room" device in the workplace.

(2025) provide a moral-disengagement-theory mechanism for how algorithmic delegation can erode personal responsibility; our M3 mechanism provides an identity-management explanation for why principals *resist* this erosion when accountability is salient."

**Methodological note:** Jolly et al. uses simulated work scenarios with measurable ethical behavior (e.g., disadvantaging customers). This is more applied than the manuscript's abstract prediction task. The convergence of effects across very different operationalisations is reassuring for both papers.

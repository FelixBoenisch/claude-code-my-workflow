# Weaver (1986) — *The Politics of Blame Avoidance*

## Research question

Are politicians primarily motivated by **credit-claiming** (the dominant assumption in political science circa 1986, following Fiorina, Mayhew) or by **blame-avoidance**? Weaver argues the latter — driven by voters' **negativity bias** (greater sensitivity to losses than gains) — and traces the implications for political strategy and policy outcomes.

## Methods

Theoretical / political-science essay. Uses examples from US federal policy (indexation of benefits, civil service reform, formula grants, Gramm-Rudman-Hollings budget process) to illustrate the prevalence of *automaticity* / discretion-limiting devices as evidence of blame-avoidance motivation.

## Key arguments

1. **Voters have negativity bias** — they respond more strongly to bad than good outcomes.
2. **Politicians anticipate this** and prefer to avoid blame rather than maximise credit-claiming.
3. **Several strategies emerge**:
   - *Agenda limitation*: avoid raising controversial issues.
   - *Scapegoating*: deflect blame to others (often agencies, foreign actors, predecessors).
   - *"Passing the buck"*: delegate decisions to commissions, bureaucracies, automatic formulas.
   - *Defection / jumping on the bandwagon*: switch sides on issues approaching a tipping point.
4. **Automaticity is the institutional manifestation** — politicians create rules that remove their own discretion, sacrificing credit-claiming opportunities to gain blame protection.
5. **The credit-blame asymmetry has policy effects**: politicians surrender discretion even when it offers credit-claiming opportunities.

## Main takeaways

1. **Blame-avoidance is a stronger motivation than credit-claiming** in politics. This reverses the conventional wisdom.
2. **The credit-blame asymmetry is rooted in voters' negativity bias** — a robust psychological pattern.
3. **Institutional design responds to this asymmetry** — automaticity, indexation, formula-driven policies.
4. **Foundational result** for the political-science literature on blame politics; widely cited.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Weaver is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 7: "The political-science and public-choice literatures have long recognized that delegation can serve as a strategy for shifting blame onto an intermediary (Weaver 1986; Fiorina 1986)."

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Foundational anchor for the blame-avoidance framing.** The manuscript's responsibility-shifting motivation has its political-science roots in Weaver. The 1986 paper provides the conceptual framework that experimental economists (Bartling, Coffman, Oexl, Hamman) later operationalised in lab settings.

**(2) The "automaticity" argument is uncannily relevant to algorithmic delegation.** Weaver's discussion of *automatic* discretion-limiting devices (indexation, formulas, civil service rules) is the political analogue of contemporary algorithmic delegation. Algorithms are the modern version of these blame-shielding institutional devices. The manuscript could draw the analogy explicitly.

**(3) The credit-blame asymmetry connects to Steffel et al. and Bénabou-Tirole.** Weaver's psychological foundation (negativity bias) is the political-science version of the same asymmetry that motivates the manuscript's choice to focus on punishment (rather than reward).

**(4) Policy framing for the manuscript's conclusion.** Weaver's argument that institutional design responds to blame-avoidance can be used in the policy discussion: AI delegation provides yet another institutional avenue for blame avoidance, and policy frameworks should anticipate this incentive structure.

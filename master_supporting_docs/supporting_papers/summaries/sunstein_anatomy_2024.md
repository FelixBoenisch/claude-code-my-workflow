# Sunstein & Gaffe (2024) — *An Anatomy of Algorithm Aversion*

## Research question

Why does algorithm aversion exist? The authors aim to provide a structured "anatomy" of the mechanisms behind people's preference for human over algorithmic forecasters and decision-makers, and to derive policy implications for federal agencies (DHS, IRS, criminal-justice bail decisions) that might wish to adopt algorithms.

## Methods

Narrative law-review essay. Surveys the experimental and behavioural literature on algorithm aversion. Not a systematic review or meta-analysis; selection is curatorial. The contribution is taxonomic: organising the diverse mechanisms identified in the literature into a coherent framework, with policy implications.

## Key results (taxonomy)

Sunstein & Gaffe identify five core mechanisms of algorithm aversion:

1. **Desire for agency / control.** People want to be the author of their own decisions; algorithms threaten this autonomy. (Cites Dietvorst, Logg, the locus-of-control literature.)
2. **Moral or emotional reaction to judgment by algorithms.** Particularly strong for decisions in the moral domain (e.g., parole, medical care, hiring). People feel that algorithmic judgment is dehumanising even when it is more accurate.
3. **Belief in unique human knowledge.** Experts (doctors, judges) are seen as possessing tacit, contextual, intuitive knowledge that algorithms lack.
4. **Ignorance about algorithms.** People do not understand how algorithms produce their outputs; opacity drives mistrust.
5. **Asymmetric forgiveness.** Larger negative reactions to algorithmic error than to equivalent human error (cites Dietvorst 2015 on the persistence of aversion after observing an algorithm err).

The article also distinguishes:
- **General comfort vs task-specific confidence** as separate dimensions: people may dislike algorithms in general but accept them for specific tasks (or vice versa).
- **Hypothetical vs real-world settings**: stated aversion may overstate behavioural aversion.

Policy implications: federal agencies adopting algorithms should communicate the comparative performance evidence, address agency-and-control concerns through "human in the loop" designs, and target the asymmetric-forgiveness bias through framing.

## Main takeaways

1. Algorithm aversion is *multiply caused*; no single mechanism accounts for all observed patterns.
2. The moral/emotional reaction (mechanism #2) is the channel most directly relevant to questions of responsibility and blame.
3. Asymmetric forgiveness (mechanism #5) is the channel most relevant for accountability-after-failure questions.
4. Policy implications: algorithm deployment in public-sector decisions will face systematic resistance whose specific form depends on which mechanism dominates.
5. The article is a legal-policy synthesis, not new empirical evidence.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Useful for the "mechanism is unsettled" framing in subsection 2 ¶28.**
The manuscript currently notes that "the mechanism, however, is unsettled: lack of trust, loss of perceived control, and anticipated reputational or moral costs have all been proposed." Sunstein & Gaffe's five-mechanism taxonomy is one of the most explicit recent articulations of this unsettledness; citing it adds legitimacy to the manuscript's claim that the mechanism question is open.

**(2) Cite for the policy relevance / motivation.**
The article's framing (federal agencies, criminal justice, bail decisions) provides motivation for why the responsibility-shifting-through-AI question matters for policy. If the manuscript wants a real-world hook in the introduction, this is a useful citation.

**(3) The asymmetric-forgiveness mechanism is conceptually close to the manuscript's signal-extraction reading of Feier.**
Sunstein & Gaffe's mechanism #5 (larger negative reaction to algorithmic error) is structurally adjacent to the signal-extraction reading of Feier (third parties extract a negative ability signal from delegation to an algorithm). Worth noting as a non-economics articulation of the same intuition.

**(4) Lower priority than Bonnefon, Rahwan & Shariff (2024).**
For the manuscript's bibliography, Bonnefon et al. 2024 (*Annual Review of Psychology*) is the more central recent review of the moral-domain subset. Sunstein & Gaffe is more useful as a *policy* or *legal* reference, complementing the European Union AI Act citation, than as a substantive review of the experimental literature. Cite both, in different paragraphs and for different work.

**Where to cite in literature.tex:**

- **Subsection 2 ¶28 (mechanism-unsettled sentence)**: append as a recent synthesis of the mechanism debate.
- **Introduction or Conclusion**: cite for the policy/legal-motivation stakes of the responsibility-shifting question.

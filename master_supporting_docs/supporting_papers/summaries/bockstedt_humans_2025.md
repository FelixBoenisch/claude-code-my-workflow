## Research question (based on abstract and references)

How does the **framing of a decision** (gain vs loss) affect humans' willingness to delegate to AI? Specifically, does **loss aversion** reverse the usual algorithm aversion when humans face decisions framed as potential losses rather than gains?

## Methods (inferred)

- Experimental studies in Management Science Special Issue tradition.
- Manipulate gain vs loss framing of decisions.
- Measure willingness to delegate to AI vs retain human decision-making.

## Key results (from prior search)

- **Loss-framing reverses the standard algorithm aversion pattern.** When tasks are framed as involving potential losses, the preference for human assistance over AI assistance disappears or reverses.
- Loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky) interacts with algorithm aversion: subjects who fear losses are more willing to delegate to AI to "outsource" the loss.
- The headline result: AI delegation rises substantially under loss framing.

## Main takeaways

1. **Algorithm aversion is framing-dependent**: gain-framed decisions show aversion; loss-framed decisions don't.
2. **Loss aversion can offset algorithm aversion**: under loss conditions, subjects are willing to use AI to share or shift the loss.
3. The paper extends the Castelo (objective/subjective) and Dietvorst (uncertainty) moderator literatures with a framing-based moderator.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Bockstedt & Buckman is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 19.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Loss framing in the manuscript's design.** The manuscript's punishment condition is essentially a loss frame — Player A faces potential loss of payoff. Bockstedt & Buckman predict that under loss framing, subjects should be *more* willing to delegate to AI. **The manuscript's H1 reversal contradicts this prediction**: under the loss-frame punishment condition, delegation *falls* (42% vs 59%). This is a substantive contrast.

**(2) Why might the manuscript's design produce the opposite of Bockstedt & Buckman?**
- Bockstedt & Buckman's loss framing is about the subject's *own* potential loss; the manuscript's punishment is loss inflicted by another party (Player B's costly punishment), making it about *accountability* rather than just *risk aversion*.
- The manuscript's design adds a third-party moral element (Player B's payoff also at stake) that Bockstedt & Buckman's design likely lacks.
- M3 (process ownership / accountability salience) is a different mechanism than loss aversion.

**(3) The contrast is worth flagging in the discussion.** "Bockstedt & Buckman (2025) document that loss framing increases willingness to delegate to AI; our manipulation (third-party costly punishment) operates on a different margin — accountability salience rather than risk aversion — and produces the opposite delegation pattern. The contrast suggests that loss aversion and accountability salience are distinct drivers of delegation decisions."

# Salatino, Prével, Caspar & Lo Bue (2025) — *Influence of AI behavior on human moral decisions, agency, and responsibility*

## Research question

Does the **"moral behavior" of an AI** (aggressive vs conservative recommendations) affect the **moral decisions, sense of agency, and felt responsibility** of human users in morally challenging situations?

The motivating context is **military decision-making** — specifically, drone-operator-style decisions under collateral-damage risk.

## Methods

- **Lab experiment**, Royal Military Academy, Brussels.
- **Subjects**: military cadets (specialised sample).
- **Task**: drone operator on a battlefield. Decide whether to initiate an attack based on the presence of enemies and risk of collateral damage.
- **Trial types**: Moral vs two No-Moral variants.
- **AI support conditions**: No-AI vs Aggressive-AI (recommends attack) vs Conservative-AI (recommends restraint).
- **Measures**:
  - **Implicit sense of agency** via Temporal Binding (TB) — the subjective time-compression between action and outcome.
  - **Explicit sense of agency** via direct self-report.
  - **Explicit responsibility** ratings.

## Key results

- **AI inputs influence subjects' moral decisions** — both aggressive and conservative AI recommendations shift decisions.
- **Implicit sense of agency increases** in morally challenging situations (more temporal binding).
- **Explicit responsibility decreases** during interaction with either AI (compared to no-AI baseline).

## Main takeaways

1. **AI behaviour shapes human moral decisions** in ethical contexts.
2. **Sense of agency is malleable** under AI assistance — both implicitly and explicitly.
3. **AI reduces felt responsibility** when present in the decision loop.
4. **Methodological contribution**: combining implicit (TB) and explicit measures of agency.
5. The military-cadet sample is unique and externally relevant for drone-operator and similar military AI applications.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Salatino et al. is **not yet cited** in literature.tex but was added to the bib in the recent lit-review pass.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Direct test of responsibility reduction under AI presence.** Salatino et al. find that *explicit* responsibility decreases under AI assistance. This is the standard responsibility-shifting result. The manuscript's H1 reversal — subjects pull back from algorithmic delegation under accountability salience — is the *opposite-direction* result. The contrast suggests:
- Salatino et al.'s subjects use AI when given the choice; they then feel less responsible. The responsibility-shifting motive operates *after* delegation is forced or accepted.
- The manuscript's subjects can *choose* whether to delegate. When accountability is salient, they choose *not* to delegate, *avoiding* the shifting before it occurs.

The two results are not contradictory. Salatino et al. shows what happens *if* you delegate; the manuscript shows what happens *when* you decide whether to delegate.

**(2) Military / drone-operator context.** This is a high-stakes, externally-relevant domain. The contrast with the manuscript's abstract prediction task is large. Comparing the magnitude of responsibility shifts across these very different domains provides external-validity context for the manuscript.

**(3) Methodological note on Temporal Binding.** The manuscript doesn't use TB or any implicit-agency measure. This is fine — the manuscript's outcome variables (delegation decision, punishment) are behavioural and directly relevant. But mentioning that *implicit* sense of agency is sensitive to AI presence (per Salatino et al.) adds depth to the discussion of why subjects might pull back from delegation in the manuscript's design.

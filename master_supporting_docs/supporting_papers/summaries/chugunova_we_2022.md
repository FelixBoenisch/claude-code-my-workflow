# Chugunova & Sele (2022) — *We and It: An interdisciplinary review*

## Type

**Interdisciplinary literature review** synthesising 136 experimental studies on how humans interact with automated agents.

**Note on PDF version**: The PDF in the folder is the MPI Working Paper No. 20-15 (preprint), not the final JBEE 2022 version. The bib correctly cites the JBEE article. Content is substantively the same; minor edits in the published version.

## Research question

How do people interact with automated agents that can take on different roles (advisor, principal, agent)? The paper synthesises evidence across disciplines (economics, psychology, computer science, management) to identify systematic patterns and reconcile seemingly inconsistent findings.

## Methods

- Systematic interdisciplinary search.
- 136 experimental studies on human-machine interaction.
- Thematic synthesis.

## Main findings

1. **People react to automated agents differently than to humans**: more rationally, less emotionally/socially.
2. **Task context matters**: people accept automated agents for some tasks (objective forecasting) more than others (moral decisions).
3. **Performance expectations matter**: gaps between expected and observed performance drive aversion.
4. **Distribution of decision authority matters**: people accept algorithmic *advice* but resist *full delegation*.
5. **General pattern**: people are willing to *over-rely* on algorithmic support but *averse to ceding* decision authority.

## Main takeaways

1. **The "two faces" of algorithm interaction**: appreciation as advisor, aversion as autonomous decision-maker.
2. **Task-context, performance, and authority are the three big moderators**.
3. **Research is fragmented across disciplines** — the review's contribution is integration.
4. **The willingness to "over-rely yet resist full delegation"** is a particularly informative pattern.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Chugunova & Sele is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 19 as one of the review references.

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) The "over-rely on advice but resist full delegation" pattern is informative.** The manuscript's setting is *full delegation* (Player A either delegates entirely or makes the prediction herself). By Chugunova & Sele's pattern, this is the regime where aversion should bite. The manuscript's ~50% delegation rates are therefore *higher than would be predicted* by a strict reading of their summary. Possible explanation: the manuscript's individual-level calibration of the algorithm makes "full delegation" psychologically closer to "advice-following" (since the algorithm is calibrated to A's own performance, delegating doesn't give up much).

**(2) Authoritative interdisciplinary synthesis.** A useful "for review, see" citation that compresses much of the literature into one reference.

**(3) Task-context moderator.** Chugunova & Sele's emphasis on task context aligns with the manuscript's focus on the moral-domain framing.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Paragraph 19 cites correctly as a review reference.

**Worth borrowing:** The "over-rely on advice but resist full delegation" framing is rhetorically powerful. The manuscript could frame the H1 reversal in these terms: "Chugunova & Sele (2022) identify a robust pattern across the literature — people accept algorithmic advice but resist ceding decision authority. Our H1 reversal sharpens this finding: even when delegation is to a *fully calibrated* algorithm, principals retain rather than cede authority when accountability becomes salient."

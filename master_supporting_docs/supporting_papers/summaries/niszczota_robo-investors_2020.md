# Niszczota & Kaszás (2020) — *Robo-investment aversion*

## Research question

Do people prefer investment decisions made by **human investment managers** rather than by **algorithms** ("robos")? And is this preference *amplified* in domains with moral content — specifically, when **morally controversial companies** are involved in the investment portfolio?

## Methods

Five experiments, *N* = 3,828. Pre-registered. Online studies.

- Subjects rate the permissibility of algorithmic vs human investment managers excluding (or including) morally controversial stocks (e.g., "sin stocks" — coal, tobacco, weapons).
- Studies 1–3 use hypothetical scenarios.
- Studies 4–5 use real choices (incentivised).

## Key results

- **Robo-investment aversion is robust and substantial** across studies (mean effect size *d* = −0.39).
- **Algorithmic exclusion of morally controversial stocks is rated less permissible** than the same exclusion by a human manager.
- The aversion persists even when subjects are told the exclusion is financially disadvantageous.
- In incentivised settings (Studies 4–5), the aversion is somewhat moderated but does not disappear.
- The aversion extends beyond controversial industries to non-controversial ones as well.

## Main takeaways

1. **Algorithm aversion extends to financial decision-making**, especially when moral considerations are involved.
2. **Even moral *exclusions* (a positive moral act) by algorithms are viewed as less permissible** than by humans.
3. **The effect is robust** to incentive structure and information about financial consequences.
4. **Policy implication**: integrating ethical/ESG considerations into algorithmic investment may face consumer resistance.

## Relation to "Algorithms and Responsibility" (this manuscript)

Niszczota & Kaszás is cited in [literature.tex](../../../manuscript/literature.tex) paragraph 21: "Niszczota & Kaszás (2020) document the same in a financial decision-making context."

**Why this paper matters for the manuscript:**

**(1) Moral-domain algorithm aversion in a high-stakes domain.** Niszczota & Kaszás document the *expected* pattern (algorithm aversion amplified in moral domain) in financial decisions involving real ethical content. The manuscript's design tests a related question — algorithmic delegation in a moral-domain prediction task — but finds *less* dramatic aversion (~50% delegation) than Niszczota's effect size would predict.

**(2) Possible explanation for the contrast.** Niszczota & Kaszás's moral content is about *who is invested in* (controversial industries); the manuscript's moral content is about *who bears the cost* (Player B). These are different kinds of moral domain. The investment-exclusion case has a direct moral act (the algorithm itself making a controversial choice); the manuscript's case is a stochastic prediction that incidentally has consequences for Player B.

**(3) Algorithmic delegation can be perceived as morally problematic even when the underlying decision is morally neutral.** Niszczota & Kaszás find aversion even for non-controversial stocks. This is consistent with a "moral discretion" or "process ownership" reading — people resist letting algorithms make moral calls *as such*, not just because they fear bad outcomes.

**Where in literature.tex:**

- Paragraph 21 cites correctly with appropriate framing.

**Worth borrowing:** Niszczota & Kaszás's framing of algorithm aversion as extending beyond competence concerns into a *moral mandate* question is rhetorically useful. The manuscript could note: "Niszczota & Kaszás (2020) document that even *morally positive* algorithmic decisions (excluding controversial industries) are viewed as less permissible than the same decisions made by humans. This suggests that algorithm aversion in the moral domain has a deeper root than mere outcome-based judgment — it concerns the *legitimacy* of algorithmic moral judgment itself."

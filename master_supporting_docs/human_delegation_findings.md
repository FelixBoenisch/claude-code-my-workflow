Curated extraction of passages reporting **findings on delegation between humans** —
the strategic/responsibility-shifting use of delegation where the intermediary is
another person (or a randomization device used as a comparison). Findings about
delegating to **algorithms / AI / machines** are deliberately excluded; those belong
to strands (ii) and (iii) of the literature section.

## A. Canonical dictator-game blame-shifting

### Bartling & Fischbacher (2012) — *Shifting the blame: on delegation and responsibility*
The canonical result, as restated by Argenton et al. (2023):
> "The effectiveness of blame-shifting is illustrated, inter alia, in economic experiments by Bartling and Fischbacher (2012). They show that **decision-makers can avoid being punished for an unfair decision by delegating the decision to another person**."

On the random-device comparison (Argenton et al. 2023, paraphrasing B&F's assumption):
> "…it is in line with the assumption of Bartling and Fischbacher (2012) that **only people and not nature can be held responsible for an outcome**."

### Coffman (2011) — *Intermediation reduces punishment (and reward)*
As summarised by Argenton et al. (2023):
> "A key finding in previous studies on delegation is that delegation is often effective in diverting punishment. For example, in dictator games with a punishment possibility, **the principal receives a lower punishment in case an unfair allocation is chosen by a delegate rather than by the principal** (Coffman, 2011; Bartling and Fischbacher, 2012; Oexl and Grossman, 2013)."
> "One exception is Coffman (2011) who … **finds that these donations are rewarded less by a third party when the intermediary role of the charity is made more salient**. Although related, intermediation is different from delegation since a delegate is usually granted more decision autonomy."

### Oexl & Grossman (2013) — *Shifting the blame to a powerless intermediary*
As restated by Hill (2015):
> "One of the main results … shows that in the context of a dictator game, **principals face less punishment by delegating a decision that produces inequitable payoffs to an agent, even if the agent is powerless to choose the equitable outcome** (Grossman & Oexl 2012)."

### Hamman, Loewenstein & Weber (2010) — *Self-interest through delegation*
As restated by Erat (2013):
> "Hamman, Loewenstein, and Weber (2010) propose that **people use agents to avoid taking responsibility for immoral outcomes**, and specifically for inequitable outcomes in a dictator game. They contend that **this rationale for delegation exists even when the same unfair outcome … can be obtained by oneself** (or even when there is no possibility of punishment like in Bartling and Fischbacher, 2012)."

(Delegation rate in Hamman et al.: ~40% in the final rounds when subjects could choose;
Argenton et al. report it as "40% in Hamman et al. (2010)".)

## B. Beyond the dictator game — other domains (line 17 territory)

### Ultimatum bargaining — Fershtman & Gneezy (2001), *Strategic delegation: an experiment*
Abstract:
> "We examine the effects of strategic delegation in a simple ultimatum game experiment. We show that **when the proposer uses a delegate, her share increases both when the delegate is optional or mandatory. This is true despite the fact that the delegate cannot be used as a commitment device.** We also show that **unobserved delegation by the responder reduces her share, as her delegate is perceived to be more willing to accept tough offers.**"

The "shield/hostage" mechanism (the reason it works):
> "The delegate's offer is accepted more easily by the responder because it is not made directly by the proposer but by a third party. Another is that **the responder may be less keen to punish the proposer because by doing so he also punishes the delegate.** Given such behavior, the proposer optimally provides incentives to his agent to make tough offers."

Magnitudes:
> "When the proposer used an agent with an observable compensation scheme, **the average proposal rose from 56.7 to 64.5 and the average payoffs to the proposer rose from 47.7 to 60.5**."
> "In the PO session, 4 out of 30 proposals (i.e., 13%) were … rejected in the ultimatum game without delegation, but **when the proposer used the agent only 1 out of the 20 offers (i.e. 5%) was rejected, even though the proposals were significantly worse for the responder.**"

Optional delegation is used:
> "Among the 19 proposers, **14 (73%) chose to use the delegation option** despite the fact that it introduced an additional level of complexity."

### Sequential voting — Bartling, Fischbacher & Schudy (2015), *Pivotality and responsibility attribution in sequential voting*
Headline:
> "**…pivotal decision makers are punished significantly more for an unpopular voting outcome than non-pivotal** [decision makers]."

Strategic avoidance of pivotality:
> "Our data further suggest that **about one-fifth of those voters who reveal their preference for the unequal allocation … vote for the equal allocation if it is possible to 'delegate' the pivotal vote to the subsequent voter.**"
> "18.6% of the … voters [choose the] option that they expect (or know) the majority of other voters to vote for … consistent with the idea that **these voters prefer to delegate the vote for the unequal allocation to subsequent voters.**"

### Deception / lying — Erat (2013), *Avoiding lying: the case of delegated deception*
Abstract / Results:
> "**A significant fraction of people employ an agent (to lie) even when they could lie themselves.** Moreover, **the likelihood of delegating to an agent depends on the incentives, with more people choosing to delegate when the lie hurts to a greater extent the person being lied to.** Finally, … **women are more likely to delegate to an agent compared to men, especially so when the harm inflicted by the lie is larger.**"

Magnitudes:
> "**25% senders in T[−2], and 34% senders in T[−6], use the agent** to send the message to the receiver." (overall ~30%)
> "Comparing the propensity to delegate, I find that **people are more likely to use an agent in T[−6] (34%) compared to T[−2] (25%)** … the propensity to delegate is larger when the harm done to others is larger."
> Gender: "within T[−6], **women are significantly more likely to use an agent compared to men (42% vs. 26%)**."

Mechanism:
> "**The agent by merely acting on the sender's behalf diffuses her responsibility thus making lying easier** (for instance, see Bartling and Fischbacher, 2012)."

### Policy-making / legislatures — Hill (2015), *Does delegation undermine accountability?*
Headline (note: vignette experiment with a *direct* measure of blame, and the result survives full information):
> "**…participants blame principals significantly less [when the principal delegates to a powerless intermediary] than in cases where the principal brings about the outcome directly.** Accordingly, this article provides the first clean evidence that **principals can delegate a blameworthy decision to a powerless intermediary and nonetheless evade responsibility.**"

Blame-shifting operates *directly*, not only through informational obfuscation:
> "**Strikingly, … participants tend to underassign blame to legislatures even when they are presented with a complete description of the respective actions** of the [legislature and agency]. … **Delegation appears to impact voter behavior directly, as well as indirectly.**"

Asymmetric effect on agencies:
> "While delegation tends to reduce blame for legislators, its effects on agencies are more variable. … **less powerful agencies [face] more blame than is rationally warranted, and autonomous agencies less blame than is rationally warranted.**"

### Financial delegation — Freer, Friedman & Weidenholzer (2024), *Motives for delegating financial decisions*
Abstract:
> "**A surprisingly large fraction of investors delegate even trivial choice tasks, suggesting a major role for the blame shifting motive.** A larger fraction of investors delegate our more complex tasks, suggesting that decision costs play a role for some investors. Some investors who delegate choose a low quality expert with high earnings, suggesting a role for chasing past performance. **We find no evidence for a fourth possible motive, that delegation makes risk more acceptable.**"

Magnitudes / identification:
> "**About half of our investors delegate even the trivial and simple choice tasks. About 60% delegate the complex task.** … Since the delegation rate is so high in the Trivial task even in the NoPay condition, we conclude that, consistent with Hypothesis 2, **blame shifting is a primary motivation** for many of our investors."
> "**Changing the task to Complex increases the delegation rate by 11 percentage points** … indicating that … complexity-related decision cost is an important motivation for some investors."
> "We were unable to detect any connection between investors' characteristics (such as revealed risk aversion, choice quality and personality profile) and their decisions whether to delegate."

(For comparison, the paper notes prior delegation rates: "35% in Apesteguia et al. 2020 and 17–38% in Holzmeister et al. (2022)".)

### Credit as the mirror of blame — Argenton, Potters & Yang (2023), *Receiving credit: on delegation and responsibility*
Abstract:
> "Evidence has shown that blame for a 'bad' decision can be shifted by delegating the decision to someone else. We … examine whether the reverse is also true: does one receive credit for taking a 'good' decision as compared to delegating the decision to someone else? Our results indicate that the answer is affirmative. **A person receives higher rewards when she makes a fair decision herself than when a delegate does.** This indicates that **responsibility attribution is a double-edged sword that applies to both bad and good outcomes.**"

Key result:
> "**The dictator receives a higher reward when directly choosing the fair allocation than when the delegate does it on her behalf** … delegation in the presence of rewards dilutes credit-taking in the same way as delegation in the presence of punishments dilutes blame-taking."

The two passages the user already found (the literature-synthesis paragraph documenting delegation of unfair decisions across dictator games, ultimatum games, lying, bribing; and the Charness 2004 / Maximiano et al. 2013 gift-exchange "responsibility-alleviation" findings) are part of this paper's §1 and are reproduced in the user's prompt.

The "Machiavelli preference":
> "Gawn and Innes (2021) … find the delegation decision to be roughly 20 percentage points more frequent when it is payoff-equivalent to a selfish decision than … a more generous decision. This supports the 'Machiavelli preference' to **retain decision rights for generous decisions and delegate them for decisions that are selfish or harmful to others.**"

Gift-exchange "responsibility-alleviation" (verbatim, from Argenton et al.'s §1):
> "Charness (2000) finds that **workers in a gift-exchange game respond to identical levels of wages with lower efforts when the wages are set by a neutral agent than when the wages are determined by a random device**, suggesting a possible 'responsibility-alleviation' effect. Charness (2004) and Maximiano et al. (2013) find that **workers respond with lower efforts when a low wage is 'intentionally' set by the employer than when it is delegated** to either a random device or a neutral agent."

## C. Choosing on behalf of others — "passing the buck" (Steffel, Williams & Perrmann-Graham 2016)
*Passing the buck: delegating choices to others to avoid responsibility and blame.* (Currently commented out at line 19.)

Core findings:
> "**People are more likely to delegate choices for others than choices for themselves, especially when it seems likely that those choices might have unappealing outcomes** … stemming from a desire to avoid feeling responsible or being blamed for such decisions rather than a desire to avoid difficult choices or a lack of concern for the decisions' consequences."

Felt responsibility operates even under anonymity (independent of anticipated blame):
> "people were more likely to delegate when choosing for another person than for themselves **regardless of whether the person for whom they were choosing knew who chose on their behalf** … suggesting that **felt responsibility itself is sufficient to motivate people to delegate** … However, … anticipated blame also contributes."

Blame-avoidance dominates credit-seeking:
> "people seem to **care more about avoiding blame for bad outcomes than getting credit for good outcomes** … people were equally likely to delegate a choice between attractive options regardless of [for] whom."

Unique to delegation (not deferral or chance):
> "people are **more likely to delegate but not delay or flip a coin** to resolve decisions for others than decisions for themselves."

Delegation requires an authority who can *assume* responsibility — not expertise:
> "**delegation is not predicated on expertise but is predicated on authority** … people delegate, not to put choices in the hands of a more capable decision maker, but to cede responsibility."
> "**automated agents like algorithms cannot take the place of human agents as potential surrogates**" (because chance/inanimate objects cannot assume responsibility). ← directly useful contrast for the present paper's pivot to algorithmic intermediaries.

## D. Mechanism nuances worth foregrounding

From the canonical papers (already partly in lines 11–13), three components of the
responsibility-shifting effect recur and are worth stating crisply:

1. **Direct interaction with the victim, not the intermediary's presence, drives the principal's punishment reduction** (Coffman 2011): the principal "can no longer avoid punishment when she directly interacts with the receiver despite the intermediary's presence."
2. **The act of choosing — not contributing to the unfairness — is what transfers blame to the intermediary** (Oexl & Grossman 2013): blame shifts even when the intermediary can only pick between two unfair options.
3. **Randomization devices absorb less responsibility than deciding humans** (Bartling & Fischbacher 2012; Oexl & Grossman 2013; and corroborated by Steffel et al. 2016, who find people will not "pass the buck" to a coin flip). "Only people and not nature can be held responsible."

These three points jointly motivate the present paper's question: an **algorithmic** intermediary is neither a deciding human nor a mere randomization device, so where it falls on this spectrum is an open question.

## E. Political-science / public-choice background (already cited, line 9)
- **Weaver (1986)**, *The politics of blame avoidance*; **Fiorina (1986)** — politicians/legislators have stronger incentives to avoid blame than to claim credit; "negativity bias" in voters' treatment of gains vs. losses.
- Argenton et al. (2023) note: "in political science, it is often argued that **politicians and legislators have much stronger incentives to avoid blame than to claim credit** … attributed to a 'negativity bias' on the part of voters … (Weaver, 1986; Pierson, 1994)."

## G. Suggestions for extending / rewriting `literature.tex` line 17

**What line 17 does now:** a one-domain-per-sentence tour (ultimatum → voting → lying →
policy → finance), each compressed to its headline. It is accurate but (i) leaves the
*mechanism* implicit, (ii) omits the credit side (Argenton, currently commented out at
line 15) and the choosing-for-others generalisation (Steffel, commented out at line 19),
and (iii) misses two findings that set up your contribution especially well: that blame
reduction in Hill survives full information, and that the motive does **not** transfer to
non-human surrogates (chance devices). All citation keys below already exist in the corpus.

### Suggestion 1 — Sharpen the existing domain sentences with their mechanisms
- **Ultimatum (Fershtman & Gneezy 2001):** add *why* the delegate helps — the responder is reluctant to punish the proposer because doing so also punishes the delegate, so tougher offers are accepted (rejection fell from 13% to 5% despite worse offers). "Extract larger surpluses" undersells the mechanism.
- **Lying (Erat 2013):** "pay an agent to lie" → add that the propensity to delegate the lie *rises with the harm it inflicts on the victim*, sharpening the responsibility-avoidance interpretation.
- **Policy (Hill 2015):** add the load-bearing nuance — delegating principals are blamed less *even when observers have complete information about who did what*, so blame-shifting is a direct preference, not merely an artefact of informational obfuscation. This is the single most useful addition, because it pre-empts the "it's just an information story" objection.

### Suggestion 2 — Fold in the credit mirror (un-comment line 15, Argenton et al. 2023)
A single sentence establishes that responsibility attribution is *double-edged*: delegation
dilutes credit for good outcomes just as it dilutes blame for bad ones. This strengthens
the framing and matches your results section (which, per the commented note, revisits credit).

### Suggestion 3 — Add the "choosing for others" generalisation (un-comment line 19, Steffel et al. 2016)
This belongs in this paragraph because it (a) generalises the motive beyond self-interested
allocations to *decisions made on behalf of others* — closer to your setting, where the
outcome falls on a third party; (b) shows the motive is **blame-avoidance, not credit-seeking**;
and (c) shows people pass the buck only to a *person who can assume responsibility*, not to a
coin flip — the natural hinge into the algorithm question.

### Suggestion 4 — Use the "humans vs. chance devices" spectrum as the bridge to line 21
Three canonical results (B&F 2012 and Oexl 2013: a random device absorbs *some* but *less*
blame than a deciding human; Steffel 2016: people won't delegate to a coin flip at all)
define a spectrum — deciding human at one end, randomization device at the other. An
algorithmic intermediary sits ambiguously between them. Stating this explicitly makes the
first of the three "departures" in line 21 land harder.

### Proposed drop-in rewrite of line 17 (uses only keys already in the corpus)

> Beyond the canonical dictator-game setting, the responsibility-shifting motive --- and the strategic use of delegation it gives rise to --- has been documented across a range of domains. In incentivised ultimatum bargaining, proposers who use a delegate secure larger shares: responders are less willing to reject a tough offer when doing so would also punish the intermediary, so the same offers that would be rejected from the proposer directly are accepted from her agent \citep{fershtman_gneezy_2001}. In sequential voting, pivotal voters are punished more than non-pivotal voters for the same group outcome, and a substantial share of voters strategically vote against their preferred allocation to avoid being pivotal \citep{bartling_fischbacher_schudy_2015}. For acts of deception, principals delegate the act of lying to an agent, and do so more often the more the lie harms its victim \citep{erat_avoiding_2013}. In policy-making, observers rate delegating legislatures as less blameworthy than legislatures who decide directly --- even when the intermediary is powerless to change the outcome and even when observers are fully informed about each actor's contribution, indicating that blame-shifting is a direct preference rather than an artefact of incomplete information \citep{hill_does_2015}. \citet{freer_friedman_weidenholzer_2024} isolate the motive in financial decision-making, finding that a substantial fraction of investors delegate even purely luck-based lottery choices, where alternative motives --- decision costs, performance-chasing, or risk tolerance --- cannot rationalise the choice. The same logic operates on the credit side: \citet{argenton_potters_yang_2023} show that delegating a fair decision dilutes the credit a principal receives, just as delegating an unfair one dilutes blame, so responsibility attribution cuts symmetrically across good and bad outcomes. The motive also extends beyond one's own payoffs: when choosing on behalf of others, people delegate more readily than when choosing for themselves --- especially when outcomes may be bad --- out of a desire to avoid blame rather than to claim credit, and they do so only by ceding the choice to another person who can assume responsibility, not to a coin flip \citep{steffel_passing_2016}.

(If the paragraph becomes too long, the Argenton and Steffel sentences can be split into a
short follow-on paragraph, or Steffel can stay in the results section as the line-19 comment
suggests — but at minimum the Hill "even under full information" clause is worth adding now.)

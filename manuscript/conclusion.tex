\section{Conclusion} \label{conclusion}
\hl{[On multiple occasions, we say that delegation carries no information about relative ability. Simultaneously, we argue that the delegation decision is a signal about intended effort (and by extension, performance?) in the prediction for Player B. Do we see a contradiction here?]}

As algorithms take over decisions that affect other people, who should answer for the results has become the subject of a lively legal, ethical, and policy debate. This paper examines the behavioral side of this debate. We ask whether the prospect of being held responsible shapes the decision to put an algorithm in charge, and whether putting an algorithm in charge shapes the responsibility assignment of those affected by the outcome. We take both questions to a preregistered online experiment in which a decision-maker either makes a payoff-relevant prediction for a recipient herself or delegates it to an algorithm calibrated, subject by subject, to match her own performance. Between subjects, we vary whether the recipient can reduce the decision-maker's earnings. We elicit punishment for every combination of delegation choice and prediction outcome, so we observe directly whether delegation shields the delegator from punishment, conditional on the outcome. Because the two conditions differ only in whether punishment is possible, the comparison of delegation rates identifies the effect of the prospect of punishment on the decision to delegate.

Both answers depart from the canonical account of delegation as a means of responsibility shifting. The prospect of punishment does not push decision-makers toward the algorithm but away from it, a reversal of the prediction imported from settings with human intermediaries \citep[e.g.,][]{bartling_shifting_2012}. Delegation, in turn, offers no protection against punishment. Recipients punish delegated and self-made predictions alike, conditioning their sanctions almost entirely on the realized outcome rather than on how the prediction was made. The punishment result offers an explanation for why the canonical prediction fails, yet not for its reversal.

We examine several explanations to reconcile these findings. Delegation preferences over task uncertainty or process ownership would explain the treatment difference only if the prospect of punishment itself activated them. Beliefs show no punishment premium attached to delegation. Prediction outcomes and screen times do not indicate punishment-induced additional effort, whereby the decision-maker would retain the decision in the hope of outperforming the algorithm. Merely signaling to exert additional effort by retaining the decision would require no measurable effort at all, but move delegation only if the prospect of punishment gave the signal value. Belief evidence speaks against such a monetary value, leaving only a non-monetary value in which the prospect of punishment makes the recipient's judgment salient in itself. However, both measures are imperfect. The belief elicitation was unincentivized and came only after the delegation decision, so it may capture post-hoc rationalization rather than the beliefs that drove the choice, while actual effort is unobserved, with prediction outcomes and screen times serving only as proxies. The measures may therefore simply fail to detect a decision-maker who fears that delegating will be read as shirking and draw harsher punishment, or who retains the decision to try to outperform the algorithm, a motive that would at least be consistent with recipients punishing bad outcomes more heavily. These limits leave the mechanism narrowed rather than settled.

Nevertheless, we contribute by providing the first direct test of whether the prospect of punishment motivates delegation to an algorithm, and show that in an environment with inherent task uncertainty and no self-serving choice to police, accountability disciplines algorithm use rather than fueling it. We also show that delegation cannot shift blame when the algorithm performs no better or worse than the decision-maker, with recipients conditioning their sanctions on the outcome rather than on how the prediction was made. More broadly, our findings add a determinant of algorithm use to those documented in the aversion literature, one that operates not through the algorithm or the task but through what delegating may convey about the person who chooses it.

\section{Experimental Design} \label{design}

We ran an online experiment via the British platform Prolific (https://www.prolific.com) with UK participants. The experiment was programmed in oTree~\citep{chen_otree_2016} and eligibility followed Prolific's standard quality screens, a minimum $95\%$ prior approval rate, a fluent-English filter, and a desktop-only restriction (no mobile or tablet) that kept the screen layout consistent across subjects.

The design combines a between-subject manipulation of whether costly punishment is possible with a within-subject elicitation of conditional punishment behavior. Within each condition, participants assumed one of two roles. Player~A decides whether to make a payoff-relevant prediction for Player~B herself, or to delegate the prediction to an algorithm calibrated to match her own measured performance. Player~B is the matched recipient whose payoff is fully determined by the outcome of the prediction, whether Player~A makes it herself or delegates it to the algorithm. In the \textit{Punishment} condition Player~B may subsequently punish Player~A, conditional on both the delegation decision and the realized outcome, while in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition no punishment is possible. Figure~\ref{fig:treatments} summarizes the basic setup.

\subsection*{Procedure}

\subsubsection*{Player A}

After providing informed consent, Player~A completed ten incentivized rounds of a visual prediction task. Specifically, in each round, she predicted a person's weight from a facial image and the person's height. She could enter predictions via a slider or type them directly in kilograms, pounds, or stones-and-pounds.\footnote{All individuals depicted in the images had explicitly consented to the use of their photographs for research purposes.}  No feedback was provided between rounds. At the end of the experiment, one of the ten rounds was randomly selected for payment. Player~A earned $\pounds 4$ if her prediction in that round was within $10$~lbs of the true weight and $\pounds 2$ otherwise. These initial rounds served three purposes simultaneously: First, they established a stake from which any later punishment could be deducted. Second, they familiarized Player~A with the task. Third, they generated the performance data needed to calibrate the algorithm.

The choice of a visual prediction task is not incidental. Algorithm preferences may depend sensitively on the algorithm's perceived relative competence~\citep{qin_ai_2025,logg_algorithm_2019}. Our design neutralizes this concern by calibrating the algorithm to match each subject's individual performance. The visual nature of the task lends credibility to this equal-performance claim, as people have everyday familiarity with judging others' build and appearance, and algorithmic error is plausible.\footnote{Predicting body weight from an image has been used in earlier experiments~\citep{logg_algorithm_2019}.} In contrast, subjects may be skeptical that an algorithm could ever fail in purely mathematical tasks~\citep{gogoll_rage_2018,feier_hiding_2022}. Finally, we chose a prediction task involving genuine uncertainty rather than a setting with an obvious self-serving motive (as in classic dictator-game variations). %\footnote{With no self-serving option available, an intention-based punishment motive should have less bite than in those settings.} 
This reflects the nature of many real-world applications of algorithms, in which outcomes are inherently uncertain, the decision-maker has no direct material interest in the affected party's outcome, and performance is evaluated ex post.

After the ten rounds, we randomly paired Player~A with another participant, Player~B, who had not completed the prediction task himself. We informed Player~A that her final prediction would not affect her own payoff but would almost entirely determine Player~B's. A prediction within 10~lbs of the true weight would give Player~B a high payoff ($\pounds 5$), and any less accurate prediction a low payoff ($\pounds 1$).\footnote{Since the final prediction determines only Player~B's payoff, Player~A has no financial interest in Player~B receiving the low payoff. As long as a better outcome for Player~B weakly reduces Player~A's expected punishment in the \textit{Punishment} condition, the incentives of the two players can be considered aligned.} Player~A then learned that she could either make the prediction herself or delegate it to an algorithm. She was also told that, based on her first ten predictions, the algorithm would have performed equally well.

We calibrated the algorithm to mimic Player~A's own performance over the first ten rounds. Concretely, if Player~A delegated the final prediction to the algorithm, the algorithm was equivalent to drawing a Bernoulli outcome with success probability equal to Player~A's empirical accuracy rate over her first ten rounds. For example, a subject who scored 3 out of 10 in the initial rounds thus delegates to an algorithm that produces the high payoff for Player~B with $30\%$ probability.

Fixing the algorithm's success probability to each subject's own accuracy makes the equal-performance claim hold subject by subject, not merely on average across a pool of participants. This matters because beliefs about relative performance are otherwise likely a dominant driver of delegation.\footnote{This motive would not inherently bias the treatment-effect comparison, since it should be balanced across the \textit{Punishment} and \textit{No-Punishment} conditions, but it might otherwise dominate the delegation decision and may obscure the responsibility-shifting motive of interest.} For example, under the session-level calibration of \citet{feier_hiding_2022}, the delegation choice is largely driven by each subject's sense of her ability relative to the algorithm. Matching the algorithm to each subject neutralizes this motive, and making this performance parity common knowledge should limit Player~B from reading delegation as a signal of Player~A's believed relative competence. This makes any difference in punishment easier to interpret as a response to delegation itself rather than to perceived overconfidence.

In the \textit{Punishment} condition, we further informed Player~A that Player~B could impose (costly) punishment of up to $\pounds 2$, and could condition punishment on both her delegation decision and the realized outcome. In the \textit{No-Punishment} condition, Player~B had no such punishment option. The two delegation options (own decision / algorithm) appeared in random order across subjects. Following prior work, we attach no monetary cost to delegation \citep{bartling_shifting_2012,coffman_intermediation_2011,feier_hiding_2022}.\footnote{This allows the delegation decision in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition to more closely reflect Player~A's intrinsic preference over delegation. Moreover, if delegation carried a positive cost, any responsibility-shifting motive would first have to outweigh that cost.}
%We attached no monetary cost to delegation. This brings us closer to Player~A's intrinsic preference over delegation in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition, since with any positive cost, the responsibility-shifting motive would need to outweigh a price effect. Thus, costless delegation is also standard in the literature~\citep{bartling_shifting_2012,coffman_intermediation_2011,feier_hiding_2022}. 
If Player~A chose to make the prediction herself, she entered her final prediction on the next screen. If she delegated instead, this screen was skipped.

We then asked Player~A to report her beliefs about Player~B's punishment in each of the four scenarios resulting from the combination of delegation decision (yes/no) and prediction outcome (high/low payoff for Player~B). We elicited beliefs on the same scale as actual punishment for Player~B ($\pounds 0$--$\pounds 2$ in $\pounds 0.10$ increments) and presented the four scenarios in block-wise random order. We chose not to incentivize belief elicitation for three reasons. First, only two of the four scenarios remain payoff-relevant after the delegation decision, making the others purely hypothetical. Second, an incentivized mechanism could induce hedging. Third, an incentive-compatible scoring rule across four scenarios would have substantially increased task complexity. Because we are nonetheless interested in comparing punishment beliefs to actual punishment, we included an attention check on the belief-elicitation screen. In the \textit{No-Punishment} condition, where no actual punishment was possible, we elicited beliefs about hypothetical punishment to keep the experimental flow identical across conditions.

We then asked Player~A to assess her own performance in the first ten rounds by assigning a probability mass (summing to $100\%$) to each possible score from $0$ to $10$.\footnote{The elicitation was incentivized. Player~A earned an additional $\pounds 0.30$ with the probability she had assigned to her actual score. While this linear scoring rule is not proper as its payoff-maximizing report places all mass on the single most likely score, irrespective of risk attitude, in practice most subjects spread probability mass across several scores.} We elicited this belief after the delegation decision, and after Player~A had been told that the algorithm would perform equally well. While learning about relative algorithm performance may lead her to revise her beliefs about her own performance, it is precisely this post-update belief that is the analytically relevant input for any subsequent behavior.  We also asked Player~A to estimate the distribution of scores in the population, assigning to each score from $0$ to $10$ the share of participants she expected to have achieved it. Combined with her belief about her own score, this locates where she places herself in the distribution and yields a measure of perceived ability relative to other participants. One score was drawn at random at the end of the experiment, and Player~A earned $\pounds 0.30$ if her estimate for it fell within five percentage points of the realized population share.

Finally, we elicited risk preferences using an incentivized multiple price list in the style of \citet{holt_risk_2002} and administered a short questionnaire on socio-demographic characteristics and technology affinity, before presenting Player~A with her preliminary payoff.

\subsubsection*{Player B}

After providing informed consent, Player~B learned that he had been randomly matched with a Player~A, and introduced to the task Player~A had completed. He learned that his own payoff would be determined by the outcome of Player~A's final prediction -- $\pounds 5$ if it fell within 10~lbs of the true weight and $\pounds 1$ otherwise -- while Player~A's payoff depended solely on her performance in the initial ten rounds. Crucially, we also told Player~B that Player~A had the option to delegate the final prediction to an algorithm that performs as well as Player~A had performed in the earlier rounds, and that Player~A was aware of this, making it common knowledge.

In the \textit{Punishment} condition, Player~B could choose to reduce Player~A's payoff by up to $\pounds 2$ in $\pounds 0.10$ increments. We focus on punishment rather than reward because the responsibility-attribution literature has predominantly operationalized blame through punishment~\citep{bartling_shifting_2012,coffman_intermediation_2011,steffel_passing_2016}. Imposing strictly positive punishment required Player~B to forgo $\pounds 0.10$ of his own earnings, ensuring that punishment was costly to the punisher. We elicited punishment decisions for all four combinations of delegation (yes/no) and outcome (high/low payoff for Player~B), implementing only the decision matching the realized combination. Because the punishment decision is the central outcome variable for Player~B, we included an attention check on the punishment-elicitation screen. In the \textit{No-Punishment} condition, where actual punishment was not possible, we elicited hypothetical punishment decisions on the same scale. Player~B's perceptions of task difficulty, his risk preferences, and a short questionnaire followed, before we presented him with his final payoff.

After Player~B's decisions were recorded, we calculated Player~A's final payoff and paid all participants on the day of participation.

\subsection*{Hypotheses and identification}

Our design tests two main hypotheses, both grounded in the literature on responsibility avoidance through delegation to human intermediaries~\citep{bartling_shifting_2012,coffman_intermediation_2011,oexl_shifting_2013,hamman_self-interest_2010,hill_does_2015,steffel_passing_2016} and on responsibility attributions to algorithmic decision-makers~\citep{feier_hiding_2022,gogoll_rage_2018}. These strands of the literature mostly focus on what \citet{gogoll_rage_2018} have coined the \textit{moral} domain. Although the underlying prediction task is not itself moral, Player~A's choice imposes monetary consequences on a matched recipient, and a poor prediction causes real monetary harm. In this respect, our setting shares the defining feature of the settings this literature studies.
The responsibility-shifting account predicts that delegation protects Player~A from punishment following an adverse outcome. If Player~A anticipates this protection and does not expect it to be offset by greater punishment for delegation following a favorable outcome, the possibility of punishment should make delegation more attractive. Accordingly, we formulate Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:delegation}.

%The account of responsibility-shifting implies a particular pattern of punishment across outcomes. In sum, following a low-payoff outcome, delegation should shield Player~A from punishment, whereas following a high-payoff outcome, there is little blame to shift, so delegation should not alter punishment. Taken together, this pattern implies lower expected punishment under delegation. If Player~A anticipates this protection, the possibility of punishment should make delegation more attractive. %We state the formal hypotheses in the order in which we will present the results, beginning with Player~A's delegation choice.

\medskip
\hypothesishead{hyp:delegation}{Delegation} \textit{The introduction of potential punishment increases the likelihood that Player~A delegates the final prediction to the algorithm.}
\medskip

Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:delegation} is grounded in empirical evidence that responsibility avoidance shapes delegation decisions involving human intermediaries. \citet{bartling_shifting_2012} also show that subjects are more likely to delegate morally consequential decisions even to a die roll, an early form of delegation to a non-intentional decision mechanism, suggesting that the motive of responsibility avoidance persists when the intermediary lacks intentionality. Whether the same logic extends to algorithmic delegates, to decisions under genuine uncertainty, and to settings in which the outcome falls entirely on the recipient is less explored. \citet{feier_hiding_2022} report that delegation rates are similar for human and algorithmic intermediaries, suggesting that the kind of intermediary may matter less than the mere availability of one. Their design, however, does not compare delegation rates across punishment regimes, leaving open the question Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:delegation} addresses. Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:delegation} is identified by the between-subject comparison of Player~A's delegation rate across the \textit{Punishment} and \textit{No-Punishment} conditions, which exploits the punishment manipulation as the only between-subject difference and holds fixed every other feature of the decision environment.

The delegation response predicted by Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:delegation} rests on Player~A anticipating protection from punishment following an adverse outcome. This gives rise to our second hypothesis.

\medskip
\hypothesishead{hyp:punishment}{Punishment} \textit{Conditional on a low-payoff outcome, delegation reduces Player~B's punishment of Player~A.}

Evidence for Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:punishment} comes from \citet{bartling_shifting_2012}, who find that delegation to a randomization device allows decision-makers to avoid some punishment following an unfair outcome, although less so than delegation to another human. Similarly, \citet{feier_hiding_2022} find that decision-makers receive more favorable evaluations following bad outcomes when they delegated to an algorithm than when they relied on their own performance.

In related settings with human intermediaries, \citet{bartling_shifting_2012} and \citet{coffman_intermediation_2011} likewise find no significant increase in punishment for intermediation when the resulting allocation is fair. At the same time, \citet{coffman_intermediation_2011} show in a separate experiment that intermediation can reduce the reward assigned to prosocial behavior. Taken together, these findings suggest that delegation may attenuate the credit attached to a favorable outcome without necessarily generating additional punishment. %We therefore report the high-payoff comparison as a diagnostic for an offsetting punishment premium, but do not formulate a separate hypothesis for it.

Existing evidence also offers some support for the absence of an offsetting punishment premium for delegation following favorable outcomes. \citet{feier_hiding_2022} find no significant difference between evaluations of delegated and own decisions following good outcomes, and in the dictator game settings with human intermediaries, \citet{bartling_shifting_2012} and \citet{coffman_intermediation_2011} find no significant increase in punishment for intermediation when the resulting allocation is fair. Moreover, although \citet{coffman_intermediation_2011} show in a separate experiment that intermediation can reduce the reward assigned to prosocial behavior, receiving less credit need not translate into punishment when there is no adverse consequence to sanction.

Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:punishment} is identified by the within-subject comparison between delegated and own predictions following a low-payoff outcome. The corresponding comparison following a high-payoff outcome provides evidence on whether delegation itself attracts additional punishment, independent of the outcome. Both comparisons isolate the effect of delegation on punishment while holding fixed both the realized consequences for Player~B and all between-subject heterogeneity in Player~B's punishment proclivity.

%Evidence for this prediction comes from \citet{bartling_shifting_2012}, who find little punishment following fair outcomes and show that, following an unfair outcome, delegation to a randomization device allows decision-makers to avoid some punishment, although less than delegation to another human. Similarly, \citet{feier_hiding_2022} find that delegated and self-made decisions are rewarded similarly following good outcomes. Following bad outcomes, decisions delegated to an algorithm are rewarded more generously than self-made decisions.

%These studies provide less direct guidance about punishment following favorable outcomes. Delegation may reduce the credit assigned for a good outcome without generating punishment, because there is no adverse consequence to sanction. At the same time, Player~B may have a preference over the decision process itself, independently of the outcome. For example, delegation may be viewed as shirking, while retaining the decision may be viewed as overconfidence. Such process preferences could either offset or reinforce the punishment advantage predicted after bad outcomes.

Under their session-level calibration, the delegation decision carries a strong signal about Player~A's beliefs concerning her own ability relative to the algorithm. A failure to delegate may therefore be punished as overconfidence rather than for assuming responsibility, obfuscating responsibility shifting through intermediation with inferences about ability.

\noindent Experimental evidence suggests that delegation to human intermediaries can be motivated by the opportunity to shift responsibility for adverse outcomes. We ask whether this also holds for delegation to algorithms. %Experimental evidence suggests that delegation can shift responsibility for adverse outcomes onto human intermediaries. %We ask whether responsibility can likewise be shifted onto algorithms and whether the prospect of such a shift, in turn, motivates delegation.
In a preregistered online experiment with $322$ participants, a decision-maker chooses to make a payoff-relevant prediction for a matched recipient or delegate it to an algorithm, calibrated to perform equally well. We vary between subjects whether the recipient can punish the decision-maker. The prospect of punishment reduces delegation by $18.3$ percentage points, from $60.8\%$ to $42.5\%$, reversing the direction that the responsibility-shifting account predicts. Delegation, in turn, offers no protection against punishment. Recipients condition punishment primarily on the realized outcome, not on whether the prediction was delegated. %Delegation offers no protection in return. Recipients punish delegated and non-delegated predictions alike and condition punishment on the realized outcome. 
We discuss several candidate mechanisms for this result.

\section{Results} \label{results}

Data collection took place between February and June 2023 through the British online platform Prolific, with participation restricted to UK residents. A total of $322$ subjects were recruited ($161$ assigned to Player~A, $161$ to Player~B).\footnote{We preregistered the collection of data from $320$ participants ($80$ subjects per role per treatment). Due to attrition and subsequent re-matching we recruited an extra pair in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition. For the Player~A analyses, we exclude two subjects in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition because they entered a prediction of $0$ lbs. in at least eight of the ten initial rounds.} On average, Player~A spent $12$ minutes completing the experiment and earned $\pounds 3.62$, while Player~B spent approximately $8$ minutes and earned $\pounds 2.40$. The treatment groups are balanced on the background characteristics reported in Appendix~\ref{appendix:sum_stats}, Table~\ref{tab:treatment_balance}.

In the following, we first test the two main hypotheses derived from our preregistered research questions (Results~1-2). The subsequent analyses serve to understand the mechanism behind these results (Results~3-5). They are exploratory in nature, though several of them were outlined as secondary analyses in the preregistration.\footnote{Unless stated otherwise, all tests are two-sided. We describe results as significant if $p<0.05$ and as weakly significant if $0.05 \leq p < 0.10$. Because the delegation decision is a binary outcome compared between conditions, delegation shares are tested using Pearson Chi-squared tests, with Fisher's exact tests yielding the same conclusions throughout. Because punishment is elicited within-subject through the strategy method, punishment comparisons use paired $t$-tests, with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as non-parametric counterparts. Regressions report robust (HC1) standard errors. The preregistration and any deviations from the preregistered analyses are described in Appendix~\ref{appendix:prereg}.}

\subsection*{Player~A -- Delegation behavior} \label{sec:result1}

We begin by examining the delegation behavior of Player~A. In the \textit{Punishment} condition, where Player~B can punish, $42.5\%$ of Player~As delegated the final prediction to the algorithm. In the \textit{No-Punishment} condition, where punishment is impossible, $60.8\%$ delegated. The introduction of potential punishment thus significantly \textit{reduces} delegation by $18.3$~percentage points%(Pearson Chi-squared, $p=0.021$)
, opposing the direction posited by Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:delegation}.\footnote{A within-subject corroborator points in the same direction. Player~As in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition were asked, after their actual delegation decision, the hypothetical question of whether they would have delegated had punishment been possible. Among the $79$ respondents, $12$ would have stopped delegating under hypothetical punishment (25\% of delegators) while only $5$ would have started (16.1\% of non-delegators), constituting weakly significant switching behavior towards making the prediction oneself with punishment possible (within-subject McNemar's test $p=0.090$), and yielding a hypothetical delegation rate of $51.9\%$.} Figure~\ref{fig:delegation_shares} illustrates the difference. Delegation shares around $50\%$ are consistent with subjects, on average, finding the claim of equal relative performance between themselves and the algorithm credible. Their behavior reflects neither systematic preference for one's own prediction nor systematic deference to the algorithm.

\subsection*{Player~B -- Punishment behavior} \label{sec:result2}

We now turn to Player~B's punishment behavior in the \textit{Punishment} condition, in which punishment was payoff-relevant. Punishment is substantial in all four (delegation, outcome) scenarios of the strategy method, with average punishment between $\pounds 0.30$ and $\pounds 0.55$ (Figure~\ref{fig:punishment}, left axis). Punishment responds strongly to the outcome. Player~Bs punish bad outcomes significantly more than good outcomes, by $\pounds 0.20$ on average%(paired $t$-test, $p<0.001$; Wilcoxon signed-rank $p<0.001$)
, indicating that measured punishment carries real informational content rather than being random. The substantial punishment after good outcomes need not reflect noise either. Player~Bs can hold views about how the final prediction should have been made, independently of the realized outcome, and can punish accordingly.\footnote{For example, \citet{coffman_intermediation_2011} likewise documents non-trivial punishment in settings where the decision-maker's action could not have been improved upon.}

Holding the outcome fixed, Player~B punishes delegated and own decisions no differently. After a good outcome, average punishment is $\pounds 0.33$ for delegated and $\pounds 0.30$ for own decisions (paired $t$-test, $p=0.53$; Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.27$). After a bad outcome, $\pounds 0.48$ versus $\pounds 0.55$. The punishment gap for bad outcomes thus runs in the direction predicted by Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:punishment} but is small and statistically insignificant ($\pounds 0.07$; paired $t$-test $p=0.13$; Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.54$).

\medskip
\resulthead{res:punishment} \textit{Conditional on a low-payoff outcome, Player~B does not punish delegated decisions significantly less than Player~A's own decisions.}
\medskip

Regression analysis in Appendix \ref{appendix:reg_punishment} Table~\ref{tab:reg_punishment} confirms this pattern. Punishment loads overwhelmingly on the outcome, while the effect of delegation is essentially zero.\footnote{In the regression, because punishment for the four scenarios is elicited within-subject, any stable characteristic of Player~B is differenced out and cannot, by construction, confound the within-subject comparison. Thus, the control variables merely capture the between-subject association with Player~B's average punishment level. We find no striking relationship between Player~B's average punishment level and either their socio-demographic characteristics or their beliefs about Player~A's absolute performance.} The same pattern holds on the extensive margin (Appendix \ref{appendix:reg_punishment_extensive} Table~\ref{tab:reg_punishment_extensive}). For each outcome, the share of Player~Bs imposing any punishment is indistinguishable between delegated and own decisions (Figure~\ref{fig:punishment}, right axis). Punishment is also far from universal. Nearly half of Player~Bs ($45.0\%$) impose no punishment in any of the four scenarios. Within each scenario, average punishment therefore reflects a punishing minority rather than light, diffuse sanctioning.

In sum, Player~B punishes delegated decisions neither less harshly nor less frequently than Player~A's own decisions. In our setting, Player~A can therefore not escape punishment by delegating the final prediction to the algorithm, but she also pays no punishment premium for doing so. Punishment responds almost entirely to the realized outcome.\footnote{Hypothetical punishment choices elicited from Player~Bs in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition corroborate this conclusion (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:punishment_hypothetical}). If anything, these Player~Bs state that they would punish a delegated decision more than a decision made directly by Player~A after a bad outcome ($\pounds 0.53$ versus $\pounds 0.43$; paired $t$-test, $p=0.069$; Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.11$).} This result also bears on Result~\ref{res:delegation} as any treatment difference in delegation frequencies driven by punishment avoidance would be inconsistent with Player~B's observed punishment behavior.

\subsection*{Mechanisms} \label{sec:mechanism}

Result~\ref{res:delegation} does not merely fail to support Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:delegation}, but the effect is statistically significant in the opposite direction. The possibility of punishment discourages, rather than encourages, delegation to the algorithm. Thus, the prospect of punishment alters Player~A's delegation decision, even though we detect no punishment penalty for delegating. In the remainder of this section, we examine the data more closely and weigh candidate explanations with the goal of reconciling the two main results.

\paragraph{Departures from canonical settings.}

Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:delegation} drew on evidence from settings that differ from ours in ways that plausibly matter. In the modified dictator games of \citet{bartling_shifting_2012, coffman_intermediation_2011, hamman_self-interest_2010, oexl_shifting_2013}, the decision-maker chooses between a fair and an unfair allocation. Her intent is therefore transparent, the chosen allocation is implemented with certainty, and the unfair option benefits her directly at the recipient's expense. Punishment in these settings sanctions a deliberate, self-serving act, and delegation is attractive precisely because it blurs the link between the principal and that act. Our prediction task departs from these settings. The outcome is uncertain, so a bad outcome may reflect bad luck rather than bad intent, and neither option affects Player~A's own monetary payoff, leaving no self-serving choice to police. With little intent worth sanctioning, any intention-based punishment motive may lose its bite, and with it does the value to Player~A of placing an algorithm between herself and the outcome. We nevertheless chose this task deliberately since prediction under genuine uncertainty, by a decision-maker with no direct material stake in the affected party's outcome, is the environment in which real-world algorithmic delegation typically operates. These departures, however, may explain the absence of the predicted effect, not its reversal. A reversal requires a motive that weighs against delegation when punishment is possible.

Player~A may expect a delegated decision to be punished more harshly than an own decision, and delegate less in response. Expected differential punishment does not predict delegation in the full sample (Columns~(1)--(2)). Among subjects who passed the attention check on the belief-elicitation screen, the relationship sharpens. A $\pounds 0.10$ larger expected punishment penalty for deciding herself is associated with a $4.7$~percentage-point higher probability of delegation (Column~(3)), and with $5.5$~percentage points under the weighted specification (Column~(4)).\footnote{In the decomposition of Column~(5), the good-outcome belief difference carries the relationship ($2.7$~percentage points per $\pounds 0.10$, $p=0.010$), while the bad-outcome difference is positive but noisier ($1.7$~percentage points, $p=0.165$). The two differences are nearly orthogonal in this sample, so we read their coefficients as additive partial slopes rather than competitors for the same variance. Under expected-utility reasoning, however, only their probability-weighted sum should enter the decision rule. We therefore treat Column~(5) merely as a descriptive decomposition. %\hl{[Does this not only hold under risk neutrality or other symmetric motives?]}
}

Taken together, the belief evidence does not support anticipated differential punishment as the mechanism behind Result~\ref{res:delegation}. For anticipation of punishment to generate an $18.3$~percentage-point delegation gap between conditions, Player~As would need to expect systematically harsher punishment for delegated decisions. The measured beliefs show no such expectation, and such an expectation would not have been correct, as Player~B's realized punishment exhibits no delegation penalty either (Result~\ref{res:punishment}). What the belief evidence does show is heterogeneity. Within the \textit{Punishment} condition, Player~As whose beliefs embody a larger penalty for deciding themselves are more likely to delegate. Anticipated punishment is thus a dimension along which subjects differ, but it provides no aggregate push toward less delegation under punishment.\footnote{A mean expected penalty of zero need not rule out an effect on delegation if Player~As react more strongly when they expect a penalty for delegating than when they expect one for deciding themselves. We find no such asymmetry.} We nonetheless read the belief evidence with caution. First, hypotheticality may introduce noise as only two of the four scenarios could still materialize after Player~A's delegation decision in the \textit{Punishment} condition.\footnote{In the \textit{No-Punishment} condition all four scenarios were purely hypothetical.} Second, the elicitation of beliefs was unincentivized and took place after the delegation decision, so reports may reflect post-hoc rationalization rather than the beliefs that drove the choice.

\medskip

\paragraph{Sample composition.} Result~\ref{res:delegation} is not an artifact of sample composition either. Treatment randomization produced balance across socio-demographic and other background characteristics (Appendix~\ref{appendix:sum_stats}, Table~\ref{tab:treatment_balance}). Initial task performance, however, is weakly higher in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition. Player~As averaged $2.76$ versus $3.15$ correct predictions out of ten in the \textit{Punishment} and \textit{No-Punishment} conditions, respectively ($t$-test, $p=0.059$; Figure~\ref{fig:performance_overview}). However, this difference works against Result~\ref{res:delegation}. As we have seen, lower-performing Player~As are more likely to delegate, so that the lower average performance in the \textit{Punishment} condition should raise delegation there and therefore attenuate, rather than generate, the observed treatment difference. Player~As in both conditions report similar perceived performance ($4.65$ versus $4.89$, respectively).\footnote{Perceived performance is measured by the weighted-average belief about own performance. This overconfidence may partly reflect the information that the algorithm performs equally well.} Finally, the treatment effect is robust to controlling for actual and perceived performance (Table~\ref{tab:del_decision_determinants}, Columns~(3)--(5)).

\paragraph{Performance and delegation.} In the Probit specifications, task performance is negatively and significantly associated with delegation (Table~\ref{tab:del_decision_determinants}, Columns~(3) and (5)). Descriptively, this relationship is stronger in the \textit{Punishment} condition than in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition (Figure~\ref{fig:performance_overview}), although the difference between the two relationships is not statistically significant. %\footnote{In a pooled Probit regression, the interaction between the punishment indicator and task performance is statistically insignificant ($p=0.548$; $p=0.487$ when background controls are included).}
For perceived performance, a directionally similar but weaker relationship emerges in both conditions. Thus, actual performance separates delegators from non-delegators and because the relationship is slightly stronger in the \textit{Punishment} condition the delegation gap between conditions widens with performance. Player~As who delegated in the \textit{Punishment} condition scored on average $2.47$ out of $10$ in the first ten rounds, compared with $3.06$ for delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition ($t$-test, $p=0.039$). It is precisely the better performers who, particularly when punishment is on the table, keep the decision for themselves.

Our individual-level calibration is designed to remove the natural explanation for this relationship, namely that worse performers delegate because they believe the algorithm outperforms them. For example, in \citet{feier_hiding_2022}, the algorithm's performance distribution is matched to the distribution of human performance at the population level. A high-performing subject who recognizes her position in that distribution therefore rationally expects to outperform the algorithm, and a low-performing subject rationally expects the reverse. Delegation that declines with performance is then exactly what expected-payoff maximization predicts. In our experiment, by contrast, performance is equalized between the algorithm and each individual subject, and this equality of performance is common knowledge. The negative relationship between performance and delegation therefore points to something other than beliefs about relative ability.%\footnote{We cannot rule out that some subjects did not fully internalize the individual-level equalization. Two observations mitigate this concern. First, any such misunderstanding should arise in both conditions alike, so it offers no systematic explanation for the treatment difference in delegation. Second, under this alternative, delegation should decline with perceived own performance, with more confident subjects retaining the decision. In the data, perceived performance is statistically indistinguishable between delegators and non-delegators.}

Two mechanisms may account for the negative association between performance and delegation. The first is a preference over \textit{process ownership}. Player~As with a higher probability of producing the high payoff may prefer to bring it about themselves rather than have the algorithm do so, making delegation less attractive as performance rises. A symmetric version of this preference, under which Player~As also wish to avoid personally producing a low payoff, yields the same negative relationship. Relatedly, subjects' preferences may depend on \textit{outcome uncertainty}. \citet{dietvorst_people_2020} document that the preference for algorithms declines as outcome uncertainty rises. In our setting, uncertainty is arguably greatest when the probability of success is near one half. Because most subjects score below $5$ out of $10$, better performance moves that probability toward this point. %\footnote{Outcome uncertainty here is distinct from the difficulty of the task, which sets the level of the success probability.} 
A reluctance to delegate more uncertain decisions could therefore likewise make better-performing Player~As less likely to delegate.

Both readings describe motives that should operate similarly in both conditions. They can therefore account for the performance-delegation relationship, but not on their own for the treatment difference in delegation. Indeed, evaluated against this negative relationship, the slightly lower average performance in the \textit{Punishment} condition would, if anything, imply more delegation. It is nevertheless conceivable that the prospect of punishment makes these concerns more salient. For example, even with equal expected punishment, being punished for a decision one gave away may feel different from being punished for a decision one made oneself. Or relinquishing control over an uncertain outcome may trouble Player~A only once that outcome carries indirect consequences for Player~A. The descriptively stronger performance--delegation relationship under punishment is consistent with such a treatment dependence in the salience of these motives. However, the treatment-specific relationships do not differ statistically. Moreover, greater salience alone does not determine the aggregate effect. For example, a stronger symmetric preference over process ownership would tend to encourage delegation among the predominantly low-performing Player~As, whereas an asymmetric desire to produce the good outcome oneself or a heightened reluctance to delegate uncertain decisions could discourage it. We therefore regard these motives merely as possible explanations for the performance-delegation relationship, but not as established explanations for the treatment difference.

Either can account for the treatment difference only if the prospect of punishment activates it. \paragraph{Effort to outperform the algorithm.} Lastly, the prospect of punishment could motivate Player~A to exert additional effort when making the final prediction for Player~B. Although the algorithm's chance of success is fixed by Player~A's past performance, the final prediction itself has yet to be made. Player~A may thus believe that, by trying especially hard, she can outperform the algorithm and thereby give Player~B a better chance of the high payoff. To the extent that she expects milder punishment after a good outcome, the prospect of punishment would then push her toward making the prediction herself. This story yields a testable prediction. Relative to the first ten rounds, non-delegators should improve their performance more in the \textit{Punishment} condition than in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition. Similar improvement in both conditions would instead indicate a punishment-independent motive, for instance altruism toward Player~B, which cannot explain Result~\ref{res:delegation}.

The data do not support this story. %Delegators do not make a final prediction themselves, so effort on the final prediction is observable only for non-delegators. 
Measuring improvement as the difference between a subject's average absolute prediction error over the first ten rounds and her absolute error on the final prediction, non-delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition improve, whereas non-delegators in the \textit{Punishment} condition do not.\footnote{Non-delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition reduce their prediction error by $8.11$ lbs on average, compared with only $0.51$ lbs in the \textit{Punishment} condition (Welch $t$-test, $p=0.027$; Mann--Whitney test, $p=0.014$). Their average initial absolute errors are virtually identical ($22.69$ versus $22.47$ lbs, respectively; Welch $t$-test, $p=0.857$)} Because all Player~As faced the same images in the first ten rounds and on the final prediction, this contrast cannot reflect variation in image difficulty. Realized outcomes tell the same story. $48.4\%$ of non-delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition earn the high payoff for Player~B, compared with $21.7\%$ in the \textit{Punishment} condition, even though the two groups' performance over the first ten rounds is statistically indistinguishable ($3.29$ vs.\ $2.98$ correct predictions; $t$-test, $p=0.316$).\footnote{Balance among non-delegators across conditions coexists with the selection pattern rather than contradicting it. Non-delegators in the \textit{Punishment} condition are drawn from a slightly weaker pool but are more strongly positively selected within it, so the two forces partly offset, whereas among delegators they compound.} If anything, performance on the final prediction is higher where punishment is unavailable, the opposite of what a punishment-motivated effort account predicts.\footnote{Employing screen time as a measure for effort points in the same direction. In the \textit{Punishment} condition Player~As spend less time on the final prediction screen than in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition (medians $16$ vs.\ $23$ seconds). The same holds within subject, as non-delegators in both conditions spend more time on the final prediction than on an average initial round, but the increase is smaller under punishment ($+5.0$ vs.\ $+8.0$ seconds).} Effort itself remains unobserved, however, and absolute prediction errors, realized outcomes as well as time spent on the decision screen are only imperfect measures of it, so we cannot rule the channel out conclusively.\footnote{For instance, if the prospect of punishment raised effort while the accompanying pressure depressed performance, additional effort would be present, yet masked.}

\medskip

\paragraph{The signaling value of not delegating.} Although the prospect of punishment does not appear to induce additional effort (Result~\ref{res:effort}), the delegation choice itself may communicate effort. By keeping the decision, Player~A may want to convey to Player~B that she takes the task seriously and intends to try hard on his behalf. Delegating, by contrast, could be perceived as shirking. The signal lies in the choice itself rather than in any effort that follows, so Result~\ref{res:effort} does not speak against this account. Result~\ref{res:beliefs}, however, seems to. The signal has monetary value only if Player~A expects delegation, as apparent shirking, to draw harsher punishment, whereas in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition, Player~A could shirk without monetary consequences. Result~\ref{res:beliefs} indicates that Player~As in the \textit{Punishment} condition, on average, hold no such expectation, which leaves little monetary gain from sending the signal.\footnote{At the individual level, this channel is consistent with the belief heterogeneity documented in Result~\ref{res:beliefs}, though a mean expected penalty of zero implies no aggregate push toward less delegation.}

Two considerations keep this channel open. First, Player~A may care about sending this signal, beyond any punishment attached to it. For this concern to explain the treatment difference, however, it would again have to be activated by the prospect of punishment itself%, even though the expected amount of punishment does not differ by delegation choice
. In our environment, where no self-serving option exists and outcomes are noisy, the delegation choice is the only deliberate act on which Player~B can condition punishment, and thus the natural focus of any judgment he forms. Second, as discussed earlier, our punishment belief measure was unincentivized and elicited only after the delegation decision, so it may capture the beliefs behind that decision imperfectly and understate the penalty Player~A anticipated for delegating. Either way, a concern about being perceived as shirking would weigh against delegation precisely when punishment is possible. 

\paragraph{Taking stock.} The treatment difference in delegation shares (Result~\ref{res:delegation}) is robust, but its mechanism remains unresolved. It is not a composition artifact, since the conditions are balanced in background characteristics, while the weak imbalance in task performance works against the treatment effect. Belief evidence suggests that anticipated differential punishment cannot explain the reversal either (Result~\ref{res:beliefs}). On average, Player~A expects no punishment penalty for delegation, mirroring Player~B's realized punishment (Result~\ref{res:punishment}), although Player~As who expect a larger penalty for deciding themselves are more likely to delegate. Nor do we find evidence that punishment-induced effort can explain the reversal. Even though actual effort remains unobserved, non-delegators spend less time on the prediction for Player~B and improve less under punishment, not more (Result~\ref{res:effort}). What the data do show is selection. It is the better-performing Player~As who retain the decision (Result~\ref{res:selection}) %and this relationship is descriptively stronger under punishment
. Preferences over process ownership or outcome uncertainty are consistent with this selection but operate in both conditions alike. %They could remain plausible candidates for explaining Result~\ref{res:delegation} only if accountability itself activated them, a hypothesis our design cannot test.
The signaling value of not delegating requires no actual additional effort (Result~\ref{res:effort}), however, to explain the treatment effect it %similarly 
rests on being activated by the prospect of punishment itself.

%\paragraph{Reconciling our findings with \citet{feier_hiding_2022}.} Two of our results sit in apparent tension with the closest precedent. First, \citet{feier_hiding_2022} document that decision-makers are punished \textit{less} for algorithmically delegated decisions; we find no significant punishment differential. Second, the natural prediction from \citet{feier_hiding_2022}'s own findings is that the prospect of punishment should make algorithmic delegation \textit{more} attractive; we find the reverse. We do not view these contrasts as contradictions, but as evidence that the design features which differ between the two studies are doing real work. The differential punishment in \citet{feier_hiding_2022} is most plausibly read as third parties extracting a signal from the delegation decision about the principal's self-perceived ability relative to the algorithm---a signal that is maximally informative when (as in their design) the algorithm is calibrated at the session level and when the principal's relative ability is uncertain. Our individual-level calibration, made common knowledge, closes off this signal-extraction channel by construction: a Player~B in our setting has no basis to read delegation as overconfidence, laziness, or ability avoidance. The disappearance of the punishment differential in our data is therefore consistent with, rather than refutes, the signal-extraction reading of \citet{feier_hiding_2022}'s result. By the same logic, the responsibility-shifting motive that \citet{feier_hiding_2022}'s setup leaves on the table is muted in ours, isolating a different and milder margin: the moral salience of accountability for the principal herself, independent of any third-party inference. The reversal we observe in the delegation decision speaks to that margin---and we are explicit that the question of which design feature is causally responsible for the sign reversal cannot be resolved without an experiment that varies relative-performance calibration directly, which we do not do here.

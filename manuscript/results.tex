\section{Results} \label{results}

Data collection took place between February and June 2023 through the British online platform Prolific, with participation restricted to UK residents. A total of $322$ subjects were recruited ($161$ assigned to Player~A, $161$ to Player~B).\footnote{We preregistered the collection of data from $320$ participants ($80$ subjects per role per treatment). Due to attrition and subsequent re-matching we recruited an extra pair in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition. Appendix~\ref{appendix:pap} reports the full ex-ante power calculations underlying this sample size and distribution.} On average, Player~A spent $12$ minutes completing the experiment and earned $\pounds 3.63$, while Player~B spent approximately $8$ minutes and earned $\pounds 2.40$. Treatment randomization was effective, as demonstrated by the balance across observable characteristics (Appendix~\ref{appendix:sum_stats}, Table~\ref{tab:treatment_balance}).

In the following, we first test the two main hypotheses derived from our preregistered research questions. The subsequent analyses serve to understand the mechanism behind these results. They are exploratory in nature, though several of them were outlined as secondary analyses in the pre-registration.\footnote{Unless stated otherwise, all tests are two-sided. We describe results as significant if $p<0.05$ and as weakly significant if $0.05 \leq p < 0.10$. Because the delegation decision is a binary outcome compared between conditions, delegation shares are tested using Pearson chi-squared tests, with Fisher's exact tests yielding the same conclusions throughout. Because punishment is elicited within-subject through the strategy method, punishment comparisons use paired $t$-tests, with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as non-parametric counterparts. Regressions report robust (HC1) standard errors.}

\subsection*{Player~A -- Delegation behavior} \label{sec:result1}

We begin by examining the delegation behavior of Player~A. In the \textit{Punishment} condition, where Player~B can punish, $42.5\%$ of Player~As delegated the decision to the algorithm. In the \textit{No-Punishment} condition, where punishment is impossible, $59.3\%$ delegated. Figure~\ref{fig:delegation_shares} illustrates the difference. The introduction of potential punishment thus significantly \textit{reduces} the likelihood of delegation to the algorithm (Pearson Chi-squared, $p=0.033$; Fisher's exact, $p=0.041$ [Comment: drop Fisher exact test result?]), which reverses the direction posited by \textit{Hypothesis~1}.\footnote{A within-subject corroborator points the same direction. Player~As in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition were asked, after their actual delegation decision, the hypothetical question of whether they would have delegated had punishment been possible. Among the $81$ respondents, $12$ would have stopped delegating under hypothetical punishment (25\% of delegators) while only $5$ would have started (15.2\% of non-delegators), constituting weakly significant switching behavior towards making the decision oneself with punishment possible (within-subject McNemar's test $p=0.090$), and yielding a hypothetical delegation rate of $50.6\%$.} \hl{[Comment: Those are the p-values for the two-sided test. \subsection*{Mechanisms} \label{sec:mechanism}

Result~1 does not merely fail to support \textit{Hypothesis~1}, but the effect is statistically significant in the opposite direction. The possibility of punishment discourages, rather than encourages, delegation to the algorithm. Thus, the prospect of punishment changes Player~A's delegation decision, even though we detect no punishment penalty for delegating. In the remainder of this section, we examine the data more closely and weigh candidate explanations that could reconcile the two main results.

\paragraph{Departures from canonical settings.} \textit{Hypothesis~1} was grounded in evidence from settings that differ from ours in ways that plausibly matter. In the modified dictator games of \citet{bartling_shifting_2012, coffman_intermediation_2011, hamman_self-interest_2010, oexl_shifting_2013}, the decision-maker chooses between a fair and an unfair allocation. Thus, her intent is more transparent, the chosen allocation is implemented with certainty, and the unfair option benefits her directly at the recipient's expense. Punishment in these settings sanctions a deliberate, self-serving act, and delegation is attractive precisely because it blurs the link between the principal and that act. Our prediction task departs from these games in all three respects. The outcome is uncertain, so a bad outcome may reflect bad luck rather than bad intent, and neither option affects Player~A's own monetary payoff, so the incentives of the two players can be considered aligned, leaving no self-serving choice to police.\footnote{We consider the incentives aligned even though Player~A's payoff is only indirectly affected by her decision, as long as a better outcome for Player~B weakly reduces Player~A's expected punishment in the \textit{Punishment} condition.} With little intent worth sanctioning, any intention-based punishment motive loses its bite, and with it, the value to Player~A of placing an intermediary between herself and the outcome. Nevertheless, we chose this task deliberately since prediction under genuine uncertainty, by a decision-maker with no material stake in the affected party's outcome, is the environment in which real-world algorithmic delegation typically operates.

This reasoning, however, explains why the predicted effect on delegation might vanish, not why it should reverse. The reversal calls for a motive that weighs against delegation when punishment is possible.

\paragraph{Anticipated differential punishment.} The most natural candidate is anticipation of punishment. Player~A may expect a delegated decision to be punished more harshly than a self-made one, and delegate less in response. Such an expectation would, for example, be consistent with evidence that observers view the delegation of morally consequential decisions to machines relatively critically \citep[e.g.,][]{gogoll_rage_2018, bigman_people_2018, jauernig_people_2022, bonnefon_moral_2024}.

Taken together, the belief evidence is mixed. The measure itself is not pure noise. Within the \textit{Punishment} condition, subjects whose beliefs embody a larger penalty for deciding themselves are more likely to delegate, so the delegation choice responds to belief asymmetries where they exist. But a margin whose average is zero provides no aggregate push. For anticipation of punishment to generate a $17$~percentage-point delegation gap between treatments, Player~As would need to expect systematically harsher punishment for delegated decisions. They do not and neither would such an expectation have been correct, as Player~B's realized punishment exhibits no delegation penalty either (Result~2). Anticipated differential punishment is thus a dimension along which subjects differ, but it cannot explain the treatment difference in delegation.

\noindent\textbf{Result~3.} \textit{On average, Player~A expects no punishment penalty for delegation, mirroring Player~B's actual punishment behavior. Within the Punishment condition, however, Player~As who expect a larger penalty for deciding themselves are more likely to delegate.}

\paragraph{Selection into conditions.} Nor is Result~1 an artifact of sample composition. Selection on observable characteristics cannot explain Result~1, as treatment randomization produced balance across socio-demographic and other background characteristics (Appendix~\ref{appendix:sum_stats}, Table~\ref{tab:treatment_balance}). The same holds for performance. Average performance in the first ten rounds is balanced across conditions ($2.76$ vs.\ $3.09$ correct predictions out of ten in the \textit{Punishment} and \textit{No-Punishment} conditions, respectively; $t$-test $p=0.12$; Figure~\ref{fig:performance_overview}),\footnote{To the extent this small raw difference matters at all, it works against Result~1, because the \textit{Punishment} condition performs slightly worse, and as we will see, it is worse performers who delegate more in that condition, so any residual imbalance would attenuate rather than generate the treatment difference.} Player~As in both conditions exhibit similar overconfidence about their own performance ($4.65$ vs.\ $4.87$ in the \textit{Punishment} and \textit{No-Punishment} conditions, respectively; $t$-test $p=0.47$),\footnote{Confidence is measured by the weighted-average belief about own performance. This overconfidence may partly reflect the information that the algorithm performs equally well.} and the treatment effect is robust to controlling for actual and perceived performance (Table~\ref{tab:del_decision_determinants}, Columns~(3)--(4)).

\paragraph{Performance and delegation.} What differs across conditions is \textit{who} delegates. A first indication already appears in the pooled delegation regression in Tables~\ref{tab:del_decision_determinants} and \ref{tab:reg_belief_specs}, where performance loads negatively and is weakly significant. This tendency crystallizes within the \textit{Punishment} condition as the correlation between Player~A's score in the first ten rounds and the delegation decision is $-0.19$, with $p=0.092$ (Figure~\ref{fig:performance_overview}),\footnote{Among attention-check passers the relationship is even stronger ($r=-0.22$, $p=0.083$).} while a comparable relationship is weaker in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition.\footnote{A similar relationship holds for perceived performance in the \textit{Punishment} condition. Player~A's weighted-average belief about her own performance enters with a small negative coefficient but is never statistically significant. Thus, actual performance discriminates sharply between delegators and non-delegators ($2.38$ vs.\ $3.00$ correct out of ten, among attention-check passers) while perceived performance does not ($4.69$ vs.\ $4.71$; Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:performance_by_delegation}). Delegators under punishment are not subjects who \textit{believe} they are worse than their algorithm, but they are subjects who \textit{are} worse, and whose confidence does not reflect it.} Framed at the level of treatment differences, the delegation gap widens with performance. The composition of delegators shifts accordingly. Player~As who delegated in the \textit{Punishment} condition scored on average $2.47$ out of ten in the first ten rounds, whereas delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition scored $3.06$ ($p=0.039$). The additional delegation in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition thus comes disproportionately from the middle and upper part of the performance distribution. It is precisely these subjects who, when punishment is on the table, keep the decision for themselves.

This relationship is interesting because its natural explanation, namely that worse performers delegate because they believe the algorithm outperforms them, is ruled out in our design. In \citet{feier_hiding_2022}, the algorithm's performance distribution is matched to the human distribution at the \textit{population} level so that a high-performing subject who recognizes her position in that distribution rationally believes she outperforms the algorithm in expectation, and a low-performing one rationally believes the reverse. Delegation that declines with performance is then exactly what maximizing the expected outcome predicts. In our design, performance is equalized at the \textit{individual} level and this is common knowledge. The pattern in the \textit{Punishment} condition must therefore reflect something other than beliefs about relative ability.\footnote{We cannot rule out that some subjects did not fully internalize the individual-level calibration. Two observations limit this concern. First, any such misunderstanding should arise in both conditions alike, so it cannot explain why the relationship between performance and delegation emerges when punishment is possible, nor the treatment difference in delegation itself. Second, under this alternative, delegation should track perceived own performance, with more confident subjects keeping the decision. In the data, perceived performance is essentially identical for delegators and non-delegators.}

One reading of the relationship between performance and delegation could be a preference over \textit{process}. Player~As who have a good chance of producing the high payoff may prefer to bring it about themselves rather than through the algorithm. we refer to this as \textit{process ownership} of the outcome. Equivalently, Player~As with little chance of success may delegate to avoid being the proximate cause of a likely failure. Yet, while this motive can rationalize a negative relationship between performance and delegation, it would predict that relationship in \textit{both} conditions and predict no treatment difference in delegation rates, unless the preference is \textit{activated} by accountability, only becoming behaviorally consequential once Player~B's stake in the decision is enforceable rather than merely material. Alternatively, Player~A's preferences for delegation could vary with the amount of \textit{uncertainty} inherent in a decision. \citet{dietvorst_people_2020} document that the preference for algorithms declines as the uncertainty inherent in a decision rises.\hl{Comment: How does uncertainty in a task relate to difficulty of a task?} In our task, where most subjects score below five out of ten, better performance moves the success probability toward one half, arguably the point of maximal outcome uncertainty, so a distaste for delegating uncertain decisions would likewise depress delegation among better performers. However, also this channel applies to both conditions equally.

\paragraph{Effort to outperform the algorithm.} Lastly, the prospect of punishment could motivate Player~A to exert additional effort in making the decision for Player~B. Despite the algorithm being calibrated to Player~A's past performance, her final prediction is not yet made. Thus, a Player~A may believe that she can try especially hard and raise the chance of a good outcome for Player~B above the algorithm's, if the decision is not delegated. To the extent that Player~A expects milder punishment after a good outcome, the prospect of punishment would then push her toward deciding herself. This possibility yields the prediction that non-delegators should improve their performance, specifically in the \textit{Punishment} condition. Similar improvement in both conditions would instead indicate a treatment-independent (for instance, altruistic) motive, which cannot explain Result~1.

The data do not lend support to this story. Delegators do not make a final prediction themselves, so effort on the final prediction is observable merely for non-delegators. Measuring improvement as the difference between a subject's average absolute prediction error over the first ten rounds and her absolute error on the final prediction, non-delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition improve on their record, whereas non-delegators in the \textit{Punishment} condition do not. Because all Player~As faced the same images in the first 10 rounds and on the final prediction, this differential cannot reflect task-specific variation. Realized outcomes tell the same story with $45.5\%$ of non-delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition earning the high payoff for Player~B, compared with $21.7\%$ in the \textit{Punishment} condition, even though the two groups' performances are statistically indistinguishable over the first ten rounds ($3.12$ vs.\ $2.98$ correct predictions; $p=0.66$). If anything, effort seems to rise where punishment is impossible, which is the opposite of what a punishment-motivated effort story would predict.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Result~5.} \textit{The prospect of punishment does not induce additional effort. Non-delegators improve on their prior performance only where punishment is impossible.}
\medskip

\paragraph{The signaling value of not delegating.} Although non-delegators do not appear to exert additional effort, the delegation choice itself may communicate effort. By keeping the decision, Player~A may want to convey to Player~B that she takes the task seriously and intends to try hard on his behalf. Delegating, in contrast, could be perceived as shirking a decision that matters for another person. The signal lies in the choice itself and requires no actual additional effort, so Result~5 does not speak against this story. Result~3 seems to, if Player~A had to expect greater punishment for shirking the effort to make a decision for Player~B but, in the absence of punishment, can shirk without monetary consequences. Instead, if Player~A expects no punishment penalty for delegation, there is little to gain from the signal. The two can be reconciled if Player~A cares about Player~B's judgment itself, beyond the punishment attached to it,\footnote{Sanctioning power may make Player~B's judgment consequential in Player~A's eyes. A concern for how the delegation choice is perceived would then bind only when punishment is possible, even though the expected amount of punishment does not differ by delegation choice. Relatedly, such a concern on Player~B's side could rationalize the positive punishment observed after good outcomes under delegation.} or if our belief measure, unincentivized and elicited only after the delegation decision, captures the beliefs behind that decision imperfectly. A concern about being perceived as shirking would then reduce delegation where punishment is possible. In our environment, where no self-serving option exists and outcomes are noisy, the delegation choice is the only deliberate act Player~B observes, which may lend this perception particular weight.

\paragraph{Taking stock.} The reversal in Result~1 is robust, but its mechanism remains unresolved. It is not a composition artifact. The conditions are balanced in observables, performance, and confidence, and the treatment effect survives the corresponding controls. Anticipated differential punishment cannot explain the reversal (Result~3). Player~A expects no punishment penalty for delegation on average, matching Player~B's realized punishment (Result~2), even though belief heterogeneity predicts delegation at the margin. Punishment-induced effort cannot explain it either, as improvement concentrates where punishment is impossible (Result~5). What the data document instead is that delegation is withheld under the possibility of punishment disproportionately by better-performing Player~As (Result~4). Preferences over process ownership or over outcome uncertainty are consistent with this pattern, but treatment-independent and could explain Result~1 only if activated by accountability itself --- an assumption our design cannot test, because the possibility of punishment and the presence of accountability coincide by construction.

%\paragraph{Reconciling our findings with \citet{feier_hiding_2022}.} Two of our results sit in apparent tension with the closest precedent. First, \citet{feier_hiding_2022} document that decision-makers are punished \textit{less} for algorithmically delegated decisions; we find no significant punishment differential. Our individual-level calibration, made common knowledge, closes off this signal-extraction channel by construction: a Player~B in our setting has no basis to read delegation as overconfidence, laziness, or ability avoidance. The disappearance of the punishment differential in our data is therefore consistent with, rather than refutes, the signal-extraction reading of \citet{feier_hiding_2022}'s result. By the same logic, the responsibility-shifting motive that \citet{feier_hiding_2022}'s setup leaves on the table is muted in ours, isolating a different and milder margin: the moral salience of accountability for the principal herself, independent of any third-party inference. The reversal we observe in the delegation decision speaks to that margin---and we are explicit that the question of which design feature is causally responsible for the sign reversal cannot be resolved without an experiment that varies relative-performance calibration directly, which we do not do here.

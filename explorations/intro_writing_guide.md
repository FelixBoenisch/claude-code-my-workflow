# Writing guide: introductions in Experimental Economics, JEEA, and Economic Journal

Distilled from close reading of six published experimental papers. Ziegler, Romagnoli and Offerman (JEEA 2024), Cappelen, Falch and Tungodden (JEEA 2025), Bauer et al. (EJ 2023), Leib et al. (EJ 2024), Oexl and Grossman (EE 2013), Hunold and Werner (EE 2025). Focus is structure, flow, and wording. Verbatim examples are quoted.

## 1. The opener

Never open with "This paper". Open with the phenomenon, in one of three registers.

- Declarative claim, then a concrete instance. "People's morals may easily take a back seat in markets. Consider the market for air travel." (Ziegler)
- Real-world questions. "Why do managers hire consultants to announce and implement layoffs, as well as outsource final production decisions? Why might a company like Merck sell the patent to a cancer drug to another company...?" (Oexl and Grossman)
- Plain factual statement with named examples. "Price recommendations are prevalent on many digital marketplaces. Platforms like Airbnb, Expedia, and eBay provide the sellers on their marketplaces with a recommendation..." (Hunold and Werner)

The first sentence is short. The second makes it concrete. Field statistics strengthen motivation where available ("12% of adults account for 68% of all trips", Ziegler).

## 2. The gap, in one sentence

The gap is a single crisp sentence, usually closing paragraph one or two, and is specific rather than generic.

- "So far, the evidence that people's morals are eroded in markets is inconclusive." (Ziegler)
- "...little is known empirically about the mechanisms that underlie these changes in behavior." (Bauer)
- Sharpest form, an unresolved either/or. "...it is not clear whether the willful choice of the intermediary of a selfish outcome... is necessary for blame shifting, or whether the mere presence of a nominal intermediary is sufficient." (Oexl and Grossman)

## 3. The pivot

One workhorse sentence turns motivation into the paper. "In this paper, we employ a laboratory experiment to investigate how the erosion of morals depends on the ability of traders to affect aggregate market outcomes." (Ziegler) "This article examines whether platforms can use algorithms that provide sellers with non-binding price recommendations to make markets more collusive." (Hunold) "In this paper, we study people's biases in punishment behavior in response to wrongdoing..." (Bauer). The pivot names the question, not the method details.

## 4. Concept before design

Where the design instantiates a mechanism, the mechanism gets its own paragraph first, so treatments read as answers rather than choices. Ziegler explains market selection and the replacement logic in two paragraphs before naming any treatment, then the treatments (SINGLE, MULTI, FULL) map onto the forces one by one. Flow principle throughout the corpus: each paragraph consumes exactly what the previous one introduced.

## 5. The design paragraph

Active voice, present tense, "We", concrete quantities, and treatment names introduced in italics or small caps. Section pointers are woven inline rather than saved for a roadmap. "We conducted an experiment, detailed in Section 2, in which a dictator may choose from one of three allocations of $20..." (Oexl and Grossman). Only the features needed to understand the results preview appear; everything else is deferred.

## 6. Results wording

Results are quantified with percentages, percentage points, ratios, or effect sizes, never with p-values, test statistics, or coefficients. The comparison lives inside the sentence.

- "Given an unfair outcome, dictators experience smaller deductions when they use the intermediary instead of choosing directly, and the intermediaries in turn experience larger deductions." (Oexl and Grossman)
- "In FULL, 83% of market participants attempt to trade units yielding minuscule gains... whereas only 16% of participants in SINGLE and 32% in MULTI." (Ziegler)
- "The estimated 'pass-on rate' from recommended prices to sales prices is between 0.22 and 0.57, depending on the recommendation algorithm." (Hunold)

Words of inference ("significant", "statistically indistinguishable") appear, backed by tests only in the body. One emphasis adverb per introduction at most ("Strikingly, we detect a full erosion of morals in FULL", Ziegler; "Surprisingly, the degree to which...", Oexl).

## 7. Mechanism and interpretation

A short chain that rules out alternatives and ends on a hedged preferred reading with honest modality. "We conclude that delegating one's harmful action to someone else might 'feel wrong' or violate a personal rule, imposing a psychological cost." (Oexl and Grossman) Bauer devotes one paragraph to eliminating explanations one by one before naming the preferred interpretation.

## 8. Differentiation sentences

Positioning against specific papers uses a fixed sentence shape. Shared feature, pivot, difference.

- "Our design most closely resembles the delegation and punishment condition of BF, who find... While in the BF design, the act of delegating forwards the whole action set to the intermediary, in our design the intermediary can only choose between the unfair options." (Oexl and Grossman)
- "...echoes Coffman's findings, but, unlike Coffman, we observe that delegating shifts blame onto the intermediary, rather than merely reducing the blame placed on the dictator." (Oexl and Grossman)

One cited paper per sentence. The contrast is carried by sentence structure, not by evaluative adjectives.

## 9. Contribution

Contribution is what the paper does, phrased with action verbs on questions, not membership in literatures. "Our main contribution is threefold. First, we investigate whether market outcomes reflect... Second, we identify whether the moral erosion is due to... Third, we disentangle how much moral erosion is due to..." (Ziegler). The woven alternative attaches one differentiation sentence per strand ("These findings complement and extend previous results on intermediation and blame-shifting", Oexl). Nobody writes "we contribute to the literature on X, which Section Y reviews" as the substance of the contribution.

## 10. Flow devices

- Topic sentences that could stand alone as an outline. Reading only first sentences of Ziegler's intro reproduces its argument.
- Plant and consume. A paragraph ends by naming what the next paragraph explains ("...distinguish between two forces that may drive erosion: (i) market selection and (ii) replacement logic", then one paragraph per force, in order).
- Enumerate only what will be consumed, and consume in the order enumerated.
- Sentence length varies by function. Motivation short, design longest, results short.
- No sub-headings inside the introduction (Leib et al. is the psych-format exception; not a template).

## 11. Roadmap

Three acceptable forms. Classic numbered paragraph (Hunold, Cappelen), prose without section numbers ("In the following, we start by positioning our paper in the related literature. We then describe the experimental design...", Ziegler), or inline pointers woven through the intro with no dedicated paragraph (Oexl and Grossman). EJ papers often drop it entirely.

## 12. Adaptation when the paper has a separate literature section

Ziegler is the model (the only corpus paper with a separate Section 2 Related Literature). The intro still cites the two or three precedents where they do argumentative work, including their numbers when the argument needs them (Falk and Szech's 45.9% vs 75.9% appears in the intro). Detailed positioning is deferred with a single unobtrusive device, in their case a footnote, "In Section 2, we position our paper more precisely in the literature." The contribution statement remains substantive and self-contained. The separate section changes where the literature is discussed, not whether the introduction makes its own argument.

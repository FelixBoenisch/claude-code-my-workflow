\subsection*{Player~B -- Punishment behavior} \label{sec:result2}

We turn to Player~B's punishment behavior in the \textit{Punishment} condition, in which Player~B was given a real, payoff-relevant punishment choice. The default sample throughout is the full $N=80$ Punishment-condition sample; we report a robustness check on the $67$ subjects who passed the preregistered attention check on the punishment-elicitation screen below.

Punishment in all four (delegation, outcome) cells of the strategy method is significantly greater than zero (one-sample $t$-test $p<10^{-5}$ in every cell). Player~B punishes more for bad outcomes than for good outcomes---a within-subject mean difference of about $\pounds 0.21$, significant at the $1\%$ level under both paired $t$- and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests---an indication that punishment carries informational content rather than being random.\footnote{\citet{coffman_intermediation_2011} similarly documents non-trivial punishment in scenarios where the decision-maker's action could not have been improved upon. Positive punishment in the good-outcome cells therefore need not reflect noise: Player~Bs may hold ex-post views about whether the \textit{delegation choice itself} was appropriate, independent of the realised outcome, and can punish accordingly.} $45.0\%$ of Player~Bs do not punish in any cell.\footnote{Among the $44$ Player~Bs who do punish in at least one cell, the punishment levels are predominantly non-trivial: only $6$ ever choose a strictly positive cell punishment of at most $\pounds 0.10$, $14$ at most $\pounds 0.20$, and $19$ at most $\pounds 0.30$ (cumulative, smallest non-zero cell per subject). Conversely, $15$ punishers choose the maximum $\pounds 2$ in at least one cell. The costly punishment option is therefore being used as a substantive sanction rather than as tokenistic enforcement.}

Conditional on the outcome, Player~A is not significantly differently punished depending on the delegation decision (Figure~\ref{fig:punishment}, panel~A). When Player~B receives the high payoff, average punishment is $\pounds 0.329$ for delegated decisions and $\pounds 0.297$ for self-made decisions. When Player~B receives the low payoff, average punishment is $\pounds 0.482$ for delegated decisions and $\pounds 0.553$ for self-made decisions; this difference points in the direction of H2 but is not significant (paired $t$-test $p=0.131$; Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.544$). Equivalently, regressing the within-subject difference in punishment (self-made minus delegated) on a bad-outcome indicator yields $-\pounds 0.03$ in the good cell and $+\pounds 0.07$ in the bad cell, with the diff-in-diff between the two cells of $+\pounds 0.10$ borderline at $p=0.07$ (Table~\ref{tab:reg_punishment}, column~2; column~3 adds socio-demographic controls and Player~B's belief about Player~A's performance, leaving the diff-in-diff unchanged at $+\pounds 0.10$, $p=0.08$). The same picture arises for punishment frequencies (Figure~\ref{fig:punishment}, panel~A, right axis): Player~B neither punishes delegated decisions substantially less harshly, nor less frequently. One alternative driver of cell-level punishment we can also rule out is heterogeneity in Player~B's beliefs about Player~A's absolute performance: although Player~A and the algorithm are calibrated to perform identically and this calibration is common knowledge, Player~Bs hold heterogeneous absolute-level beliefs about Player~A's score, and those beliefs are uncorrelated with chosen punishment in every cell (Pearson $|r|\le 0.03$, all $p>0.8$).

The result is essentially identical when we restrict to the $67$ Player~Bs who passed the punishment-screen attention check (mean difference $\pounds 0.077$ vs $\pounds 0.071$; paired $t$-test $p=0.153$ vs $p=0.131$; Table~\ref{tab:reg_punishment}, columns~5--6).

In our experimental setting, Player~A therefore cannot reduce her expected punishment by delegating to the algorithm: punishment is determined almost entirely by the realised outcome and not by the delegation decision. This non-result also bears on \textit{Result~1}: a treatment difference in delegation rates motivated by responsibility avoidance would require Player~B to differentially punish delegated decisions, which we do not observe.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Result~2.} \textit{Player~A cannot avoid punishment by delegating the decision to the algorithm.}
\medskip

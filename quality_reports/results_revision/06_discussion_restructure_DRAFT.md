\paragraph{Selection on performance.} Nor is Result~1 an artifact of sample composition. Selection on observable characteristics cannot explain Result~1, as treatment randomization produced balance across socio-demographic and other background characteristics (Appendix~\ref{appendix:sum_stats}, Table~\ref{tab:treatment_balance}). The same holds for performance. Average performance in the first ten rounds is balanced across conditions ($2.93$ correct predictions out of ten; Figure~\ref{fig:performance}), Player~As in both conditions exhibit similar overconfidence about their own performance,\footnote{Measured by the weighted-average belief about own performance. This overconfidence may partly reflect the information that the algorithm performs equally well. It does not differ across conditions.} and the treatment effect is robust to controlling for actual and perceived performance (Table~\ref{tab:del_decision_determinants}, Columns~(3)--(4)).

\paragraph{Performance and delegation.} What differs across conditions is \textit{who} delegates. A first indication already appears in the pooled delegation regression in Table~\ref{tab:del_decision_determinants}, where performance loads negatively and approaches significance ($p=0.068$). This tendency crystallizes within the \textit{Punishment} condition: among attention-check passers, the correlation between Player~A's score in the first ten rounds and the delegation decision is $-0.19$, with $p=0.092$ (Figure~\ref{fig:performance_delegation_scatter}), while no comparable relationship is evident in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition.\footnote{A similar relationship holds for perceived performance. Player~A's weighted-average belief about her own performance enters with a small negative coefficient but is never statistically significant. Thus, actual performance discriminates sharply between delegators and non-delegators ($2.38$ vs.\ $3.00$ correct out of ten) while perceived performance does not ($4.69$ vs.\ $4.71$; Figure~\ref{fig:performance_by_delegation}). Delegators under punishment are not subjects who \textit{believe} they are worse than their algorithm; they are subjects who \textit{are} worse, and whose confidence does not reflect it.} Framed at the level of treatment differences, the delegation gap widens with performance: among Player~As with at most two correct predictions in the first ten rounds, $51.5\%$ delegate in the \textit{Punishment} condition against $62.5\%$ in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition; at three correct predictions, $45.5\%$ against $61.1\%$; and at four or more, $28.0\%$ against $54.8\%$ (Fisher's exact $p=0.059$).\footnote{Among attention-check passers the corresponding differences are $-12.1$, $-16.2$, and $-28.9$ percentage points (Fisher's exact $p=0.065$ in the top group). The interaction between treatment and performance in a Logit model of the delegation decision is positive but not statistically significant ($p=0.29$ in the full sample, $p=0.34$ among passers), so the widening pattern is descriptive rather than a powered test.} The composition of delegators shifts accordingly. Player~As who delegated in the \textit{Punishment} condition scored on average $2.47$ out of ten in the first ten rounds, whereas delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition scored $3.06$ (two-sided $p=0.039$). The additional delegation in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition thus comes disproportionately from the middle and upper part of the performance distribution. It is precisely these subjects who, when punishment is on the table, keep the decision for themselves.

This relationship is informative because its natural explanation, namely that worse performers delegate because they believe the algorithm outperforms them, is ruled out in our design. In \citet{feier_hiding_2022}, the algorithm's performance distribution is matched to the human distribution at the \textit{population} level: a high-performing subject who recognizes her position in that distribution rationally believes she outperforms the algorithm in expectation, and a low-performing one rationally believes the reverse. Delegation that declines with performance is then exactly what maximizing expected utility predicts. In our design, performance is equalized at the \textit{individual} level and this is common knowledge. The pattern in the \textit{Punishment} condition must therefore reflect something other than beliefs about relative ability.

\paragraph{Interpreting the performance--delegation relationship.} Two candidate readings of the relationship between performance and delegation remain, both involving preferences rather than beliefs. The first is a preference over \textit{process}. Player~As who have a good chance of producing the high payoff may prefer to bring it about themselves rather than through the algorithm --- we refer to this as \textit{process ownership} of the outcome. A mirror-image variant loads on the other tail of the performance distribution: Player~As with little chance of success may delegate to avoid being the proximate cause of a likely failure. The second reading is a preference over \textit{uncertainty}. \citet{dietvorst_people_2020} document that the preference for algorithms declines as the uncertainty inherent in a decision rises; in our task, where most subjects score below five out of ten, better performance moves the success probability toward one half --- the point of maximal outcome uncertainty --- so a distaste for delegating uncertain decisions would likewise depress delegation among better performers.

Neither reading, however, explains Result~1. In their pure form, both are properties of Player~A's preferences that apply in the two conditions equally: they can rationalize a negative relationship between performance and delegation, but they predict that relationship in \textit{both} conditions and predict no treatment difference in delegation rates. The failure-avoidance variant of process ownership, moreover, carries a treatment-dependent prediction of its own --- low-performing Player~As should delegate \textit{more} when punishment is possible --- which we do not observe: delegation among the lowest-scoring group is, if anything, also lower in the \textit{Punishment} condition. Process ownership could generate Result~1 only under the auxiliary assumption that the preference is \textit{activated} by accountability, becoming behaviorally consequential once Player~B's stake in the decision is enforceable rather than merely material. Because the possibility of punishment and the presence of accountability coincide by construction in our design, this activation assumption cannot be tested here. We therefore regard process ownership and uncertainty preferences as interpretations of the relationship between performance and delegation, not as explanations of the treatment effect.

\paragraph{Effort to outperform the algorithm.} In contrast to preferences over process or uncertainty, a punishment-induced effort motive is treatment-dependent by construction and could therefore, in principle, generate Result~1. Despite the algorithm being calibrated to Player~A's past performance, her final prediction is not yet made. Thus, a Player~A may believe that she can try especially hard and raise the chance of a good outcome for Player~B above the algorithm's, if the decision is not delegated. To the extent that Player~A expects milder punishment after a good outcome, the prospect of punishment would then push her toward deciding herself. This possibility yields the prediction that non-delegators should improve their performance, specifically in the \textit{Punishment} condition. Improvement in both conditions would instead indicate a treatment-independent (for instance, altruistic) motive, which cannot explain Result~1.

The data do not lend support to this story. Delegators do not make a final prediction themselves, so effort on the final prediction is observable merely for non-delegators. Measuring improvement as the difference between a subject's average absolute prediction error over the first ten rounds and her absolute error on the final prediction, non-delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition improve on their record, whereas non-delegators in the \textit{Punishment} condition do not. Because all Player~As faced the same images in the first 10 rounds and on the final prediction, this differential cannot reflect task-specific variation. Realized outcomes tell the same story with $45.5\%$ of non-delegators in the \textit{No-Punishment} condition earning the high payoff for Player~B, compared with $21.7\%$ in the \textit{Punishment} condition, even though the two groups' performances are statistically indistinguishable over the first ten rounds ($3.12$ vs.\ $2.98$ correct predictions; two-sided $p=0.66$). If anything, effort seems to rise where punishment is impossible, which is the opposite of what a punishment-motivated effort story would predict.

\paragraph{Taking stock.} The reversal in Result~1 is robust, but its mechanism remains unresolved. It is not a composition artifact: the conditions are balanced in observables, performance, and confidence, and the treatment effect survives the corresponding controls. The two channels that could, in principle, generate a treatment difference are both inconsistent with the data. Anticipated differential punishment requires a delegation-specific asymmetry that is absent from Player~A's beliefs on average --- consistent with Player~B's realized punishment (Result~2) --- even though belief heterogeneity predicts delegation at the margin. Punishment-induced effort predicts improvement concentrated in the \textit{Punishment} condition, whereas improvement concentrates where punishment is impossible. What the data document instead is a descriptive clue: the delegation withheld under the possibility of punishment comes disproportionately from better-performing Player~As. Preferences over process or over outcome uncertainty are consistent with this pattern, but in their pure form they are treatment-independent and could explain Result~1 only if activated by accountability itself --- an assumption our design cannot test, because the possibility of punishment and the presence of accountability coincide by construction. Identifying the channel therefore requires a design that varies accountability and the attribution of process separately. We return to this in Section~\ref{conclusion}.
